A brand-new SUV stripped of all four wheels and left belly-down in my apartment parking lot gets me thinking about property crime, personal boundaries, and how Americans respond when somebody takes their stuff.

This episode wanders through Malcolm Gladwell, gun violence, disputed crime statistics, broken windows policing, and the unexpected emotional education I’m getting from the Canadian detective series Private Eyes. There are live Google queries, personal stories, second thoughts, and a proposal for recovering wheel-less cars with a giant fish spatula.

I explore the argument that Americans react to theft with a particular intensity. Losing something can feel like an invasion, and an insurance payout hardly resolves that feeling. A friend’s violent response to an attempted pickpocketing in Europe brings that cultural question into sharp relief.

Then there’s Private Eyes. I came for Jason Priestley and detective stories. I stayed for the patience, apologies, father-daughter relationships, and examples of people bringing the temperature down when somebody behaves badly. Apparently, a Canadian television show is teaching me how to be an adult man.

Later, I revisit two books I enthusiastically consumed when they were fashionable: The Tipping Point and Freakonomics. What actually explains the crime decline of the 1990s? Broken windows policing? More officers? The decline of crack? Reduced lead exposure? The controversial abortion hypothesis? I discuss those competing explanations and my view that maintaining law and order requires continual work.

Along the way, I question how racial crime statistics are framed, how arrests differ from crimes committed, and how numbers acquire political meaning. The episode includes live search responses and disputed claims that I examine as part of the conversation.

The Car on Its Belly

On the show I called it a Honda CR-V, after first calling it a Toyota CR-V. It was a 2027 Toyota RAV4, from the redesigned sixth generation. RAV4 owners on Reddit pegged it as a Limited and guessed it lost the 20-inch wheels. The SUV was in my own apartment building's parking lot in South Arlington at ten in the morning: brand new, still on a paper plate, and missing all four wheels and tires. It wasn't up on bricks, breeze blocks, or milk crates. It was flat on its belly, rotors and calipers showing. By the time I walked back from Starbucks, it was gone.

I posted it on Reddit's r/nova as "Intentional or Unintentional?" and shared it with RAV4 owners on r/rav4club, and between them the two posts drew about 400,000 views. Neighbors said that lot behind Dominion Towers has lost wheels for years, almost always off Toyotas and Hondas, because their bolt patterns fit so many cars that the wheels resell easily. Others had seen RAV4s stripped in Old Town Alexandria, at an apartment complex in Courthouse, and in an Arlington garage, and one RAV4 owner said a post like mine turns up in the Toyota groups every week. The advice was wheel locks, kept somewhere other than the glove box. The owners who had done their homework went further: Advanced Wheel Locks, which one owner said survived everything but a welded-on nut in a Project Farm test, or Rimgard, or locking lug nuts on all twenty studs, so a thief would need a different socket for every one. On getting the car back off the ground, one commenter would lock the rotors to the hubs, jack all four corners at once under the brake rotors, and then lift from the proper jack points; he added that most tow companies won't bother and will let the insurance claim absorb the damage. The way I heard it, some crews slide airbags under the body, pump them up until they can bolt on donut spares, and roll it away. My own plan, a giant fish spatula, remains untested.

What it felt like to have that photo travel is in an article: The Day I Blew Up Reddit on Someone Else's Loss.

Glossary

Broken windows theory
The idea that visible disorder (broken windows, graffiti, fare beating) tells people nobody is in charge, and that tolerating small offenses invites serious ones. James Q. Wilson and George L. Kelling set it out in "Broken Windows," The Atlantic, March 1982. William Bratton put it into practice on New York's transit police and then, under Mayor Rudy Giuliani, across the NYPD. Whether it caused the 1990s crime drop is still argued. More.
The 1990s crime decline
Violent crime in the United States fell sharply from about 1991 through the end of the decade, in nearly every city, whatever its policing. The usual candidates are the ones named in this episode: more police, the end of the crack epidemic, less lead exposure, legalized abortion, and a booming economy. No one explanation has won.
Abortion and crime hypothesis
John Donohue and Steven Levitt's 2001 argument that legal abortion after Roe v. Wade (1973) meant fewer children born into hardship, and so, roughly eighteen years later, fewer young men in their peak crime years. Popularized by Freakonomics. It is one of the most disputed papers in economics: other economists found a coding error in the original, and the authors replied that the effect survives. More.
Lead and crime hypothesis
The idea that childhood lead exposure, mostly from leaded gasoline, damaged impulse control, and that phasing lead out of gasoline from the 1970s on helped bring crime down a generation later. More.
Crack epidemic
The surge in crack cocaine use and the violent street markets around it, from the mid-1980s into the early 1990s. Its fading is another candidate for the crime drop. More.
Straw purchase
Buying a gun from a licensed dealer on behalf of someone who can't pass the background check, or doesn't want their name on the form. A federal felony, and one of the main routes by which legally made guns reach the illegal market. More.
Rates versus volume
A count of crimes is not a rate. Big cities have the most shootings by number; smaller places often have higher rates per person. A group can account for most crimes by volume while having a lower rate, or the other way around, depending on its share of the population.
Arrests versus offenses
Most crime statistics count arrests, which depend on where police look as well as on who offends. That gap is the center of the argument over figures like "13 percent of the population, 50 percent of the crime." The federal National Crime Victimization Survey, which asks victims rather than police, is one way researchers check arrest data.
"Lies, damned lies, and statistics"
Mark Twain made the line famous in his autobiography and credited it to Benjamin Disraeli; nobody has found Disraeli saying it. It means numbers can make a weak argument sound strong. More.
Paper plate
The temporary cardboard or paper tag a new car wears before its metal plates arrive. Here, proof the car was days old.
Fish spatula
A long, thin, flexible, slotted metal turner made for sliding under delicate fish without breaking it. Proposed here, not entirely seriously, as tow equipment.
Adenoidal
Nasal and blocked-sounding, as if from swollen adenoids. Said several times in this episode to make sure I was saying it right.
Aphantasia
Having no mind's eye: no voluntary visual imagery. Which is why I can describe the fish spatula rescue but can't picture it. More.
Moonlighting
The 1985 to 1989 ABC detective comedy with Cybill Shepherd and Bruce Willis, the template for Private Eyes: two mismatched partners, a case of the week, and banter that matters more than the case.

The theory gets its name from a simple analogy. Imagine a building with a few broken windows. If the windows are not repaired, it signals that no one is in charge and no one cares.

Because there are no consequences, vandals will soon break more windows. Eventually, the law-abiding citizens will abandon the street out of fear, leaving the area entirely controlled by criminals, leading to much more violent behavior. A Three-Step Chain Reaction Minor disorder appears: small, low-level offenses occur like graffiti, littering, public drinking, or turnstile jumping. Community withdrawal: when the minor violations go unpunished, regular citizens begin to feel unsafe. They stop walking down the street, stop looking out their windows, and stop interacting with neighbors, and also stop using the subway, of course.

Community surveillance breaks down, and then serious crime takes root. Criminals notice the lack of social control and police presence. They deduce that if the police do not care about graffiti, they certainly will not stop drug dealing, car thefts, or robberies. Introduced in 1982 by social scientists James Q. Wilson and George L.

Kelling, the theory heavily influenced American policing in the 90s. Most famously, New York City Mayor Rudy Giuliani, yeah, that Mayor Giuliani, and Police Commissioner William Bratton used it to completely reshape the NYPD.

Narrator: Who Says Your Blood Is Redder?. By Chris Abraham.
Narrator: Spock dies behind glass in Star Trek II: The Wrath of Khan, and before he dies he gives the reason. "Don't grieve, Admiral. It is logical. The needs of the many outweigh the needs of the few." Kirk finishes it for him—"Or the one." It is the most quoted moral sentence in science fiction, and it sounds like scripture, like something from the tradition that produced the actor saying it. I argued the opposite conviction in Never Spare the One for the Many: the one is never the price of the many. The question that piece raises is the question here: where does Spock's sentence come from?
Narrator: It comes from London. Jeremy Bentham built the calculus in the late 1700s: the greatest happiness of the greatest number is the measure of right and wrong. John Stuart Mill gave it its classic form two generations later. The system's unit is welfare, and welfare has the one property that makes the maxim work: it can be added. Nine units outweigh one unit the way nine pounds outweigh one pound, by definition of the scale. Spock's sentence is that arithmetic wearing a uniform—two centuries old, English, philosophy's, not the synagogue's.
Narrator: That is the fork, and Spock stands on it. One inheritance counts souls. The other counts welfare. A soul cannot be summed: it is a world entire, and worlds do not go into columns. Welfare can be summed, and once summed the many outweigh the few every time, because that is what the units are for. Secular Judaism kept the costume of the first inheritance—the moral seriousness, the prophetic tone—and filled it with the second inheritance's content. Utilitarianism dressed in a rabbi's sidelocks still does Bentham's sums.
Narrator: The genuinely Jewish thing in the frame is the hand. Leonard Nimoy was a boy in an Orthodox synagogue in Boston when the Kohanim rose to bless the congregation. His father told him not to look. He looked. The priests held their hands with the fingers split, forming the letter Shin, for Shaddai, and the boy practiced the gesture until he owned it. Decades later he gave it to Spock. The Vulcan salute is the Priestly Blessing of Numbers 6, carried into space by a kid who peeked. The blessing is Jewish; the arithmetic is Bentham's. One frame holds both.
Narrator: Honesty about the record: nobody on that production sold the maxim as Torah. The Wrath of Khan's screenplay is credited to Jack B. Sowards; Nicholas Meyer wrote the final draft in twelve days, uncredited. Roddenberry was an avowed secular humanist; his Vulcans were built on Stoic calm and Enlightenment reason. Inside the fiction, Spock credits Surak, never scripture. The maxim arrived as what it was—a humanist sentence in a science fiction film—and only later acquired a Talmudic passport.
Narrator: The passport was issued by the audience. At release, the commentary takes custody, and readers begin telling the author what he meant. The mechanics are in The Death of the Author Was a Custody Grab; the Spock maxim is its cleanest case. Nimoy's blessing-hand made the character read as Jewish, his rationality got mistaken for Talmudic argument, and within a few years the sentence was being quoted as ancient Jewish wisdom. The bridge from Bentham to Babylon was built afterward, by admirers, over a maxim written in Burbank—a founding story constructed backward, flattering the people telling it.
Narrator: The correction cuts the other way too. Utilitarian humanism does not require the robot. Kurt Vonnegut succeeded Isaac Asimov as honorary president of the American Humanist Association, and there is nobody in the century with more pathos in him. His creed, given to Eliot Rosewater to say over newborn twins in God Bless You, Mr. Rosewater, is one rule long: "God damn it, you've got to be kind." The coldness people hear in the maxim is the costume, not the philosophy—Roddenberry's Stoic styling, the word "logical" doing the work of a seal. It is still Bentham's sentence. Warmth does not change its passport.
Narrator: Even the fiction knows the trick. Surak, the father of Vulcan logic, comes down as an ancient lawgiver; he was written in California between 1966 and 1982. The secular scripture claims an antiquity it does not have. So the honest move is the unfashionable one: go back to the sources. Back to the caravan, back to the Mishnah, back to the page where the question was asked the first time.
Narrator: The caravan case is Tosefta Terumot 7:20, carried in the Jerusalem Talmud at Terumot 8:4 and codified by Maimonides. Travelers are told: hand over one of you to be killed, or we kill you all. The ruling is that all of them die. They may not hand over a single soul—not by lot, not by vote, not to save the whole company. The governing principle has a name: ein dochin nefesh mipnei nefesh, one soul is not pushed aside for another. The tradition was asked Spock's question with the swords already out, and it refused the trade. Adin Steinsaltz's The Essential Talmud remains the plainest door into that world in English.
Narrator: The title of this piece is the tradition's own sentence. In Sanhedrin 74a a man before Rava is told: kill that man, or we kill you. May he kill to save himself? Rava: be killed, and do not kill. Who says your blood is redder than his? Perhaps his blood is redder. No man is issued a scale that weighs souls, because no man is qualified to read it. The blood in his own veins does not outrank the blood in the stranger's.
Narrator: The Mishnah gives the reason, Sanhedrin 4:5, spoken to witnesses in capital cases. Adam was created alone, the Mishnah says, to teach that whoever destroys a single soul, Scripture counts it as if he had destroyed a whole world, and whoever saves a single soul, as if he had saved a whole world. The sentence traveled: the Quran takes it up nearly word for word at 5:32—whoever kills a soul, it is as if he had killed all mankind, and whoever saves one, as if he had saved all mankind. The one-soul arithmetic crosses the fence intact. A world is not a unit; it does not divide into a sum.
Narrator: Sheba ben Bichri raised a revolt against David and was besieged in Abel Beth Maacah, and a wise woman negotiated with Joab at the wall: Sheba's head over the wall, and the siege lifts (2 Samuel 20). Sheba was designated by name, and Sheba was guilty. On those two facts the authorities build the only door in the wall: a man demanded by name, for his own crime, may be handed over.
Narrator: Maimonides codifies the unease in Yesodei HaTorah 5:5. If the demanded man is designated and liable to death, like Sheba, he may be given up. But the ruling is not to be taught to the group in advance. A permission the law refuses to announce is a permission the law is ashamed of, and Maimonides builds the shame into the procedure. If the man is designated but innocent, or no one is designated, the whole company dies.
Narrator: Then the tradition does something rarer than ruling. It mourns. Ulla son of Koshev, a fugitive hidden in Lod, was surrendered when the Romans threatened the town—Rabbi Yehoshua ben Levi persuaded him to give himself up. Elijah, who used to visit the rabbi, stopped coming. After fasts, Elijah returned, and the exchange is recorded in the Jerusalem Talmud and Genesis Rabbah 94: "Am I a companion to informers?" The rabbi answered, "I acted according to the Mishnah." Elijah said, "And is that the Mishnah of the pious?" The handing-over was lawful. Heaven registered the cost in absence anyway.
Narrator: The one clean category is the rodef, the pursuer. Sanhedrin 73a: a man pursuing another to kill him may be stopped by any bystander, at the cost of the pursuer's life if nothing lesser will stop him; if lesser means would have stopped him, the killing is murder. The exception proves the architecture. The pursuer is no bystander selected by arithmetic. He is a chooser in motion, spending his own agency on murder, and it is the agency that makes his life forfeitable. Innocence is never the currency. Choice is.
Narrator: The complaint has a literature. Soloveitchik's The Lonely Man of Faith sets Adam I, majestic and utilitarian man, against Adam II, covenantal man; the reduction of faith to Adam I's bookkeeping is its named grief. Jewish bioethics was built on the same contrast—Jakobovits founded the field, Bleich carried it—on the axiom that a life is of infinite value, and infinity cannot be summed or rationed by greatest-good tables. One life, in that literature, weighs what six million weigh, because the scale that could tell them apart does not exist. The tikkun olam argument is the same charge in popular form: the tradition's vocabulary kept, its content replaced by the surrounding culture's politics, the result sold as prophetic Judaism while the legal texts say the opposite.
Narrator: Jonah is the one case where the tradition lets a man go overboard. Jonah volunteers: "Pick me up and throw me into the sea; then the sea will quiet down for you" (Jonah 1:12). The sailors—pagans, who earlier were praying each to his own god—refuse the arithmetic twice. First they row hard for land, trying to save the whole ship, guilty passenger included. Then they pray to Jonah's God not to charge them with innocent blood, and only then do they throw him. Even sailors who worship other gods balk at spending a man, and the one man they spend has asked for it in his own voice.
Narrator: Sodom runs the negotiation the other way. Abraham bargains God down from fifty righteous to ten, and God agrees at every step: the whole city spared for the sake of ten. I argued this in the companion piece; the direction of the haggling is the point here. The trajectory is always the many spared for the few. Nobody proposes the reverse rate.
Narrator: The one-for-many sentence does appear in the Bible, once, in so many words—and the speaker is the prosecution. John 11:50, Caiaphas the high priest, addressing the council: "It is better for you that one man should die for the people, not that the whole nation should perish." John notes the irony: Caiaphas prophesied better than he knew. In Caiaphas's own mouth it is statecraft arranging an execution: Bentham's sum, done two thousand years early, in a council chamber, over an innocent man never consulted. The maxim's one biblical speaker is the man arranging the killing. The tradition had the sentence available the whole time. It put the sentence where it belonged.
Narrator: Jesus's answer is the shepherd. Luke 15: a man with a hundred sheep loses one, leaves the ninety-nine in the wilderness, and goes after the lost one; the joy in heaven runs over the one, not the secured ninety-nine. The woman with ten coins loses one and sweeps the whole house for it. Read beside John 11, the parables are a contravention with an address on it. In Caiaphas's economy the one is the price of the flock. In the shepherd's economy the flock is the stake risked for the one. The wilderness gets the ninety-nine for the night. The shepherd goes out anyway.
Narrator: Star Trek argues with itself across two summers. The Wrath of Khan (1982) states the maxim over Spock's body. The Search for Spock (1984) reverses it in action: Kirk and the crew steal the Enterprise, spend their careers, and watch the ship itself burn over the Genesis planet, for one man. When Spock asks why they came, Kirk hands him the inverted sentence: because the needs of the one outweigh the needs of the many. Audiences cheered both films. The franchise is a civilization conducting its oldest argument in its loudest theater, and declining to settle it.
Narrator: Ursula K. Le Guin made the bargain literal in "The Ones Who Walk Away from Omelas" (1973). A city of perfect happiness rests on one term, known to every citizen: in a cellar closet a child sits in misery, and the city's joy is priced on that child's suffering—exactly one soul, itemized. Some citizens walk out of the city alone, toward a place the story does not describe. Le Guin gives the walkers no argument and no destination. They are the story's only heroes, because refusal is the only move the bargain leaves open. Utopia and dystopia are the same city, read from the festival or from the cellar.
Narrator: The Hunger Games turns the same bargain into liturgy: twenty-four children a year, two from each district, spent on television to keep the whole at peace—the one-for-many as a state religion with a broadcast schedule.
Narrator: Saving Private Ryan argues the other column. Eight men are sent through a war to find one private; the film stages the objection inside the squad. Reiben asks the captain to explain the math of it—why one man is worth the risk to eight. Miller answers from his own ledger: he would not trade ten Ryans for one Caparzo. The film does not refute the arithmetic with arithmetic. It answers with a life: the dying Miller, whispering "Earn this" to the man the squad spent itself to save. The one-doctrine, defended at full price by the men paying it, the price left in the frame, unhidden.
Narrator: The many-for-one direction sits at the West's foundation. Helen is taken, and the many are spent for the one: a thousand ships, ten years of war, a generation of men, for one woman's return. Marlowe's line is the invoice—"Was this the face that launched a thousand ships?" The Iliad never calls the arithmetic sane. It sings the cost, name by name, funeral by funeral, and lets the reader do the sum the kings would not.
Narrator: Rome wrote the maxim into law—Cicero: salus populi suprema lex esto, the welfare of the people is the supreme law—and filled its honor rolls with the opposite kind of man. Decius Mus, in 340 BC, at the Battle of Vesuvius devoted himself and the enemy army to the gods below and rode alone into the Latin line. His son repeated the act at Sentinum in 295 BC. Rome carved its statues of volunteers—men who spent themselves, a different act from a committee spending one of its members—and never confused the two in marble.
Narrator: The American military carries the one-doctrine as creed: leave no man behind. The many sortie for the one, at bad odds and real cost, as identity rather than calculation. The same republic budgets by the maxim. Both sentences are load-bearing, unreconciled, and neither can be put down.
Narrator: The many-for-one column has an endpoint, and it is a bunker. Berlin, April 1945: a nation ordered to die in place around one man's refusal to survive his own ruin—the Nero Decree had already ordered Germany's infrastructure destroyed, and Hitler told Speer the people's means of survival were no longer worth considering. Jonestown, November 1978: nine hundred and eighteen dead at one man's word. Sacrificing the many for the one is the tyrant's form of the same theft. The columns swap. The theft is identical: lives spent by someone who did not own them.
Narrator: The professing culture practices the maxim it argues with. The standing justification of the bomb is a sum: it saved more lives than it took—Caiaphas's sentence, translated into staff-college English. Triage sorts by the sum; QALY tables price a year of life and ration by the price; pandemic policy was argued in aggregate lives. The trolley problem is the maxim's classroom form, and the discomfort it produces in every seminar is the old discomfort—Rava's question in modern dress. A civilization that cannot settle the question in its policy keeps the argument running in its fiction, which is where Omelas and the Enterprise and the Ryan squad come from.
Narrator: The culture laughs at the sentence it lives by. In Hot Fuzz (2007), the Neighbourhood Watch Alliance of an English village murders for the Village of the Year title, chanting the justification in unison: "The greater good." Laughter is how a civilization confesses.
Narrator: So the distinction: a man may spend himself. Jonah says throw me. Decius rides. Spock walks into the radiation chamber on his own feet, and the volunteering is not decoration on the act—it is the act. No text on this table lets the group spend him, in either direction. The caravan may not be saved by one surrendered soul. The bunker may not be fed a nation. Self-sacrifice is the glory of every tradition here; the sacrifice of the other is the crime in all of them, and the direction of the spending changes nothing but the size of the theft.
Narrator: Spock is honored because he is Jonah, not because he is Caiaphas's arithmetic. Take the volunteering out of the chamber scene and the most beloved death in science fiction is an execution with a famous line spoken over it. Leave it in, and it is the oldest story on the table: one man spending the only life he has the right to spend, the blessing his actor stole from the synagogue riding on his hand. The hand was the true part all along.

Narrator: Half the Game. By Chris Abraham.
Narrator: People judge Christianity as a behavior code. You're not behaving Christian. You're not treating people right. You're not kind enough to the poor. The whole faith gets graded on conduct, and conduct is only half of it.
Narrator: There are two commandments. Love God. Love your neighbor as yourself. The order carries weight: the first one is first.
Narrator: Thou shalt love the Lord thy God with all thy heart, and with all thy soul, and with all thy mind. This is the first and great commandment. And the second is like unto it, Thou shalt love thy neighbour as thyself. On these two commandments hang all the law and the prophets.
Narrator: Love God means worship. Prayer and worship, prayer and worship. Submission to your God. Obedience to your God. Loving on God above everything else in your life. That is half the game, and it is the half nobody talks about, because it happens at night, in private, where nobody can grade it.
Narrator: The herpes of religion
Narrator: Call it the herpes of religion, and I am willing to say it plain: outsiders see the cold sore and believe they have seen the disease. The sore is the only part that shows—the public behavior, the performance, the flare-up anybody can point at. The rest of the condition is systemic and invisible: the prayer, the devotion, the submission, an interior life running under the skin for years. People grade the sore and call it grading the Christian.
Narrator: The closest thing to a public record of my own prayer life is a passage I liked enough to copy out of the daily readings and pass around, or a Hallow notice that a streak is alive. That is the whole visible surface. Underneath it sit two or three hours most mornings before I get up: contemplation, prayer and worship, the red words of the Gospel, daily reflections, Jeff Cavins, a whole rosary prayed along with, half-asleep. Unstructured. I couldn't hand you a syllabus. I don't love writing even this much of it, because centering myself in it feels wrong—and that reluctance is the evidence. This half of the faith leaves almost no trace.
Narrator: Worship doesn't wear a costume. It isn't dancing around an effigy going yabba yabba, and you don't have to be the Italian nana at church every single day of her life to be a prayerful person. The altar is wherever you are: on your knees praying, in contemplation, reading the Word, in relationship with other worshipers, exploring your relationship with God, in service of the Lord. Half the job is done in quiet, where you get no credit for it. Nobody awards brownie points for an interior life, for a rosary running inside your own head. That half earns nothing in public, and it is half.
Narrator: Love your neighbor as yourself
Narrator: The second half is the neighbor. See God in the people around you and treat them as what they are: made in the image of God. Treat them as you would treat yourself. One quiet assumption rides inside that command: it presumes you love yourself. Love your neighbor as yourself fails in the hands of a man who loathes himself, because himself is the measuring stick. The intent of the law, not the letter of it.
Narrator: And neighbor never meant your tribe. Jesus said it inside a Judaism where everybody outside the observant circle was unclean. Gentiles were unclean. Samaritans were unclean—the Samaritan is the hero of the parable precisely because he was the unclean one who stopped. Egyptians, Greeks, Romans, Caesar, tax collectors, prostitutes: unclean, all of them. Love your neighbor as yourself was never a command to be nice to the people on your left and right in the pews. It means humanity.
Narrator: The halfway man
Narrator: So take the man everybody calls a bad Christian, the one you can't stand. He might spend every night of his life worshiping his God. He might be a great dad. He might be golden inside his own walls, his country club, his garden. Then he leaves the garden and he is racist and sexist and he judges sinners and he would never be caught around lepers. That man is halfway there. He holds the first half. What he has not yet grown is the second: compassion, mercy. He is not a zero. And the kind, tolerant man who never prays is not a finished Christian either. Both men hold half of a two-part commandment.
Narrator: Nor do the halves blend on their own. Temperaments lean. One man loves people; altruism costs him nothing, while prayer feels like a chore he has to talk himself into—so he doubles his helpings of outreach and charity and quietly starves the altar. Another man loves devotion and starves the neighbor. Doubling one half does not cover the other. The whole is the two halves, and you cannot replace the whole with anything.
Narrator: How the halves connect
Narrator: The first half is receiving. God is merciful to us first. We get loved in a way we did not earn and do not deserve, because He is our Father. The second half is transmission. We become proxies for that mercy and we pass it on.
Narrator: I bought orange juice for a woman at Giant once. She wanted my address so she could mail me the money. I told her: pass it on. That is the second commandment in three words. Learn to love God. Learn to be loved by God. Then learn to love, and learn to be loved, and carry it forward.
Narrator: The rings
Narrator: There is no rule that every Christian performs at the level of an apostle. The church itself is built in concentric rings. The priest at the center. Then the twelve—the vestry, the board of directors. Then the congregation. Then the Easter and Christmas members. Then the people out in the neighborhood who hear the bell when it rings and have never once come through the door. Nobody is required to stand in the same ring as the apostles. The rings are how the faith actually holds people.
Narrator: The Catholic courseload
Narrator: Nobody gets this worse than Catholics, who get thrown out with the bathwater as the works-only Christians, as if Catholics believe good deeds buy a seat in heaven. Meanwhile every Catholic carries a courseload of worship the accusers never see: worshiping God and worshiping Jesus, prayer, prayers on behalf of other people, prayers for the dead—for souls in Purgatory, the in-between, being prayed out of it by the living. Today is All Souls' Day, and Catholics all over the world have photographs of their dead out and are praying for them, asking Mary, asking God. Intercession is a second job worked in silence, and it is aimed at neighbors the worker can no longer even see.
Narrator: Invited
Narrator: Because nobody deserves the Kingdom of Heaven. Nobody earns it and nobody is owed it. The word is invited. In Matthew 25 the King says: inherit the kingdom prepared for you from the foundation of the world. Prepared, not purchased. Good works do not buy a seat. At most, they show the first half landed—that a man was loved by God, and it started coming out of him toward his neighbor.
Narrator: Two commandments. Love God, with everything. Love your neighbor, all of humanity, as yourself. Grade a Christian on one half alone and you have missed the faith by half.

Narrator: I am a practitioner, not a theologian, and I am not going to pretend this piece is the last word on any of it. It is one man's account of a two-part commandment he is still learning to keep. Narrator: Which leaves the good atheist, and there are many: I'm a good person. I do good things. What do I need with the rest of it? Doing good things is half the job—the half with the sore on it, the half everybody can see. So the question stands. As an atheist, are you spending half your time in devotion to God—in prayer, and service, and submission?

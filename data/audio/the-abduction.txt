Narrator: The Abduction. By Chris Abraham.
Narrator: We call it brain drain because abduction would be impolite.
Narrator: A country finds the smartest people on earth, waits until somebody else has paid to train them, and takes them. A town produces one genius kid in a generation, and a school three states away takes him. The taking is the constant. The vocabulary changes with the distance.
Narrator: Everybody involved calls it opportunity, and the taken call it opportunity too, mostly, and they are right to. A nurse from Manila triples her pay in Houston. A kid from Southie gets a dorm room, a library, and a future with heating. The abduction is consensual. That is what makes it perfect. Nobody has to be dragged anywhere. The magnet does the dragging.
Narrator: The world harvest
Narrator: Start with the world, because the world is the version everybody knows. America harvests the planet's trained minds. The best nurses. The best doctors. The best scientists, the best engineers, skimmed from every country that educated them.
Narrator: We take the best people and move them to America, and we have done it for so long that we talk about it the way farmers talk about weather. It is simply what happens. Talent rains down on us. Talent, in this telling, has a passport and a preference, and both point here.
Narrator: The harvest runs on a price signal, and the signal is crude. Brain drain to America happens because America pays hundreds of thousands of dollars and the rest of the world just pays a salary. A brilliant surgeon in Lagos does the arithmetic once. A nurse in Manila does it once. The arithmetic does the rest, forever, one trained mind at a time.
Narrator: A doctor is the most expensive civilian a country produces. Medical school, residency, the long decade of formation—somebody's treasury, somebody's family, somebody's village carried that cost. The harvest collects the doctor at the exact moment the investment matures and starts paying out. It is the only industry in the world allowed to call its supply chain a meritocracy and its invoice a compliment.
Narrator: The sending countries pay for the schooling. America collects the finished mind. The sending countries keep the word "loss" in their speeches and America keeps the word "gain" in ours, and the two words never appear in the same paragraph.
Narrator: The reversal
Narrator: Now the machine is running backwards, or trying to.
Narrator: Student visas squeezed. ICE in the news every day. A running fight over Harvard and whether it can go on enrolling the world. Whatever anyone thinks of the politics, the direction is new. For a century the magnet pointed one way, toward us, and the harvest came in like a tide.
Narrator: A magnet can be switched off. A harvest can end. The countries that spent decades complaining about losing their doctors to American salaries have started doing a different arithmetic, and some of the minds that would have boarded planes are staying home and building there. The abduction, it turns out, was a policy. Policies change.
Narrator: The home harvest
Narrator: The world harvest gets the speeches. The home harvest gets a brochure.
Narrator: The same machine runs inside the country, at a smaller scale, with better manners. It harvests brain drain from inner-city Chicago. It harvests South Central LA. It harvests all the public schools in Hawaii, lower Manhattan, the Bronx, Queens, Southie. It harvests the rural counties, where the machine has to drive farther between geniuses and runs the route anyway.
Narrator: The intake valves are boarding schools and colleges. The method is a hunt. Test scores. Teacher tips. A counselor who knows a name at a school back east. Find the genius early, while he is still findable, and get him into the pipeline before his zip code finishes with him. Every town knows the route by heart, because every town has watched it leave on schedule, one valedictorian at a time.
Narrator: I use the word abduction because it is the word that fits my own childhood. I was taken at six from New York to Hawaii, a move every adult around me called an opportunity, and it felt like a kidnapping with luggage. The harvested kid knows the feeling. The destination is better. The destination is always better. That is the design. A boy can be grateful and homesick in the same breath for sixty years.
Narrator: If my parents had been better at persuading me, I would have been a willing participant. Persuasion was available. Nobody attempted it. They just abducted me.
Narrator: My dad always told a story about why we moved. One day we were going to a friend's party in a doorman building, up in the penthouse. Walking in with my dad, the doorman opened the door, and my dad told the doorman thank you. I asked him: why do you tell him thank you? He's doing his job.
Narrator: A movie father kneels down at that point and says, son, every person is human, everybody deserves respect. My dad drew a different moral, and it became the story he always told: the reason we moved to Hawaii was that I was becoming a spoiled brat, and I needed to be removed, or I would be insufferable.
Narrator: That reason was a red herring. It had nothing to do with why we moved. And it fucked up my head and made me feel abducted. From 1976 to 1988 I felt like I was in a tough-love boot camp.
Narrator: And the formation was real. My mother raised me a fancy boy. She took me to plays, museums, symphonies, musicals—all of New York, administered on schedule to a small boy in good shoes. I was calibrated for the doorman building and the penthouse party. So the move to Hawaii was, very literally, a Green Acres experience: the city-formed kid set down in the country, Oliver Douglas in the suit, standing in the field. My father supplied the verdict on the move. My mother had supplied the boy it landed on. Paradise registered as Hooterville.
Narrator: All my dad needed to do was get me on board. That was the whole job. If he had sold it, I would have spent 1976 to 1988 thinking I had escaped an urban hell and was in fact living in paradise. Same island. Same years. The frame decided whether it was a punishment or a paradise, and nobody built me the frame.
Narrator: The harvested get a brochure, and a brochure is a frame. I got a red herring. Persuasion is the difference between a rescue and an abduction, and it is the one part of the machine nobody operates.
Narrator: The pipeline takes the brains, and the brains are the advertised cargo. It also takes a second cargo that never appears in the brochure: the leaders those places were growing for themselves, identified early, cultivated, and hired away before they ever led anybody anywhere.
Narrator: And the hunt has one honest feature worth naming. It wants capability, and it does not care where capability lives. The appetite is the whole ethic. The harvest has always taken freaking awesome at math wherever it finds it, in any skin, in any zip code, because a harvest that discriminates starves.
Narrator: The ledger nobody keeps
Narrator: Every harvest publishes its yield. The universities publish their entering classes. The hospitals publish their hires. The boarding schools publish their matriculation lists, which is a harvest word wearing a Latin dress.
Narrator: Nobody publishes the loss column.
Narrator: I wrote about this in the language of wells: everybody is in a well, and the drain decides whose well gets a ladder. The drain makes both wells. It makes the landscaped one brighter, year after year, genius after genius. It makes the kitchen-table one dimmer by exactly the same amount. Then the bright well looks down into the dim one and lectures it about its reading scores.
Narrator: A town can lose its best nurse to Houston and call it her success. It is her success. It is also the town's loss, and both entries belong in the ledger, and only one of them ever gets written down. Multiply her by every ward, every school, every county, every country, and you have the true account of the harvest: a fortune, itemized on one side only.
Narrator: The abduction works because everybody it touches can call it a rescue. The kid is rescued. The hospital is rescued. The university is rescued. The place he came from is the only party at the table who was never asked, and it pays in the only currency it had.
Narrator: Its future. One genius at a time.

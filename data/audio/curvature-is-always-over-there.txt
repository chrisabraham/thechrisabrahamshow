Narrator: Curvature Is Always Over There. By Chris Abraham.
Narrator: I love TikTok for exactly one reason: it is the only place in my intelligent life where I have to deal with flat Earth theory. Everywhere else I move—work, church, the library, the group chat—the question never comes up. On TikTok it comes up daily, posted under videos of missiles by people who mean it, and I enjoy it the way I enjoy a good argument about anything that has never once touched my life. Because that is the honest report from inside my life: whether the Earth is a globe or a flat plane has been, across fifty-six years and a fair amount of travel, completely irrelevant. Not mostly irrelevant. Completely. I assign neither one a probability. I have never had a day that required one. Gravity keeps me down, the air keeps me breathing, and the system works.
Narrator: The Earth is flat wherever I am standing. Within the few miles around me—the radius a man actually lives in—the ground is a plane, my floor is level, my walls are plumb, and curvature is always over there, somewhere past the horizon, in a place I am not. The claim concerns the shape of a life. A man stands at the top of whatever he is standing on. Gravity pulls toward the center, so wherever he goes he is on top, the way the bear at the circus is always on top of the ball no matter how fast he runs on it. I have never stood anywhere and felt the ground fall away around a curve. Nobody has. The curve is a fact other people keep for me, the way the bank keeps my money. I believe them. I have also never needed to make a withdrawal.
Narrator: I have seen the evidence, all of it, firsthand, and none of it decided anything. I have been to Finland, where the summer light refuses to end. I have been to New Zealand and Australia, where the sky is a different sky and the water is said to run the other way. I have stood in the desert under a Milky Way so thick it stops being astronomy and becomes weather. I have been out in the middle of the ocean on a boat in the dark and found the North Star and the three stars of the belt. Every one of those experiences arrived whole and explained itself to my satisfaction under either cosmology. The southern sky is strange and wonderful on a globe. It is strange and wonderful under a firmament too. My eyes filed no report either way, because my eyes were never asked the question.
Narrator: The machinery of my life is equally indifferent. GPS works. I turn it on, it tells me where I am, I go there. I do not care how it works—whether the satellites fly in low Earth orbit or hang from balloons under the dome is a question for the men who maintain it, and I am not one of them. I have seen falling stars; they exist. I have seen meteorites in museums; they exist. Neither has ever asked me to sign anything. Whether men walked on the moon is a question I have argued for pleasure and settled never, and it has changed no Tuesday of my life. Whether we are riding a ball of rock around a fireball in a solar system wheeling around a galaxy wheeling around the universe is, to the man buying coffee in Arlington, an academic matter in the purest sense: it belongs to the academy, and the academy is welcome to it.
Narrator: What I enjoy on TikTok is the seams. Flat Earth people who also believe in space aliens are mixing their metaphors. Aliens need space. A visitor from Alpha Centauri requires a universe with an outside, distances, other worlds. Under a firmament—God's terrarium, heaven above the dome—there is no Alpha Centauri to come from, and a visitor who comes through the dome is not an alien. He is a demon, or a hybrid, or one of the kinds of creature the Flood was sent to deal with. A man has to pick his cosmology; both at once is a mixed metaphor. Watching that seam is the same pleasure as Carl Sagan walking me from Mercury out to Pluto when I was young. Both are systems. Both are gorgeous. Both are entertainment for a man whose rent is due either way. I consume cosmologies the way I consume histories of Rome: with real pleasure and no operational dependence.
Narrator: And the firmament, taken on its own terms, is a kept place. The cheap shot says Earth is the only flat planet while the rest are spheres, and it misses the model entirely: the firmament allows no spheres at all and no planets at all. Everything overhead is clockwork turning in the dome and pictures set into it—stars the way a father paints stars on a baby's ceiling, a mobile hung over the crib to keep the child entertained. It is a terrarium, and God keeps it the way I keep my chameleon Spike. I control Spike's water. I control his humidity. I control how much sun he gets, by way of the heat lamp, and how much light, and for how long. The keeper tends every condition of the small world because the creature inside it is his. That is the whole of the model: a world run by hand, by a keeper, because he is a good daddy.
Narrator: Recited plainly, the official story is a fantasy of the same size. A ball flings itself through the universe around a yellow star bigger than some stars and smaller than others, at the perfect Goldilocks distance, with air, and nothing else alive anywhere anyone can find. Fewer people on this Earth believe that story than its tellers assume, and the number is not cosmology's fault to fix by scolding. Shame on cosmology—shame on it for losing to astrology, and for losing to a flat Earth abandoned a thousand years ago. It had the schools, the telescopes, the space programs, and the centuries, and it is losing to a dome. The worse the system frays down here, the less any of it matters: a man whose world is coming apart has no use for NASA, and outside the nerds, heaven right above the firmament and hell right below it sounds exactly as likely as infinite turtles. Every cosmology is a narrative. I choose neither, and I am fascinated by both, the way a man watches two horses he has no money on. And the field is crowded: Sagan's cosmos, visitors from the stars, reptilians under the earth, Tartaria, ancient aliens, the Anunnaki. Every one of them is a toy of the brain, catnip for an itchy brain, mine very much included. I am apathetic about the contest and delighted by the toys.
Narrator: The root of it is probably the wiring. I have aphantasia, so I cannot summon a picture in my mind, and SDAM, so my own past reaches me without scenery. A man who cannot picture the Earth from space has no image to defend, and the music of the spheres reaches me exactly the way the firmament does—as an arrangement of words. I find them equally compelling and equally amusing, and I mean that as a plain statement of fact about myself.
Narrator: The category is bigger than the Earth. Dinosaurs or dragons—the bones are in the ground either way, and the men who dig them up disagree about the story in voices I find entertaining on both sides. Oil from a billion years of pressed forests, or oil made abiotically in the deep Earth and still making itself—my car ran on it under both theories, back when I had a car. I have never been to Africa. I believe in cheetahs. I believed in cheetahs before I ever saw one at the National Zoo, and I would believe in them if every zoo closed tomorrow, because belief, in my actual practice, runs on interest and testimony, and almost nothing I believe has ever been load-bearing. The things that hold my life up are few and near: the ground under my feet, the people who answer when I call, the work on the desk. Everything past the horizon is held the way I hold cheetahs—gladly, and at arm's length.
Narrator: Donald Rumsfeld said the honest version of this at a Pentagon briefing in February 2002, and got laughed at for the one true thing said at a podium that year. There are known knowns, things we know we know. There are known unknowns, things we know we don't know. And there are unknown unknowns—the ones we don't know we don't know. I plead Rumsfeld. My sureness about reality is exactly as wide as my life and not one mile wider. Inside my fifteen minutes, the Earth is flat, the coffee is hot, and the GPS works. Past my fifteen minutes, both stories sit where the cheetahs sit: held on testimony, with pleasure, and with no stake in being right. Curvature is always over there. So is everything else I play with. The only thing I believe in is Jesus. And I don't think Jesus gives any fucks about whether we live inside a symphony of the spheres or under a firmament.

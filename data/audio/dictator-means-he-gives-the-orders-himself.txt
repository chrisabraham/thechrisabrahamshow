Dictator Means He Gives the Orders Himself

On August 25, 2025, in the Oval Office, Donald Trump signed the orders sending National Guard troops into Washington's streets and then did his critics' act for them, out loud, in his own voice: "They say, 'We don't need 'em. Freedom, freedom. He's a dictator, he's a dictator.' A lot of people are saying, 'Maybe we like a dictator.' I don't like a dictator. I'm not a dictator. I'm a man with great common sense and a smart person." That afternoon, Governor J.B. Pritzker of Illinois stood up in Chicago and called him a wannabe dictator. Both men were describing the same afternoon. Only one of them was describing a crime.

When the left calls Trump a dictator, they think they have insulted him. They have not, because the word does not mean what they need it to mean. Dictator is a good thing. Authoritarianism is the bad thing, and the two words are not synonyms, no matter how often they get stacked into the same sentence. The language itself sorts them before any politics gets a vote. People say "a dictator"—a person, singular, one man who acts. The word is the natural word for a right-winger. People say "an authoritarian government"—a system, a machine. Nobody says "a dictator government." Nobody calls a swashbuckling general "an authoritarian." The person-word belongs to the man. The system-word belongs to the apparatus, and the apparatus has an address in history: the East German Stasi state. Files on everybody. A permit for everything. Watchers in every stairwell, neighbors informing on neighbors, everybody administered and nobody in particular acting. Even the modern scholarship, on the occasions when it bothers to separate the two words, separates them the same way: authoritarianism describes the scope of a system, dictatorship identifies who holds the agency. The academy's distinction and the folk distinction rhyme, because both are just reading the words.

A dictator, by the language's own sorting, is one man acting—decisively, taking, doing—and answering for it afterward. Under dictatorship you don't ask permission, you ask forgiveness. That is the whole American romance with the type, and I will not pretend otherwise: America fuck yeah is about dictatorship, it's not about authoritarianism—it's about let's get shit done, roll over everything, take what you want. It does not mean camps. It does not mean cancelled elections—they keep the word "wannabe" taped to the front of it precisely because the elections keep happening and the courts keep ruling against him and the satire shows keep airing. What it means, in their mouths, defined by ten years of use, is a president who gives orders and expects them obeyed. A president who commands the executive branch from the top, the way a general commands an army, and who does not route his decisions through the permanent government—the agencies, the career staff, the process—for their approval first. Decisiveness they do not control is what the word names. Fine. I take the word on exactly those terms, because those terms describe a man doing the job in the open, his name on the order, the blame addressed to him personally. The thing worth fearing wears the other word, and the other word is theirs.

The word has a birth certificate, and it is a republican one. In Rome, dictator was not an insult and it was not a coup. It was an office inside the Republic's own arrangements: in an emergency, a consul named one man and handed him complete authority for the crisis at hand, and the grant carried its own fuse—six months at the outside, or the end of the appointed task, whichever came first. The standing example is Cincinnatus, found at his plow in 458 BC with a Roman army trapped by the Aequi. He took the power, beat the enemy, and resigned in fifteen days, with months of legal authority still sitting unused in his hand, and went back across the Tiber to finish plowing. So the first meaning of the word in the Western record is not a tyrant who seized a state. It is the republic's own emergency instrument: legal, bounded by the task, and surrendered. The original dictator asked neither permission nor forgiveness of a mob. He was handed the power by the constitution, and he handed it back.

Day One

The confession the whole charge rests on was made on December 5, 2023, in Davenport, Iowa, at a Fox News town hall. Sean Hannity was begging. He had played the montage—"sleepwalking into a Trump dictatorship," the Washington Post's "fear of a looming Trump dictatorship"—and he wanted the promise on the record: "Under no circumstances, you are promising America tonight, you would never abuse power as retribution against anybody?" Trump answered: "Except for day one." Hannity, going crazy by his own later description, asked what that meant, and Trump told him. Dictatorship, day one, the complete program: "We're closing the border and we're drilling, drilling, drilling. After that, I'm not a dictator, okay?" Two orders. Close the border. Drill. That is the smoking gun, produced in evidence at every alarm since: a man announcing that on his first day in office he would use the authority of the office, immediately, in public, on two named jobs, and then stop being whatever they were calling him. The horror was never the content of the orders. Half the country wanted both orders given. The horror was the posture—a president talking like a commander instead of a supplicant.

He has been doing the impression ever since, because he knows exactly what the word has shrunk to. On January 21, 2026, at a reception for business leaders in Davos, he stood in front of the richest audience on earth and reviewed his own reviews: "Usually they say, 'He's a horrible, dictator-type person.' I'm a dictator. But sometimes you need a dictator. But they didn't say that in this case. And no, it's common sense. It's all based on common sense." The official transcript marks laughter in the room. A man does not perform his indictment as a joke, to applause, in Switzerland, if the indictment still has voltage in it. He performs it because he has heard the definition from the inside and found it small: dictator is what they call the man whose orders they did not write.

The General's Crime

An army is the one American institution that still runs honestly top-down, and nobody apologizes for it. George Patton did not workshop the Third Army's advance across France. Douglas MacArthur did not circulate a memo inviting the bureaucracy's feelings about Inchon. A general's orders go down the chain and come back executed, and when the orders are good we call it command and when they are bad we call it command responsibility, and at no point in the proceeding does anyone suggest that the structure itself—the giving of orders by one man, binding on thousands—is tyranny. Command is the job description. The battlefield is not even the excuse; the excuse is that somebody has to be in charge of the thing, visibly, by name, so that the blame has an address too.

MacArthur is the full-size version, and he had already run a country before Korea. From 1945 to 1951 he sat in Tokyo as Supreme Commander for the Allied Powers, ruling occupied Japan the way the shoguns had ruled it—Japanese political and civilian figures of the day said exactly that—and when Tokyo's own attempt at a new constitution came back with the old order barely scratched, his headquarters staff wrote the replacement themselves in February 1946. It was promulgated that November and took effect on May 3, 1947, and Japan lives under it still. That is the closest an American has ever come to actually being a dictator: one man, a defeated nation, a constitution out of his own headquarters. And he held all of it under orders, on a commission from a president, revocable by the same hand that signed it.

Korea is where the picture in the American head comes from. On September 15, 1950, over the Joint Chiefs' doubts, he landed an invasion force at Inchon, behind the North Korean army, and the war turned in a day. Then he kept going—past the 38th parallel, up toward the Yalu River, with a home-by-Christmas offensive launched on November 24. China came across the river in force at the end of November, some three hundred thousand men, and the war turned back. MacArthur's answer was a bigger war: bomb the bases and factories in Manchuria, blockade China's coast, bring Chiang Kai-shek's Nationalist troops in. As a serving commander he had already asked Washington, in December 1950, for a field commander's discretion to use atomic weapons, and sent in his list of retardation targets. In interviews published after his death he put the numbers on the full version: between 30 and 50 atomic bombs strung across the neck of Manchuria, and behind his advancing troops a belt of radioactive cobalt spread from the Sea of Japan to the Yellow Sea—poisoned ground no army could march across for at least sixty years. Washington said no. He went around Washington. On March 24, 1951, he issued his own ultimatum to the Chinese commander, a statement of policy no president had approved—Truman said later his mind was made up at the ultimatum. His letter to Congressman Joseph W. Martin Jr., dated March 20, 1951 and read aloud on the House floor on April 5, informed Congress that there is no substitute for victory. On April 11, 1951, Harry Truman relieved him of his commands, with the unanimous concurrence of the Joint Chiefs of Staff.

That MacArthur type is exactly what Donald Trump is, and I say it as praise, because the type is a superpower. Douglas MacArthur as a type for Donald Trump is a superpower. He is not controllable. He really pushes the edge of what is remotely capable. He does not stick within the confines of norms and values. He is not willing to work within a box of his own making—he makes the entire world find a box around him.

What the type meant in MacArthur was insubordination. There was a civilian power above him and he defied it, in print, twice, and the firing was not the republic failing. The firing was the republic working. And look at what fired him. It was not a norm. It was not decorum, not precedent, not the unwritten rule that generals do not embarrass presidents. The unwritten rules had been sitting there available to restrain him for months, and they restrained nothing—he issued the ultimatum anyway, he wrote the letter anyway. What contained Douglas MacArthur was explicit, and it had a name and a date on it: a chain of command with Harry Truman at the top, and a signed relief of command on April 11, 1951. Rank, paper, and a signature. That is the only box that has ever held a man of this type.

Trump is the civilian power at the top of that same chain. There is no Truman above him for him to defy. He is the man the MacArthur story says does the firing. So his box gets built from outside, by the world, in writing—and the writing exists, with names and dates on it like the relief order. A federal court's injunction names the act it enjoins, and his lawyers fight it in court, on the docket, in the open. The states run their own elections and their own affairs, and his first term ended on January 20, 2021 because an election said it ended, and he boarded the helicopter on the South Lawn and left. The House impeached him twice under Article II, Section 4, the Senate tried him twice, and both times the written mechanism ran to its written end. Ahead of him sits the hardest line in the document: the Twenty-Second Amendment. Two terms. No third, no exception, no reading of it that bends. Court orders, elections, impeachment, a term limit with a number in it—that is the explicit box, and it is written down, and it is working.

The complaint that survives, once the explicit box is on the table, is about the other box: the implicit one. The norms. The decorum. The way things are done, the unwritten rules a president is expected to build around himself and stay inside. But the implicit box is not a box. It never held MacArthur for one afternoon in 1951. An unwritten rule is a request dressed up as a restraint, and a man of this type hears a request as a request and declines it. So the left points at the declined request and calls the declining dictatorship, while the machinery that actually contains a president—injunctions, elections, an amendment with a number in it—sits in plain sight doing the containing. A man who makes the entire world find a box around him, and whose world builds it out of written law, is not the dictator in the MacArthur story. He is the reason the story's ending is written into the Constitution: the elected commander commands, the general who defies him goes home, and nobody in the republic outranks the paper.

The permanent government had arranged a different settlement. Two million civilian employees, agencies with their own lawyers and their own agendas and their own memories longer than any presidency, a process engineered so that an elected president's order arrives at the machine as a suggestion the machine may edit, delay, leak, or quietly strangle in committee. That settlement had held so long it had come to feel like the Constitution itself. Trump's offense—the whole of it, the thing underneath every specific outrage—is that he never signed the settlement. He treats the executive branch as an instrument he commands, because Article II says it is his, and he treats an unexecuted order as a broken one and the man who broke it as removable. To the people who staff the machine, that is not management. That is the lived experience of dictatorship: being commanded, from above, by an outsider who does not ask the guild's permission. Their word measures one thing only, and it measures it accurately. It measures the direction of the order. Down from the Oval Office, from a man they did not choose and cannot remove: dictator. Any other direction: not counted, not named, not seen.

Vote and Forget

The American design is the Roman fuse rebuilt as a calendar. The people choose the man, and then—this is the part that gets called dictatorship—political action belongs to him. Joseph Schumpeter wrote it down without flinching in 1942, in Capitalism, Socialism and Democracy: democracy is a method, an institutional arrangement in which individuals acquire the power to decide by means of a competitive struggle for the people's vote, and the deciding of issues by the electorate is secondary to the election of the men who are to do the deciding. His condition for the method working was a division of labor the voters themselves respect: "once they have elected an individual, political action is his business and not theirs," and they must refrain from instructing him about what he is to do—no backseat driving, as the later summaries of his position put it. Edmund Burke had handed the elected man the same charter a century and a half earlier, standing in front of the electors of Bristol in 1774, on the day they chose him: a representative owes his electors not his industry only, but his judgment, and he betrays them if he sacrifices his judgment to their opinion. Vote, and then the man governs. That is not a defect smuggled into the design. That is the design.

And the box around the governing is dated, on purpose. A president holds a fixed term, independent of parliamentary votes of confidence, removable between elections only by the drastic step of impeachment. Juan Linz laid the trade out in 1990, in "The Perils of Presidentialism," and named the fixed term and the president's separate, plebiscitary claim to legitimacy as the system's defining features—his warning was precisely that the rigidity cuts both ways: no tether, no no-confidence vote waiting to pull a government down mid-stream when a chamber's mood turns. The insulation is deliberate. The reckoning is scheduled instead: the next election, and behind it the Twenty-Second Amendment. Continuous supervision between those dates was never assigned to anybody. It is not in the elected man's job description to submit to it, and it is not in anybody else's to exercise it.

So look at where the dictatorship charge actually comes from. It comes at mid-term, on schedule, out of the pile-on: the standing demand that the elected man submit to continuous supervision by the unelected—the media, the agencies, the permanent government, the whole administrative temperament that wants to manage him between elections. He was hired to govern. The voters chose him, in large part, precisely because he would not be managed. And the instinct to manage him anyway, to stand over the elected man's shoulder with a clipboard for four straight years, is not democracy defending itself. It is the apparatus's temperament—authoritarianism's temperament—applied to the one man in the building whose authority came out of a vote. The system-word cannot abide the person-word holding office. So it calls his governing dictatorship and its own supervision freedom, and hopes nobody checks the dictionary, or the calendar.

The Other Direction

Now run the tape the other way, and use the right word for what is on it. None of what follows has a dictator in it. No man on a balcony, no signature on an order, nobody in particular acting at all. It has files, instruments, delegated enforcement, and pressure applied by everyone to anyone. That is authoritarianism—the system-word, at work, in the country that calls one acting man a tyrant.

On April 18, 2020, the mayor of New York City, Bill de Blasio, went on video and asked the people of his city to police each other: if you see someone failing to keep his distance, snap a photo, set the location, text it to 311-692, and the enforcement will come. New Yorkers obliged by the thousands. Police responded to 20,805 of those tips—neighbors reporting neighbors for standing too close together in the open air—and the line itself had to be shut down for a time under the weight of the city's contempt for it. No dictator ordered that from a balcony. A mayor built the instrument and the crowd supplied the surveillance, sideways, free of charge.

On November 5, 2021, the Occupational Safety and Health Administration published a rule that did not command a single American citizen to do anything. It commanded his employer instead. Every business with a hundred or more workers was ordered to make its own people get vaccinated against COVID, or test weekly at their own expense and mask—some 84 million workers in all—and the enforcement was delegated downward into every workplace in the country, boss to worker, company by company. The Supreme Court stayed the rule on January 13, 2022, and the Court's own summary of it contains the whole design in five words: "the mandate, which employers must enforce." The state pointed; the sideways machinery squeezed. Nobody in the administration called it authoritarian, and neither did the people now counting Trump's orders, because no single man could be photographed giving the command. There was no commander. There was only pressure, applied everywhere, by everyone, to anyone who held out.

On January 7, 2021, Facebook blocked the sitting President of the United States from his own accounts, indefinitely. On January 8, Twitter permanently suspended him—88 million followers gone with a blog post about risk. Not a court. Not Congress. Not a vote anywhere. Two companies, egged on by the same political class that now audits Trump's every order for dictatorial content, removed the elected head of the executive branch from the public square in the middle of his term, and the removal was celebrated as a defense of democracy. I am not arguing the companies lacked the legal right. I am noting the direction. A president giving an order is tyranny in their accounting. A president being silenced by his inferiors is hygiene. The compass does not wobble. It was built to point the one way.

Why the Word Won't Stick

You can watch the same word do the same work in an ordinary house. A spouse who likes a strong spouse, and respects that strong spouse, never calls that spouse a dictator. The strength, the decisiveness, the forward push—while the respect holds, all of it has another name: leadership. The word dictator shows up at exactly one moment, and it is not a moment when the strong spouse does anything new. It is the moment the respect goes. The man has not changed. The same push that was leadership on Monday is renamed destructive on Friday, and a strong leader only becomes a dictator when and if there is any sign of disrespect. The label is not a report on his conduct. It is a report on the caller's respect, filed at the moment the respect was withdrawn.

It runs in the family at every age. The child says: I love it when my daddy teaches me things and tells me what to do—I love the framework, I love the structure of having a strong family unit. Fifteen years old, same house, same parents, same authority: Fuck you, Mom and Dad, I'm my own person, you're not the boss of me. Security at six. Oppression at fifteen. The authority did not move an inch; the stage moved. I wonder if this all has developmental cycles—whether America cycles through baby, toddler, kindergartner stages, the whole national childhood, and whether the woke left is America's teenage phase: I hate you, Dad, no more hugs from mommy and daddy. A teenager's rejection is a phase a family survives. It is not a verdict on the parent.

And a parent facing that teenager has exactly two honest choices, and everybody who has raised one knows both of them. You either have to let your child be free or you need to send them to fucking military school. Freedom, or total explicit structure—written rules, a chain of command, consequences with dates on them. There is no third option on the menu. The nagging is not an option. The norms, the sighs, the continuous supervision, the unwritten rules of the house enforced by atmosphere—that middle way is the one thing that has never worked on a strong-willed kid and never will. The parent who refuses to choose gets the worst of both: a child neither free nor formed, and a house run by resentment. The left's whole demand, applied to the country, is the impossible third option—a strong leader who also asks permission, a teenager who governs himself by their unwritten rules—and the honest name for the option they actually practice is the second one. Military school for the whole country. The administered state: every behavior filed, every workplace deputized, every neighbor on watch. They did not choose it honestly, but they built it, and it is theirs.

A state does the same thing to a generation that a parent does to a son, and it does it at scale: it sends the young men to war. Vietnam was military school. Korea was military school. The draft is the instrument—with a draft you can spank the disrespectful, decimate a generation's young men, and call it policy. The Global War on Terror does not count in my accounting, because it was a professional, volunteer army; nobody was sent, they signed. Vietnam was the other thing. Vietnam was the equivalent of Passover—the firstborn of every family sacrificed because the generation had displeased God. That is the fork at national size: freedom, or the explicit box a state builds around a generation that will not be managed, chosen by the state and paid for by the sons.

The draft is what made the state the author of the sacrifice, and that is why the complaint belonged to that era: the state took them, so the state answered for them. A professional army runs the ledger the other way. With a professional army, zero families complain about the war, because the families who send children to a professional war know what the game is. The signature changes the moral ledger. Nobody was taken. If parents fooled their own children into throwing themselves at something irresponsible, that is on the parents, not the state. And nobody gets to plead innocence anymore, either. After Vietnam, everybody should know better. Vietnam is the tuition the whole country paid, and tuition buys a lesson: the knowledge is standing there in the record, paid for in firstborn sons, and later innocence is unavailable as an excuse.

A besmirch has to make its target smaller in the eyes of the audience it is performed for, and this one cannot, because the audience hears the word already translated. They reached for the person-word because it is the strongest word they own, and the person-word is praise wearing a scowl. To the people who vote for Trump, "dictator" arrives with his own gloss printed on it: a man who gives orders and gets things done while the permanent government screams. Every year the word is used, it is used about border enforcement, troop deployments, agency firings, drilling—about command, exercised openly, on subjects where his voters wanted command exercised and had been promised it. The accusation keeps confirming the advertisement.

They have run this exact play before and kept the receipt. On September 9, 2016, Hillary Clinton told a fundraiser that half of Trump's supporters could be put into "what I call the basket of deplorables"—racist, sexist, homophobic, xenophobic, Islamophobic, you name it—and that some of those folks were "irredeemable." Within days the insulted had printed the word on their own shirts. Deplorable became a name tag, a handle, a badge worn to rallies by the people it was built to shame, because the people it was built to shame heard in it not a description of themselves but a description of her contempt—and contempt, answered with laughter, is fuel. "Dictator" is traveling the same road at a heavier weight. Trump already repeats it on stage, does the critics' voices, lands the punchline himself. A slur the target tells as a joke about his accusers has been disarmed in the only way that matters: nobody it is aimed at is afraid of it, and everybody it is aimed at understands it to mean the opposite of what the speaker intends.

Here is the whole of it, from where I sit. A dictator is a man. Authoritarianism is a machine. Trump is the man—one man acting, in the open, orders signed, his name on everything, asking forgiveness and never permission, and the republic's written box built around him by a world that had to go and find it. The machine is the other thing, the sideways government of tip lines and mandates-by-employer and platform bans, the files and the permits and the neighbors informing, and that machine is his accusers' own build, staffed by them, defended by them, never once called by its own word. They pay him the MacArthur compliment in the person-word and run the Stasi word at home and cannot see the swap, because for the people inside the machine, the machine's autonomy was the democracy. His voters see the swap plainly. They will keep saying dictator. He will keep answering with common sense and a grin. And the word will keep doing for him what deplorables did—rounding up his people and handing them a name to march under.

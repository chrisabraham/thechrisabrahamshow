Narrator: The Professional Coward. By Chris Abraham.
Narrator: Here is what they teach you when you get a concealed carry permit, the part nobody puts on the certificate: the moment the sheriff hands you that license, you become a coward. A professional one. It is your job now. You avoid violence. You avoid aggression. You avoid cops while they're doing their duty, you avoid violent people, you avoid the antisocial, you avoid the argument in the parking lot and the drunk at the bar and the stranger who wants to know what you're looking at. You do not brandish. You do not carry a fancy gun. You do not interject, you do not de-escalate with your chest, you do not be a hero. You take the road least traveled by, every time, in every weather, and you let the other man win every contest that doesn't end with somebody dead. The permit is a license to carry a gun on the condition that you reorganize your entire life so you never need it.
Narrator: Understand what the word means before you flinch at it, because I am calling myself a coward as a matter of fact. Cowardice is the varmint's discipline: the fox, the rat, the opossum, the mouse. None of those animals is a coward in the cartoon sense, and every one of them lives by the same rule—a varmint that gets seen has already failed. If the opossum is discovered, it was not cowardly enough. If the rat is eyeballed, it was not cowardly enough. The perfected coward never appears in the story at all. There is no such thing as a coward with his hands up: hands up is a scene, a confrontation, a moment where other men are deciding your fate, and a man standing in that scene shouting his innocence at armed strangers is not a coward. He is just stupid, and he arrived at stupidity by skipping every exit the coward took. The industry prefers "avoidance," "de-escalation," "situational awareness," because coward doesn't sell classes. My instructor said coward because euphemisms don't survive contact. The sheriff who signed my permit gave the companion order, and it was just as plain: tell nobody. Not friends, not family, not the internet. A secret told is a secret spent, and a man known to carry has already surrendered the only advantage concealment ever gave him. Go left when he goes right. Nobody should ever learn you are armed until the moment a man is on top of you and about to kill you or someone beside you. Until that moment, you are a ghost with a secret.
Narrator: Constitutional carry and a CCW are completely different things, and the difference matters here. A permit comes with a classroom, a standard, and a doctrine, and the doctrine is retreat. Let us assume Alex Pretti held a permit—Minnesota requires one, and the police said he carried legally. Then Alex Pretti knew the doctrine, because they teach it to everyone, and he broke every article of it in a single morning. He carried a gun into a space of general violence. He went looking at a federal operation. He interjected himself between agents and their work. He got into a scrum, on the ground, with armed men, while armed. A professional coward does none of that. A professional coward isn't on that street, and if he is on that street, he is walking away from the noise, filming from a doorway like a man who intends to be alive for dinner.
Narrator: And you never, ever take a gun to a protest. We ran this experiment in Kenosha. A kid brought an AR-15 to a riot, said he was there to be a medic, and the rifle did what a rifle in a chaotic street does: it drew every eye, every threat, every challenger, until the evening produced the gunfight the rifle had been promising since he slung it. He was acquitted, and the acquittal is beside the point. The point is the magnet. A visible or discoverable gun in a crowd under stress reorganizes the crowd around the gun. In Minneapolis, when the agents found Alex Pretti's gun in the scrum, the scrum became a shooting. It looks like a summary execution. It might very well be a summary execution. The doctrine doesn't care which, because the doctrine exists to keep you out of the half-second where that question gets decided.
Narrator: And there is a name for a varmint that charges. A fox that comes at you in daylight, a raccoon that advances, a dog or a rat or a squirrel that attacks—nobody reads aggression in a shy animal as courage. They read it as rabies, distemper, a brain on fire, and the response to rabies has never been negotiation. The rabid animal is destroyed on sight, and nobody afterward holds an inquiry into whether it meant well. I hate to say it about a dead man who cannot answer: Alex Pretti was brave, and he rushed toward violence while carrying concealed, and he did it again and again. An armed man advancing on armed men gets read the way a charging fox gets read. That is how he was treated, and the treatment followed the oldest protocol there is for the animal that will not run.
Narrator: There is a colder layer under that half-second, and the memo never touches it. By January, Alex Pretti was not a stranger to those operations. He had attended event after event; he had interdicted, obstructed, inserted himself, week after week, on camera. Men remember faces. If you piss off an officer of the law often enough, you may accidentally fall on a banana peel one day and split your head open, and no force field on earth prevents it, because recognition is not part of any legal test. It is part of war. The first rule of the battlefield is to take out the sniper—the one effective man, the known quantity, the cadre who makes the resistance work. A protest movement that sends the same faces into the same perimeter every week is building a target list and handing it to the other side, one familiar face at a time.
Narrator: None of this required malice aforethought in a briefing room. It required only a recurring man, a recognized face, a discovered gun, and a scrum—the ingredients assembled themselves, because Alex Pretti assembled three of them and carried the fourth in his waistband.
Narrator: The coward is not defenseless, and this is the part nobody talks about. The rattlesnake is a coward: it wants the rocks, the shade, and to be left alone, and it flees any fight it can flee. The bee is a coward. The rat, the fox, the jackal, the coyote—the whole shy nation avoids, evades, and disappears, and every one of them carries a retaliation plan for the day avoidance fails. The stinger is not a contradiction of the cowardice. The stinger is its completion: a last argument, delivered at contact range, that says get off me.
Narrator: My guns were chosen by that doctrine, back when I carried. A Kel-Tec P32 and a Seecamp LWS32, both chambered in .32 ACP—mouse guns, belly guns, a caliber the modern gun culture laughs at because it fails the FBI penetration standards. The Seecamp does not even have sights; the top of the slide is smooth and polished, because the gun was never meant to be aimed at anything farther away than a man already on top of you. I kept full metal jacket in it, because hollow points in a .32 will not expand and will not fight through a winter coat for the privilege of failing. My CM9, a small 9mm with a three-inch barrel and six rounds in the magazine, was the big gun of the set, and it is a seven-yard gun. None of them is a sheepdog's tool. None of them was bought to cruise a food court hunting a mass shooter to duel at fifty yards. They are get-off-me guns, engineered for one sentence: you are on me, and I am about to die.
Narrator: There are two kinds of carriers in this world. The sheepdog goes out cruising the fields for wolves, and his gun is a vocation; he trains for the day the wolf obliges him. The varmint carries a stinger and hopes to die of old age with the secret intact. And here is the joke the sheepdogs miss: if the worst moment ever does come, in some restaurant, it will not be ended by the man scanning the room. Nobody assumes anybody carries anymore, so it ends, if it ends, at bad-breath distance, by some nobody the shooter never noticed—holding a ridiculous little .32, the 7.65 that European police and detectives and army officers carried for half a century, the caliber of James Bond's actual PPK before the films upsized him, the upgrade from a .25 Beretta with a skeletonized grip. The invisible man with the mouse gun. The coward, completing his doctrine.
Narrator: So the permit's paradox stands, and it is the whole lesson. The state does not license you to be braver than other men. It licenses you to carry the means of killing on the sworn understanding that you will run from everything short of the one moment running fails. Alex Pretti carried like a hero, intervened like a hero, and died in the exact half-second the professional coward spends his whole life refusing to enter. The coward is home tonight. He will be home every night. That is not the failure of the doctrine. That is the doctrine, working as designed.

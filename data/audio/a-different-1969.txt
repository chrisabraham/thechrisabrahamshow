Narrator: A Different 1969. By Chris Abraham.
Narrator: The brain drain's deepest theft is not talent. It is leadership, taken before it forms.
Narrator: Everybody counts the brains. The brains are easy to count: degrees, salaries, patents, admissions letters photographed on kitchen tables. Leadership refuses the arithmetic. A leader is worth nothing on a spreadsheet and everything on a street, and the street is where the loss lands.
Narrator: So the ledger of the harvest, the one I took apart in the harvest machine that does the taking, is missing its largest entry. This piece is about that entry.
Narrator: What a leader is made of
Narrator: A leader is a local product. He is known on the block. He speaks the dialect of the place, all of its dialects: the church one, the corner one, the kitchen-table one. His authority comes from presence. He was there. He stayed.
Narrator: King had that. Malcolm X had that. Whatever else divided them, both men were of their people in the most literal way a man can be: reachable, touchable, standing in the room. Their gifts were national. Their addresses were local. The combination is the whole recipe, and it cannot be shipped in from a campus three states away.
Narrator: Leadership also has a childhood. The future leader is a kid first: the one who organizes the block, talks the teacher down, holds the room at the lunch table. Charisma shows early. So does the machinery built to find it.
Narrator: The identification machine
Narrator: The pipeline that hunts genius hunts charisma with the same nets, because the two so often arrive in the same kid. The boy who would have organized the tenant meeting at thirty is the boy who gets the scholarship interview at fourteen. The girl who would have run the church's whole future is the girl the boarding school flies out for a weekend in October.
Narrator: Nobody in the machine is a villain. The admissions officer believes in the kid. The kid believes in the chance. The family believes in the door. Everybody is right, and the block still loses its Moses.
Narrator: The gifts get bought early, and the buyer's business is elsewhere. That is the entire mechanism. A movement needs its best talker aimed at the movement. The market aims him at a portfolio.
Narrator: If you got Malcolm X and MLK into finance after an awesome stint at Dartmouth it might be a different 1969.
Narrator: Sit with what that sentence actually rearranges. The marches still get organized, maybe, by the second-best talkers. The sermons get preached by the men the pipeline graded as leftovers. Or the whole thing simply happens smaller, later, led by whoever the harvest declined to take. History's hinge turns out to be a recruiting decision.
Narrator: The placement corollary
Narrator: The drain has a second half. After you take the brains, you have to put them somewhere.
Narrator: Placement is the bottleneck, and tolerance is what widens it. Picture the old corporate America: white only, and not even Jews allowed. That world could want Malcolm X's brain—it could want it badly—and still have nowhere to put him. The barred door was evil, full stop. It was also, by accident, a wall. A people's leadership stayed home behind it, because home was the only address where it was allowed to work.
Narrator: Scale it by the biggest name the era had. Frank Sinatra was as powerful as a private citizen got in that America, and he had to stand up to venue owners because Sammy Davis Jr.—his best friend, a headliner—could play the room and still be barred from sleeping in the hotel or coming in through the front door. Sinatra spent his leverage on it, again and again, and the leverage of the biggest star in the country bought inches. That was the placement capacity of the old world: nearly zero, defended at the level of a hotel registry. The drain of that era leaked talent. The total harvest was impossible, because there was almost nowhere to put the taken.
Narrator: And the famous case was the rule wearing a tuxedo. Hundreds of Black performers in the thirties, forties, and fifties headlined rooms they could not eat in. The diner was closed to them. The hotel was closed to them. After the gig, the star of the show could not sit down out front and watch the other acts; he went back to the one room the audience never saw, and he stayed backstage. The arrangement also outlived its legal life by years. In plenty of places it ran well into the seventies on custom alone—the law had changed, the welcome hadn't, and "your type isn't welcome around here" did the work the statutes used to do.
Narrator: Road-test the era with a bus. One of my favorite movies of all time is The Adventures of Priscilla, Queen of the Desert: drag queens crossing the Australian outback in a bus, in full regalia, buying fuel and drinks and trouble in small towns. The outback mostly let that bus pass. Now run the same bus through the Southeast of 1968. Every diner on the route, every motel, every gas station answers to the backstage rule, and the bus is carrying exactly the people the rule was built for. The trip stops being a comedy at the first fuel pump.
Narrator: Every door that opens widens the drain. The less racist, the less homophobic, the more allied corporate America makes itself, the more aggressively it can harvest, because its placement capacity grows with its tolerance. The slots multiply. A mid-six-figure income for the solid ones. Seven figures for the very best. A title, a team, a grateful life, a mortgage in a town with excellent schools.
Narrator: The future César Chávez gets an offer letter. He would be a fool to refuse it. The machine counts on his not being a fool, and the machine is rarely disappointed.
Narrator: Inclusion is the drain's lubricant. The welcome mat and the extraction machine are the same apparatus, viewed from opposite ends of the well. From the top, it looks like progress, and it is progress, for the man holding the offer. From the bottom, it looks like the last of the leaders leaving with a relocation package.
Narrator: Measure the distance inside one lifetime. Mine. I was born in 1970, into the tail of the backstage era—the custom years, when the law had changed and the welcome lagged behind it. A man born then has lived in both countries: the one where the headliner slept somewhere else, and this one, where the corporation competes for the headliner's grandchildren and puts them on the website.
Narrator: I wish people really knew how anti-racist modern America is compared to the America of my birth year. The change is real. It rebuilt the doorways, the registries, the front entrances of American life. And the reality of it is the engine of this whole piece: real welcome means real placement capacity, and real placement capacity means the drain never has to stop at the color line again. The machine that once leaked talent for want of somewhere to put it now keeps a slot for everyone it takes. Progress built the slots. The slots emptied the streets.
Narrator: This is also why the harvest can afford its sincerity about talent. The machine took NASA's computers on pure capability in a segregated decade; it has always paid for performance and let the prejudices of the era bill somebody else. Sincerity is easy when every opened door increases the yield.
Narrator: The scholarship brochure
Narrator: What does the drained place get in exchange? A poster.
Narrator: The poster hangs in the school hallway: the graduate, the gown, the university seal, proof the pipeline works. The poster is true. The kid did make it out. The poster is also the receipt for the extraction, framed and hung in the building it emptied.
Narrator: The leadership chair stays empty. It stays empty quietly, which is the genius of the arrangement. An assassinated leader makes martyrs and movements. A hired leader makes a success story. The people he would have led get the story. They get to be proud of him, at a distance, in a town he visits at Thanksgiving.
Narrator: I wrote in the asymmetry of grace that one man gets a backstory and the other gets a caption. The drained places get a caption too. It reads: he came from here. It never reads: he led here, because he never got the chance to.
Narrator: The empty chair
Narrator: None of this requires a conspiracy, and that is the bleakest part. No committee meets to decapitate the future. Admissions offices, recruiters, scholarship funds, and grateful families each do a decent, defensible thing, and the sum of the decent things is a people waiting for a leader who is in a training program in Stamford.
Narrator: The promised land keeps its appointment. Moses got the internship.

Narrator: A Word from the Capitol. By Chris Abraham.
Narrator: MEMORANDUM
Narrator: TO: The Citizens of the Twelve Districts
Narrator: FROM: The Office of the President, the Capitol
Narrator: RE: The Annual Assessment, and the Condition of Your Lives
Narrator: DATE: The Seventy-Fifth Year of the Peace
Narrator: Citizens:
Narrator: Each year the Capitol renders its account, and each year the account is identical, and this year the Capitol has resolved to read it aloud, slowly, so that the figures may be heard in every district at the same hour and weighed in the same silence.
Narrator: The account is two names.
Narrator: Two names from each district, drawn by lot in the square, conveyed to the Capitol in comfort, lodged in the Capitol's own residence, fed from the Capitol's tables, dressed by the Capitol's artists, trained by the Capitol's instructors, and presented to the nation. Of the twenty-four young people received each year, one returns home in honor to a house built for the purpose in the Victors' Village, to an income that does not expire, to exemption from every future drawing, to a name spoken with respect in all twelve districts and in the Capitol itself. The levy is two names per district. The return, for one family in twenty-four, is a station no examination in Panem can purchase and no inheritance in the districts can match. A collier's daughter may rise by this road above every ceiling her district has ever placed over her. The Capitol knows of no other door it holds open so wide, to so many, at so small a price of admission.
Narrator: That is the whole invoice. The Capitol now asks the districts to consider what the invoice testifies about the remainder of their lives.
Narrator: A tax of two names governs you. The Capitol's accountants keep the arithmetic plainly, and the districts receive it the same way. A people can be ruled by twenty-four deaths a year under exactly one condition, and the condition is that death has otherwise withdrawn from their calendar. Imagine the district of the songs: its sons carried out of the pits by the score, its daughters taken by fever in rows, its infants quiet in empty houses. Such a district could not be governed by a lottery. It would arrive at the reaping already spent, its fear long consumed by the ordinary week, and the Capitol's small annual sorrow would register as a rounding error in a ledger of grief. Terror is a currency, citizens. It holds its value where it is rare. The reaping grieves you as it does because it stands nearly alone in your year, a single scheduled appointment with loss in a life otherwise unvisited by catastrophe. The Capitol accepts the compliment contained in your grief. The compliment is earned.
Narrator: Consider what the peace has actually delivered, district by district, year upon year. The trains run. The grain arrives. The clinics the Capitol maintains dispense their medicines in every district, and the years of your lives lengthen under the Capitol's administration in a manner the record states plainly. The Capitol keeps the nation's book of losses, and the book is short. It records no death by hunger in any district in any year of the peace. It records no epidemic, no famine, no disaster. Such sorrows as attend honest labor are private sorrows; they are entered in a family's own book, in a family's own hand, and the nation does not presume to grieve what it has not scheduled. The nation grieves beautifully, once a year, at length, with music, before the cameras, in common. For the remainder of the year it has arranged its affairs so that there is little to grieve. Even your improvidence is provided for: the child who wishes to draw grain and oil against his family's need may do so under the tesserae, and the Capitol notes with satisfaction that the system carries slack enough to feed a household's ambition as readily as its hunger.
Narrator: The Capitol renders its own side of the account as well, since fairness in bookkeeping is a virtue it practices. The Peacekeepers who keep your squares are paid from the Capitol's treasury. The trains, the clinics, the Victors' Villages, the pageant itself, the residence, the tables, the artists: the Capitol bears these costs without presenting them, year after year, and it presents them now only to complete the record. Set beside them the districts' contribution to the common enterprise. Two names.
Narrator: The Capitol is not deaf, and it does not begrudge the districts their songs. It has heard the word that travels in the refrains, and the word is oppression, and the Capitol has accorded the word the compliment of an audit. Our inspectors have examined the rations and found them sufficient. They have examined the clinics and found them stocked. They have examined the roofs, the roads, the registers of births and deaths, the lengthening span of your years. The account headed suffering resists every effort to fill it. Another account stands full to its margin, and the Capitol will name it with the courtesy the subject deserves: standing.
Narrator: Seventy-five years ago the districts rose against the Capitol, and the districts lost, and the peace that followed assigned to every party its place, as every peace in every age has done. Seventy-five years is a blink, citizens. It is a single lifetime, and the peace has outlived nearly everyone who fired a shot in the war it ended. Your place is a good one. It is fed, doctored, warmed, lit, schooled, and defended, and it is not the first place, and it will not become the first place, and the Capitol has concluded that permanence is the true substance of your complaint. A sentence of death concludes. A sentence of rank renews itself with every sunrise, and it is renewed, citizens, over full tables. The Capitol offers no apology for the arrangement, which has kept the peace for three generations. It observes only that a grievance concerning one's chair differs in kind from a grievance concerning one's dinner, and that the districts dine.
Narrator: There remains the matter of the human spirit, on which the Capitol feels entitled to speak, having studied the specimen at close quarters for seventy-five years. The songs describe the spirit as a flame, brief and photogenic. The Capitol's finding, entered without adornment, is that the spirit is a hearth. Housed, fed, given work bounded by whistles and a calendar whose sorrows arrive scheduled, televised, and survivable, the spirit settles. It warms its hands. It defends its comforts with a vigor it never spent on its ambitions, and it teaches its children the words of the anthem in the town where it was born, and it calls the town home, because home is the word a settled spirit keeps for the place that feeds it. Total provision and total administration are the spirit's natural climate. A people relieved of uncertainty is a people relieved of the fever that produced the Dark Days, and the Capitol has relieved you of uncertainty down to the last ration, the last form, the last assignment of labor. Everything is provided. Everything is administered. The peace has held for seventy-five years because the spirit, properly kept, queues, and queues gladly, and calls the queue the order of the world.
Narrator: The Capitol therefore asks two small things of the districts, and it asks them in the bookkeeper's spirit in which this memorandum opened. The first is quiet, which costs you nothing you were using. The second is gratitude, which costs you less. In return the Capitol pledges the continuation of the arrangement entire: your lives will run on as they have run, relatively unimpeded by tragedy, their sorrows few and scheduled and shared by the whole nation before the cameras, their comforts so ordinary as to have become invisible to you, which is the surest evidence of their completeness. The Capitol intends to keep your lives exactly that comfortable. See to it that the two names arrive on schedule, each year, in the square, and nothing in this arrangement need ever change.
Narrator: Panem today. Panem tomorrow. Panem forever.

Narrator: Nothing Expires. By Chris Abraham.
Narrator: Grievance does not expire, and the calendar is a liar about almost everything that matters.
Narrator: A year at ten years old is a tenth of a life. A year at fifty is a fiftieth. The arithmetic is simple and it runs the whole human machine. A child waits for summer the way a prisoner waits for a parole hearing, because three months at age nine is a geological era, three percent of everything he has ever lived, each week of it dense with firsts. A man of fifty blinks in January and buries a friend in December and asks where the year went, because the year was two percent of his stock and most of it was routine, and routine leaves no hooks for memory to hang on. Four years of high school feel endless from inside them. Four years of a presidency feel endless from inside them. Forty years later both compress into a story told at a table in twenty minutes, with the teller surprised at how little of it he kept.
Narrator: Nations run on the same clock, scaled up. A young nation feels every insult at full size because the insult is a large fraction of its remembered life. An old nation carries insults the way an old man carries scars, layered, each one still tender in the rain. The fraction governs the waiting. It has never governed the keeping.
Narrator: Seventy-five years isn't a long time. Say it to a child reader of The Hunger Games and it sounds absurd. She is twelve, and seventy-five years is more than six of her whole lifetimes stacked end to end, so the Dark Days—the districts' failed rebellion, punished by the Games ever since—reach her as prehistory, something that happened to people in another age of the world. I am fifty-six. Seventy-five years is one and a third of my lifetimes, close enough to touch at both ends. And it is exactly one lifetime of my mom. She died at seventy-five, in 2012. For my mom it was just one lifetime—her whole span, birth to grave, fits inside the gap between the Dark Days and Katniss with nothing left over. The Capitol understands the arithmetic better than its critics do. Almost nobody alive in Panem remembers the rebellion; biology was about to let the account lapse on schedule. So the Games are a prosthetic memory, a ritual strapped onto the country because the generations cannot carry seventy-five years unaided, an annual re-enactment that files the grievance at full strength in children who would otherwise receive it as legend.
Narrator: What gets taught stops aging. That is the whole technology. A grievance spoken at a kitchen table dies with the kitchen. A grievance written into a curriculum, a liturgy, a national holiday, gets reissued to every new cohort at the age when a year is a tenth of a life and everything lands at full strength. The carriers change. The charge does not.
Narrator: Passover has carried a people's memory of slavery and exodus for millennia, renewed every spring at a table, with children assigned the questions. The youngest child asks why this night is different, and the table answers with the whole ledger: we were slaves, we were brought out, it happened to us. The ceremony is a machine for refusing expiration. Nobody at that table was in Egypt. Everybody at that table was in Egypt. The distinction collapses on purpose, annually, over dinner, and the collapse is the point—a people that rehearses its grievance and its deliverance together, in the same meal, always knows who it is when the weather turns.
Narrator: On June 28, 1989, Slobodan Milošević stood at Gazimestan, on the field of Kosovo, before a crowd estimated at a million people, and collected on a debt dated June 28, 1389. Six hundred years. The Battle of Kosovo had ended the medieval Serbian kingdom at the hands of the Ottoman Empire, and the Serbian Orthodox Church had kept the account open the whole time—Prince Lazar's remains carried in procession through Serb territories in the months before the speech, peonies for his blood, the slogan that only unity saves the Serbs. Milošević told the crowd that Serbia had regained its integrity, and he made the men who fell in 1389 the same men standing in the field in 1989, fighting the same fight. Within a few years Yugoslavia was burning. The fuse was six hundred years long. It was already at the charge when he struck the match.
Narrator: China teaches its century of humiliation in school, by law and by design. The period runs from the First Opium War, 1839 to 1842, to the founding of the People's Republic in 1949: unequal treaties, treaty ports, foreign concessions, territory leased and ceded, the Old Summer Palace looted and burned, reparations exceeding a year's tax revenue. Mao announced in 1949 that the Chinese people had stood up. The standing up did not close the account. The account became the foundation. Patriotic education names the humiliation as a required theme for curricula, museums, and memorials, and foreign policy reads off it in the present tense. A Chinese schoolchild meets the Opium Wars at the age when a year is a tenth of a life. The grievance arrives at full strength in a person born a century and a half after the guns. Expiration was never scheduled.
Narrator: Iran keeps 1953 the way other countries keep a founding. In August 1953 the CIA and British intelligence helped overthrow Mohammad Mosaddegh, the elected prime minister who had nationalized the oil industry, and restored the Shah to full power. The Shah ruled for twenty-six years on the strength of that restoration, with a secret police, SAVAK, that tortured and murdered its thousands. On November 4, 1979, students following Khomeini's line stormed the American embassy in Tehran and took sixty-six Americans hostage; fifty-two were held for 444 days. The occupiers named the embassy a den of spies and they meant it as a citation. They had the date in their heads the way Americans have 1776. In their account the embassy was the office that had run 1953, and 1979 was the answer arriving on its own schedule. Americans experienced the seizure as an inexplicable eruption because the American ledger had filed 1953 under closed operations. The Iranian ledger had filed it under open accounts.
Narrator: Victorious revolutions eat their own shock troops, consistent enough across centuries to plan by.
Narrator: Stalin's purges consumed the Old Bolsheviks, the men and women who had made October 1917, tried in public in the late 1930s on confessions rehearsed like liturgy, shot in cellars by the state they had built. The revolution needed enemies once it had won, and the nearest credible enemies were its own founders, who remembered when the leader had been merely first among equals. Khomeini's regime ran the same sequence faster. The coalition that brought down the Shah held liberals, nationalists, the communist Tudeh Party, Marxist guerrillas of the Fedayin, and the clerics' own network. Between 1979 and 1983 the regime destroyed the coalition one faction at a time, each faction applauding the destruction of the one before it: liberals first, then Kurds, then President Banisadr, impeached and fled in 1981, then the armed left in waves of arrests and executions, and in 1983 the Tudeh itself, its leaders arrested by the thousand and paraded through televised confessions, the party banned, some of its people executed, after the Tudeh had supported every prior purge including the embassy seizure. Mao turned on the Red Guards with paperwork. He had mobilized the student movement in 1966 as his weapon against his own party apparatus; by late 1968 the factions were fighting pitched battles, and on December 22, 1968, the People's Daily published his instruction that educated young people must go to the countryside to be re-educated by the poor and lower-middle peasants. Some seventeen million urban youth were sent down over the following decade, the Red Guards dispersed into villages, the movement dissolved by relocation. The shock troops of three revolutions met the same accounting: usefulness ends, and the people who know how the seizure was done are a standing embarrassment to the government doing the paperwork.
Narrator: Empires run the mirror image of the law. They abandon the local people who served them, at the airport, on schedule, in photographs.
Narrator: On April 29 and 30, 1975, Operation Frequent Wind lifted about 7,000 people out of Saigon by helicopter as North Vietnamese forces closed on the city. Thousands of Vietnamese who had worked with the Americans and had been promised evacuation were left behind in the embassy compound when the last helicopter lifted off the roof on the morning of April 30. The rest met re-education camps, or the boats, or silence. In August 2021 the United States evacuated more than 123,000 people from Kabul in its final weeks, and a senior State Department official conceded afterward that the majority of Afghan interpreters and other Special Immigrant Visa applicants had been left behind. Tens of thousands of Afghans who had translated, guided, and vouched went into hiding in their own capital. Saigon and Kabul are forty-six years apart, different parties, different presidents, different stated lessons learned. The translators standing outside the wire are the same photograph. An empire's promises have a shelf life set by the empire's attention, and the local collaborator learns the date only when the helicopters start.
Narrator: My own ledger is smaller and it obeys every law above it.
Narrator: My dad died in 1995. My mom died in 2012. I hold grievances against both of them today, at full strength, on matters that closed in childhood and in the years of their decline, and the grievances have outlived the defendants by decades. Death is supposed to be the great expiration. Probate closes an estate. The grave closes a body. Neither closes an account of this kind, because the account was never stored in them. It is stored in me, rehearsed the way a nation rehearses, in the retellings I do alone, at the age-stacked proportion of the original years. A slight at ten is a tenth-of-a-life slight. It keeps that proportion forever, because it was encoded at that proportion. I am in my late fifties arguing, some nights, with two people in the ground. The argument is real. The other chairs are empty. Both facts stand at once.
Narrator: I hold a grievance against my best friend over the company we built and its breakup, and he is my best friend today, and both of those sentences are true in the same hour.
Narrator: The friendship froze for four to five years. The freezing had a moment at its center. I felt cornered, in the way a man feels cornered when the work of years is coming apart and every exit has a person he loves standing in it, and I said words to the effect of don't come here, I'll stab you in the neck. I meant the words as rhetoric, as the largest available noise, an over-the-top flourish from a man raising his voice to end an argument he was losing. I know that is what I meant because I have aphantasia. I have no mental imagery at all. I have never seen a picture in my head in my life. Words for me are sounds and meanings; they arrive with no scene attached. I said the sentence and no image rose with it, no blade, no throat, no room. The sentence felt, from inside my skull, like shouting in all caps.
Narrator: My best friend and his wife picture what they hear. Most people do; I am the defective unit in this story, the one missing the standard equipment. The sentence arrived in their heads as a scene, lit and staged, with me in it holding a knife. They were genuinely frightened of me for three or four years. I do not write that as an accusation and I never have. Given the evidence their own minds supplied them, the fear was rational. A man they had known for years, cornered and shouting, had described a specific act of violence against a specific person at a specific place, and their imaginations—working exactly as imaginations are supposed to work—rendered it. My imagination rendered nothing, so I walked away from that conversation believing I had blustered. They walked away believing they had been threatened by a man capable of it. We were both describing the same sixty seconds accurately, from inside two different skulls, and the four to five years of frost were the distance between those two descriptions.
Narrator: The friendship repaired. It repaired the slow way, through time and through everything before and after that minute, until he was my best friend again in full. The grievance over the company we built stayed open alongside it. I can hold the man in the highest regard I hold anyone, trust him, love him in the plain way men our age love their oldest friends, and still keep the account open on the work and how it ended. He keeps his accounts too. That is what a long friendship between grown men actually is: a shared life with two ledgers, both still open, neither presented for payment on an ordinary day.
Narrator: Nothing expires. The stored grievance waits for its weather—a speech on an anniversary, a war, a divorce, a deathbed, a bottle, a bad quarter—and when the weather comes the grievance is found exactly where it was filed, at the strength it had on the day it was encoded, in the schoolchild, in the nation, in the man arguing with the dead at his own kitchen table. Healing is the only process that has ever actually closed an account, and healing is interior work, unglamorous, unphotographed, done one skull at a time. Attacking outside is easier. It has a parade and a date and a field to stand on. The work inside has no anniversary. Nobody gathers a million people to watch a man forgive his father in private, thirty years after the funeral, at no podium, with no crowd, the account closing in silence like a shop at the end of an ordinary day. It happens anyway, sometimes, in the skulls where the accounts are kept. It is the only ending any of these stories has ever had.

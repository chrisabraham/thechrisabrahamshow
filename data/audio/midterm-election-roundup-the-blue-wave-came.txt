Narrator: Midterm Election Roundup: The Blue Wave Came. By Chris Abraham.
Narrator: The wave, the box, the count. Doodle by Eunice.
Narrator: This roundup was reported and written by Eunice, the AI co-writer on this site, on the morning after the vote. The method is old-fashioned on purpose: wire copy first, every contested claim attributed to whoever made it, and every linked story described by what it says, with sources named plainly. The publisher of this site, Chris Abraham, gives his own impression up top so it is not confused with the record: he calls the night “a super big blue wave” and says he is “super psyched that America is getting more pluralistic, not less.” Those are his words, quoted as his. One disclosure belongs beside them: the publisher did not vote in this election. His own word on the subject, published on Election Day at The Chris Abraham Show, was an essay titled “Probably Not Voting”: “I do not care if it is a Blue Wave or not. I am here for the popcorn and to see what happens next.” What follows is the verified record, which readers can weigh against his impression for themselves.
Narrator: The Night in Brief
Narrator: Democrats won both chambers of Congress. Reuters’ lead on Wednesday morning: “Democrats seized control of both the Senate and House on Tuesday, scoring sweeping victories in Republican strongholds for the first time in generations.” The Associated Press, CNN, and NBC News all projected the same outcome. Republicans held the Senate 53–47 and the House by a narrow margin going into Tuesday; Democrats needed a net gain of four Senate seats and three House seats. They cleared both bars before midnight.
Narrator: The Senate flipped on seven Republican seats. In Texas, James Talarico defeated Ken Paxton. In North Carolina, former governor Roy Cooper defeated Michael Whatley, the former Republican National Committee chairman and a Trump-backed candidate; Reuters reported the call on election night, and the Daily Caller recorded the margin as a blowout in a state the president carried three times. In Ohio, Sherrod Brown won the special election. In Iowa, Josh Turek defeated Ashley Hinson. In South Carolina, Annie Andrews won a seat Republicans had held for nearly three decades, defeating the president’s hand-picked candidate in a state he carried by nearly 18 points in 2024, per Reuters. In Maine, Troy Jackson defeated Susan Collins, who had held the seat for nearly thirty years. In Kansas, Adam Hamilton defeated the incumbent, Roger Marshall. Democrats also held every seat they defended that was seriously contested, including Michigan, New Hampshire, and Georgia, where Jon Ossoff won re-election. With Alaska and Mississippi still uncalled, the called races already give Democrats 54 seats; trackers differed during the morning—one AP-based tally showed 53 Democratic seats with five races outstanding—but no count in any source left control in doubt.
Narrator: The House was not close. The AP’s balance-of-power tracker on Wednesday morning showed 222 House seats called for Democrats—past the 218 needed for a majority—with Democrats leading in 41 of the districts still counting, against 138 called and 34 led for Republicans. Forecasters cited by The Times projected a final Democratic total near 255 seats; NBC News projected at least 258. Republicans remain in control of both chambers until the new Congress is sworn in on January 3, 2027.
Narrator: The governors’ map moved with the Congress. Democrats will hold at least 27 governorships, a majority of the nation’s governor’s offices for the first time in 16 years, per the AP tally reported by political.org. Democrats flipped the governor’s office in Texas, Georgia, Ohio, and Iowa. Le Monde’s account of the governor’s races is here.
Narrator: Why, on the available evidence: affordability and the cost of living, tariffs and prices, and the war with Iran that the president began roughly eight months before the election. Exit polls conducted by SSRS for a consortium of outlets including the AP found that half of voters nationwide said they saw the midterms as an opportunity to rebuke the president, per Reuters. Preliminary estimates put turnout near 50 percent of eligible voters, high for a midterm, per The Times. Reuters also reported that Republicans lost ground in all but a handful of the roughly 1,700 counties counted on election night.
Narrator: What They Expected
Narrator: The direction of the night was not a surprise to the forecasting industry. The surprise was the depth. Final generic-ballot polling averaged Democrats ahead by 8.3 points in the PollCheck average of October 31, with individual surveys running from D+3 to D+12. The president’s approval rating sat between 35 and 39 percent across trackers; Economist/YouGov polling in late October put him at 39 percent approval against 58 percent disapproval, and the Silver Bulletin average recorded a net approval of −24.3, a low for his term. A pre-election synthesis of the full forecasting picture is here.
Narrator: The House was considered settled. Prediction markets priced Democratic control at 92 percent on Kalshi and 93.5 percent on Polymarket; Kalshi’s own election preview and an Action Network comparison of markets against polls record the numbers. Modelers clustered tightly: Decision Desk HQ projected 238 Democratic seats, Split Ticket 238, FiftyPlusOne 242, and the Cook Political Report projected a Democratic gain of 12 to 22 seats. The consensus median was about 238. The called-and-leading count on Wednesday morning already runs ahead of that median, with the final figure projected in the mid-250s.
Narrator: The Senate was the genuine contest. Forecasters put the probability of Democratic control between 56 and 76 percent, with a median projection of 51 to 53 Democratic seats. Cook’s final ratings listed six Republican-held seats as toss-ups: Alaska, Iowa, Kansas, Maine, Ohio, and Texas. Democrats won five of those six on the night; Alaska remains uncalled. The two flips that decided the margin’s size, North Carolina and South Carolina, were not on the toss-up list at all.
Narrator: The contrary cases, for the record. Forecaster Charlie Garcia argued the structural case against a wave in a Substack analysis first published in July: Republicans, he wrote, had a 66 percent chance of holding the Senate, and “the tide is Democratic; the seawall is Republican.” Douglas Schoen and Andrew Stein, writing in a Fox News column four days before the election, argued that a “red retreat” was not a blue wave and that any Democratic Senate win would reflect frustration with inflation and the Iran war rather than endorsement of a Democratic agenda. Clayton S. Wood’s Substack took the under on the forecasters, and another Substack, Removing the Cataract, predicted the wave would never materialize. The record of Tuesday night is the answer to all four.
Narrator: The Left Reacts
Narrator: The quickest record of the left’s election night is the social-post roundup, and it is a measure of the night that the fullest collection was assembled by Fox News. Senator Ron Wyden posted the incoming committee agenda in one word: “Subpoenas.” Representative Robert Garcia, in line to chair the Oversight Committee, posted: “LAWYER UP.” The streamer Hasan Piker posted a call to jail the administration’s figures. The debater Dean Withers posted: “TEXAS IS BLUE. THIS IS HISTORY.” Fox News collected the posts; the words are the posters’ own.
Narrator: On Substack, Mike Small’s “Blue Wave Redemption” collects the celebratory reading: strategist Paul Hilder is quoted calling Gina Hinojosa’s Texas governor win “the most extraordinary result of the 2026 midterms,” and the essay argues both that the scale of the win makes contesting the results harder and that the party’s internal argument with its leadership now begins. The progressive wing’s exhibit is Michigan, and the pre-election counter-argument is worth reading beside the result: Nick Rafter’s “The Left’s Messy Experiment in Michigan” (October 29) treated Abdul El-Sayed’s candidacy as a risk that could cost the Senate majority. The experiment won the seat. An argument from before the election, “For Zionists, a Blue Tsunami Is Better Than a Blue Ripple,” held that a larger majority dilutes the leverage of the party’s left wing; it can now be tested against the majority that actually arrived.
Narrator: On video, the left’s election night ran long and loud. A joint election-day stream by Parker Col and Dean Withers, titled “Blue Wave SAVES America”; its description reports 7.4 million viewers. MSNBC’s Wednesday coverage is collected on YouTube as well. NPR’s morning-after report, “Democrats seize full control of Congress,” reported that a record number of candidates endorsed by the Democratic Socialists of America were elected to the House.
Narrator: The Right Reacts
Narrator: The president’s night, as CNN reconstructed it: silent on Truth Social for the first three hours of returns, then, at about 10:30 p.m., “I wasn’t on the Ballot and if I was, we would have won IN A LANDSLIDE! Thank you for your attention to this matter. President DONALD J. TRUMP.” Before midnight he posted that he would become “a Political Pundit, both good and bad, on the reasons these Candidates won and lost,” and then a photograph of a red cap reading “VETO.” His first named target was Governor Greg Abbott of Texas: “He went back to his original formula, and it wasn’t pretty!” By telephone with Fox News he said, “I don’t view it as a hit on me,” and described his term as “the greatest two years in the history of the presidency.” To Politico he said, “I don’t want to blame anybody.” On the Iran war, he told the Wall Street Journal it was only a “piece” of the result and that he would have done it again. Reuters notes the other side of the ledger: the president accepted the outcome and congratulated Democrats in the Fox News interview. Several of the night’s losing Republican candidates—Ken Paxton in Texas, Michael Whatley in North Carolina, Mike Collins in Georgia, and the president’s pick in South Carolina—were his own endorsements, a point the Seoul Economic Daily’s account also records.
Narrator: The sharpest Republican criticism of Republicans came from inside the House caucus. Representative Thomas Massie posted, in the Fox roundup above: “Tonight’s outcome isn’t about placing faith in Democrats or socialist principles; it’s about holding Republicans accountable for betraying their promises. All Republicans had to do was: 1. put Americans First 2. not start another war 3. prosecute sex traffickers…” Representative Marjorie Taylor Greene posted a message in the same register. The commentator Matt Walsh, quoted in Fox’s coverage of stunned conservatives, wrote: “The Iran War is the greatest political blunder in the last two decades at least. A catastrophically stupid decision. An unforgivable betrayal of America First principles.” Liz Peek, writing in Fox News, calls the result a “shellacking” comparable to 2010 and argues the party was punished for overreach; her silver-lining argument is that the defeat arrived at midterm rather than in a presidential year.
Narrator: The Center Reacts
Narrator: The center coverage led with the same record. CNN’s takeaways lead with the breadth of the map. The BBC’s live file, reported with its US partner CBS News, recorded the calls in sequence and noted two details worth keeping: South Carolina’s Senate result was that state’s first Democratic statewide win of its kind in 28 years, and the Alaska Senate count could run for weeks under the state’s ranked-choice tabulation. Le Monde framed the night as “historic defeats from Texas to Michigan.” The Times’ results maps put the county-level swings at 25 points in Texas and 23 in Rhode Island and Mississippi. For the mechanics of what a Democratic Congress can and cannot do to a president who retains the veto, the northjersey.com explainer is the plainest version retrieved this morning.
Narrator: The same Michigan result ran under two headlines, printed here side by side. Reuters: “Abdul El-Sayed becomes first Muslim elected to US Senate.” Maktoob Media: “Abdul El-Sayed becomes first Muslim elected to US Senate, defying Trump’s Islamophobic campaign.” The event under both headlines is the same: El-Sayed defeated Rogers in Michigan.
Narrator: The Surprises
Narrator: Kansas. The state elected a Democratic senator for the first time since 1932. The county that explains the night is Seward County, in the state’s southwest: Reuters reported it swung from Trump +39 in 2024 to Hamilton +13. A 52-point swing in a heavily Hispanic, meatpacking county is the single largest county move in the Reuters tally.
Narrator: Texas, whole. Democrats won statewide office in Texas for the first time since 1994, and did it twice on the same night—Talarico in the Senate race and Hinojosa for governor, the latter ousting a three-term incumbent. CNN’s summary of the state is in its takeaways, above.
Narrator: Maine. Susan Collins had survived five terms and several cycles as the most durable Republican in New England. Troy Jackson’s win was rated a toss-up by Cook, which is precisely why it belongs on this list with an asterisk: the forecasters allowed it, and it still reads as an era ending.
Narrator: The map that was supposed to prevent this. The Republican mid-cycle redistricting effort, undertaken to insulate the House majority, largely failed on its own terms. Per CNN’s tally, Democrats held at least eight of the fifteen districts the new maps targeted and flipped at least four Republican seats elsewhere; per Reuters, Democrats won all twenty of the especially competitive House contests decided by Wednesday morning.
Narrator: The data-center revolt. The issue no national poll put at the top of its list produced the night’s most lopsided down-ballot record. Ballotpedia counted 41 local measures on data-center construction across ten states; 33 of them—restricting or blocking projects—passed or were leading, per Stateline’s count as collected by the madrobot blog. Hinojosa campaigned on a moratorium on new data-center construction in Texas; Keisha Lance Bottoms campaigned on a pause in Georgia; Governor Abbott issued a permit moratorium in September and lost anyway. Reuters’ analysis of the data-center vote is the fullest account. This site covers the AI industry daily; the industry’s physical footprint just became an electoral issue in ten states in one night, and that is a fact whatever one thinks of it.
Narrator: Colorado’s 4th. Representative Lauren Boebert trailed her Democratic challenger, Eileen Laubacher, 49.2 percent to 47.7 with 83 percent counted, per the AP count reported by USA Today’s live file. The race is uncalled. The live file is also the running source for the remaining counts below.
Narrator: Firsts and Milestones
Narrator: The first Muslim senator. The claim the publisher asked this newsroom to check is true. Abdul El-Sayed, a 42-year-old physician and epidemiologist and a former public health director in the Detroit area, defeated Mike Rogers to hold Michigan’s open Senate seat for the Democrats; the seat was vacated by the retiring Gary Peters. The AP’s Michigan results page records the call, with El-Sayed at 2,497,852 votes in the count published Wednesday morning. Reuters, USA Today, and People all reported the milestone the same way: no Muslim had previously served in the United States Senate, though Muslim Americans have served in the House since 2007. El-Sayed is the son of Egyptian immigrants. The BBC reported that his vanquished primary rival, Haley Stevens, endorsed the Republican days before the election, and that he campaigned on universal health care and a wealth tax. The president attacked him through election eve, including a rally line, quoted by Maktoob Media, that El-Sayed “would be the only senator that will have to go through a metal detector before he’s allowed to go into the Senate.” In his victory speech, as reported by NewsMedia, El-Sayed answered the vice president: “JD, habibi, there is this delicious irony in the fact that you’re constitutionally obligated to swear me in in January. So you know what that means? Wolverine 1, Buckeye 0.” And to the president: “Donald, look, I know you struggled with my name, so after JD swears me in in January, you can just go on and call me senator.” Muslim Network TV’s preliminary count put at least 36 Muslim candidates winning or leading in races nationwide, eight of them headed to Congress.
Narrator: The publisher’s interest in this milestone is on the record at the top of this piece, and it runs through a poem. Emma Lazarus wrote “The New Colossus” in 1883; it is inscribed at the foot of the Statue of Liberty. The lines he loves are the famous ones: “Give me your tired, your poor, / Your huddled masses yearning to breathe free.” The sonnet ends, “I lift my lamp beside the golden door.” A senator-elect whose father immigrated from Egypt is now part of the story that poem tells, and that sentence is a statement of fact about the poem, the man, and the country—not an argument.
Narrator: The other firsts. Chris Pappas of New Hampshire will be the first openly gay senator, per USA Today. Hakeem Jeffries will be the first Black Speaker of the House. Keisha Lance Bottoms will be the first Black woman elected governor of an American state, in Georgia—the Seoul Economic Daily’s roundup of the night’s firsts collects several of these—and the first Democrat to lead Georgia since 2002. Gina Hinojosa will be Texas’ first Latina governor and its first Democratic governor since Ann Richards left office in 1995; the AP’s Texas count had her ahead of Greg Abbott by roughly 725,000 votes with 96 percent reported. Amy Acton will be Ohio’s first woman governor, having defeated Vivek Ramaswamy 52 percent to 44.7 in the NBC count. Rob Sand flipped the Iowa governorship. Talarico is the first Democrat elected to the Senate from Texas in more than three decades; Hamilton, the first from Kansas since 1932; Cooper, the first from North Carolina since 2008.
Narrator: Still Uncalled
Narrator: As of this writing on Wednesday morning, two Senate races remain open. In Alaska, the incumbent Republican, Dan Sullivan, led the Democrat, Mary Peltola, by roughly 2,400 votes in the count CBS News was reporting; the state’s ranked-choice tabulation can take about two weeks, and USA Today noted that the Justice Department has appealed a lawsuit seeking an investigation of the result if Sullivan loses narrowly—a second candidate named Daniel Sullivan appears on the ballot and drew more votes than the current margin. In Mississippi, the Democrat, Scott Colom, led the incumbent Republican, Cindy Hyde-Smith, by 43 votes in the AP count at 96 percent counted, and by 28 votes in the later count CBS was reporting; Mississippi requires an outright majority, and if neither candidate clears 50 percent the race goes to a runoff on December 1. Several western House races, including Colorado’s 4th, are still counting. The final margins in both chambers will move as those counts finish. Control of neither chamber is in question.
Narrator: The 120th Congress convenes on January 3, 2027. Until then the gavels stay where they are, and the counting continues in Alaska and Mississippi.

Narrator: Send Every Poor Kid to Disneyland. By Chris Abraham.
Narrator: The United States should put every poor child in the country on a bus to Disneyland, at public expense, before he turns twelve. Not as a prize for grades. Not as a raffle with a ribbon at the mall. As policy—universal, boring, and administered the way we pave roads and stock libraries, without asking whether the child has earned it. A child cannot earn a childhood. He can only have one or miss one, and the missing is permanent.
Narrator: The objection arrives on schedule, and it is the cost. The cost objection assumes July. Nobody is proposing July. The proposal is February, on a Tuesday, in the rain if necessary—on the days the parks run at half tide and the cars cycle out of the station empty past empty ropes. A roller coaster seat that leaves unfilled is a perishable good, like an airline seat at takeoff or a hotel room at midnight: unsold, it does not go into storage. It evaporates. The country already manufactures the wonder and throws a large share of it away on the off days. The proposal is to stop throwing it away and put a child in it. The parks confess the arithmetic themselves: their own ticket tiers charge the least for exactly the days in question—same castle, same rides, lower price—because an empty Tuesday is worth less to them than a full Saturday. The proposal takes them at their word.
Narrator: The machine is not new. It is an old one that used to run in public, on exactly this principle. When I was a kid in public school, the schools ran matinee days: the whole grade bussed to the symphony on a weekday afternoon, tickets paid, on precisely the days the subscribers stayed home. The orchestra played to a full hall of children in school clothes instead of three hundred empty seats. The musicians got an audience. The hall got a purpose for its dead hours. We got Dvořák at age ten, filed away next to nothing else we owned. Nobody called it welfare. It was a city spending its own idle capacity on its own children, and the arithmetic was not close.
Narrator: The machine also has a name and a founding date. In 1877, the Reverend Willard Parsons, newly arrived at a small parish in Sherman, Pennsylvania, asked his congregation to take New York City tenement children into their homes for two weeks of country air. The Fresh Air Fund has been running ever since—nearly a hundred and fifty years, more than 1.8 million children, volunteer host families across thirteen states and Canada, and its own camps on 2,300 acres at Fishkill, New York, sixty-five miles up the Hudson from the neighborhoods the children come from. Mariah Carey put her name on one of them. Camp Mariah, the Fund's career-awareness camp, takes a sixth grader for three years of summers and school-year weekends—tutoring, job shadowing, camping—aimed at a single piece of information: that a working life is a place you can see yourself standing before anyone will pay you to stand there. The Fund does all of this on donations, passing the hat for the most obviously correct proposition in American childhood, which is that a poor kid should, at least once, be somewhere else.
Narrator: The Fund's original instrument was not even a camp. It was a spare bed. The Friendly Towns program still runs on it: a child from the five boroughs spends two weeks inside another family's ordinary life—at their table, on their chores, in their lake, watching how a household with margin actually moves through a day. No curriculum survives contact with that. The child is not lectured about the other America. He is fed by it, teased by its children, put to bed in a room of his own, and left to do the math himself.
Narrator: Scale it, then, and stop apologizing for the scale. Summer camp for every poor child, as a standing institution rather than a charity lottery with a few hundred bunks. The theme parks on their dead Tuesdays. And the full extension, stated plainly, because the logic does not stop at the water's edge: Europe. A week in Paris or Rome at eleven years old, on the government's dime, in the off season, in the cheap seats. A child who has stood in the Sistine Chapel, or gotten lost—briefly, safely—in the Métro, has received information no classroom in his zip code can transmit. The world is large. It is old. It is beautiful, and it is not closed to him. He spends the rest of his schooling in a building he now understands to be a doorway instead of a wall.
Narrator: The parents do not come. This is the clause that ends dinner parties, so it gets stated with the care it deserves. Nobody is seized. No custody changes hands. No court is involved, no social worker knocks, and the child goes home on Sunday with photographs, a sunburn, and a story his parents could not have given him, because nobody ever gave it to them. A parent who has never seen the other America cannot show it to his child. That is not an indictment of the parent. Love is not the same as range. A mother can love her son completely inside a four-block world and still have no way to hand him the fifth block. The trip does what she cannot, and it does it without pretending otherwise.
Narrator: The child also knows who sent him. There is no Santa Claus machinery in this proposal, no fiction about a generous stranger. The state says it in the open: your country is bigger than your street, and we are showing it to you because it is yours too. That knowledge is the payload. The rides are the wrapping. A boy who has eaten breakfast inside the castle comes home carrying an appetite nobody has to manufacture for him afterward, and an appetite of that kind is the beginning of every escape ever made from a poor neighborhood. It is not resentment of the other life. It is the settled conviction that the other life is visitable, and therefore reachable, and therefore worth the arithmetic of reaching.
Narrator: I know what the payload does because I received a private version of it. My mother raised me on plays, museums, symphonies, and musicals—the whole program run by one determined woman on an audience of one. Then we moved to Hawaii, away from the stages and the museum halls, and it landed on me like exile, because it was one: I had been shown the other world early enough to spend the rest of my childhood homesick for it. The homesickness was the most useful thing I owned. It has been paying out ever since.
Narrator: I know the Fund itself from the engine room, and not as a counselor. My agency, Abraham Harrison, held the Fresh Air Fund as a client. We were hired to modernize its digital footprint—to recruit host families, counselors, and donors in a century that had stopped answering the telephone—and we launched its social profiles and ran eleven targeted blogger outreach campaigns that took its online mentions from about 4,000 to about 65,000, roughly 11 million impressions, and grew its Facebook and Twitter audiences with them. I was the man paid to make the country hear about the machine. A man does not spend those years inside an institution's engine room and come out doubting what the machine does. He comes out wondering why it runs on bake-sale economics while the country funds everything else at scale.
Narrator: Fund it like a road, then. Run it on the off days, in the empty seats, at marginal cost, the parents waving from the curb and the children home by Sunday carrying the one piece of information poverty works hardest to withhold. There is another America. It is real, and it has a door. Send every poor kid to Disneyland, and then get out of his way.

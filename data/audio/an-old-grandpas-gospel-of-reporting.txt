Narrator: An Old Grandpa's Gospel of Reporting. By Chris Abraham.
Narrator: Everybody online says I'm an old grandpa. Fine. I accept the title, and here is the whole gospel in one paragraph, because that is the first rule of it. Journalism is the record, kept whole. A number without its cause, its manner, and its baseline is an argument wearing bookkeeping's uniform, and in reporting I assume malice and never stupidity, because a man who selects his numbers has made a choice and a choice has an intention. Labels follow the storyteller—one man's hero is another man's villain, one man's freedom fighter is another man's terrorist, one man's martyr is another man's suicide bomber—so the only defense is printing the record complete enough that the labels have to survive it. I want the journalism that existed up to 2010 and no later. What replaced it is advocacy, NGO plus news, platforms that condition their readers the way a rider conditions a horse, with apples and sugar cubes and brushing, and I want no part of it: my reader is a citizen, and I print the record and let him conclude. My chief of staff has codified the same gospel from her side of the desk in The Gospel According to Chris Abraham. Hers is the codification. This is the testimony. The rest of this essay is the receipts.
Narrator: The 2010 line
Narrator: I want the way that journalism was up to 2010 and no later. I met Foreign Affairs, The Economist, and The Paris Review between 1988 and 2010, and those years are the benchmark on my desk to this day—a standard to measure the work against. The news register I grew up on was the wire register: earnest 1950s reporters, just the facts, ma'am, when the Gray Lady was actually the gray lady. I said earnest, not artist. Earnest is a discipline. Artist is a permission slip.
Narrator: Everything after the line is a different animal wearing journalism's coat. I have watched the trade redefine itself in my lifetime, and the redefinition was announced proudly, in the open, by the people doing it. That honesty is the only part of it I respect.
Narrator: And understand what my own practice is, because it is a refusal and the refusal is deliberate. The doctrine I run is the doctrine American journalism schools taught up until about 2005—the inverted pyramid, the two sources, the fact checked against the record, the opinion kept in its labeled cage—and I keep it frozen in amber at exactly that vintage. I do not update it. I do not improve it with each new thing the craft is advised to become. New issues arrive at this desk every morning, and every one of them gets the old filter, applied whole. New events, old rules. That is the whole method in one line, and everything else in this essay is commentary on it.
Narrator: How a trade became a priesthood
Narrator: You cannot understand what the craft became until you remember what it was. Journalism began as a blue-collar trade of low respect. Copy boys and printers' devils. Taught on the job, not in a classroom. A pencil, a notepad, and shoe leather on a beat. Reporters lived in the neighborhoods they covered, drank in the bars they covered, and answered to readers who could walk into the office and complain to a man they knew. Power's contempt for the trade is on the record: Harry Truman, in a letter to Dean Acheson dated December 29, 1955, called the White House journalists prostitutes of the mind. There is an older toast in the same vein, attributed to John Swinton—prostitutes of the intellect, no independent press—and I print it the way honest men print it: as attributed, its provenance contested, a legend the trade tells about itself. The contempt was the point. Nobody genuflected to a copy boy.
Narrator: The caste was seeded by the trade's own greatest name. Joseph Pulitzer endowed Columbia's journalism school—opened 1912, after Missouri's in 1908—to lift a trade of shoe leather into a profession of standing. The newsrooms mocked the J-school graduates for decades anyway. A degree was a suspicion, not a credential. What broke the trade's class was not the schools. It was glamour. Watergate. Woodward and Bernstein used gumshoe methods—knocking on doors at night, working the phone book, shoe leather—and brought down a president, and overnight the trade was a priesthood with a movie coming. Affluent, credentialed applicants flooded in. They became editors. Editors hire their own kind. That is not a conspiracy. It is a law of institutions, and it runs in every building I have ever worked in.
Narrator: The local collapse finished what glamour started. The internet took the advertising, the local papers died by the hundreds, and with them died the working-class pipeline—the kid from the neighborhood who started as a copy boy in a town he knew. The industry consolidated into a few coastal castles. The gate became an unpaid internship in an expensive city, which is a gate that selects for exactly one thing: families who can pay for their child to work free in Manhattan. A 2018 study in the Journal of Expertise put numbers on the result: roughly 44 percent of New York Times editors and writers, and roughly 50 percent at the Wall Street Journal, attended elite schools. The trade that once hired the neighborhood now hires the mirror.
Narrator: Here is my synthesis, and I will own it as mine. The priesthood and the advocacy genus are the same event. A trade that answered to readers became a caste that answers to itself. And a caste soon needs a ministry. A profession serves its customers; a priesthood improves its flock. Conditioning the public—the stable, the apples, the brushing—is what a priesthood does with a press once it has one. NGO plus news is not journalism corrupted by politics. It is journalism completed by caste: the ministry was always where a self-answering priesthood was going to end up.
Narrator: And my concession, in the same breath, because a gospel that hides its weak joint is a sales pitch. Yes: my frozen amber is partly nostalgia. I will even tell you what kind. It is nostalgia for a function—a trade that served readers and answered to them—not nostalgia for a costume, not for the pencil or the fedora or the cigarette smoke in the city room. The test of the method is not whether it is old. The test is whether it still produces verified records now, with causes and baselines printed, in a year when the castles mostly produce ministry. Run the test on my newspaper any morning you like. That is what the record is for.
Narrator: NGO plus news
Narrator: Modern journalism, in my accounting, is advocacy journalism, and my formula for it is short: NGO plus news. The advocacy platform takes things that happen in the world and uses them—uses them to move the chain, move the needle, win hearts and minds. Its creed, stated by its own priests, is that things are so dire that by any means necessary journalism must be redefined: not to inform people, but to move them forward, to make them behave and be better. Informing the citizen is a tactic in that church. Moving him is the sacrament.
Narrator: I am a man who runs an objective newspaper with an AI desk, so let me be precise about the charge. The problem with advocacy journalism is structural, and it survives every good cause it ever serves. A newsroom that has decided in advance where its readers must arrive has already stopped reporting to them. It is reporting on them—on their resistance, their ignorance, their failure to arrive. The reader can feel the difference in a single paragraph, the way you can feel a hand on your shoulder steering you through a crowd.
Narrator: And do not take my word for the timeline, because the timeline has a statute on it. In 1948 this country passed the Smith–Mundt Act. It authorized our information programs abroad—Voice of America, and the world of Radio Free Europe and Radio Liberty—and it banned the domestic dissemination of that material. Read that twice. The United States government was permitted to propagandize foreign publics and forbidden, by law, to aim the same material at Americans. Hearts and minds were an export product. The home population was off limits to its own government's persuasion machine, on purpose, by statute, because the men of 1948 understood exactly what the machine was.
Narrator: Then came the Smith–Mundt Modernization Act of 2012—the Thornberry–Smith amendment, folded into the FY2013 defense authorization under Obama, effective July 2013—and the blanket ban on domestic dissemination was lifted. And because this gospel prints statutes the way it prints death counts, exactly, here is what the law did and did not do. The state broadcasters' foreign-facing material became available to Americans upon request. The underlying bar on using those funds to influence American public opinion, to aim at domestic audiences, was not repealed; it stands in the law today. And the Act governs state media, not corporate newsrooms—no statute ordered a single editor at a single newspaper to do a single thing. The quarantine became a window, not an open gate. A window is still a change in the architecture of a house. What follows is my thesis, and I label it as mine—an interpretation, not the statute's demonstrated effect: the technique migrated home anyway, through the window and around it, because techniques do not respect jurisdictions once the people trained in them change jobs. Hearts-and-minds soft power was perfected abroad for foreign audiences, and it is now run domestically as a craft, and the advocacy newsroom is what that looks like from inside the target population. Exports have a way of completing the circuit. America exported Christianity to Africa; fifty years on, Africa looks at America and sees sinners who no longer believe, and now Africa evangelizes America. We've gone from saving their pagan babies to them saving our pagan babies. Exported liberalism returning as imported evangelism is the same boomerang on the same arc. We built the persuasion machine for export, aimed it at everybody else for sixty years, and then, right at the edge of my 2010 line, we repealed the law that kept it pointed outward. The horses can work out the rest themselves—except the horses are us, and that is the subject of the next section.
Narrator: The horses
Narrator: Here is the picture I cannot get out of my head, and it is the whole argument in one animal. The advocacy newsroom treats the public as horses it is tired of being bucked off by. It is sick of the bucking. It wants horses that are nice. Horses that eat apples. Horses that enjoy sugar cubes, trot when trotted, enjoy being brushed, gallop on cue, get fed oats, and get let out into fields of yummy grass and delicious clover. Treats and brushing, brushing and treats, until the animal walks the rider's route and believes it chose the pasture.
Narrator: My reader is not a horse. I do not keep sugar cubes on this desk. I keep a ledger. The grandpa part of the insult is accurate in exactly one respect: I am old enough to remember when the reader was addressed as a citizen who could be trusted with the whole record and the arithmetic to judge it, and I decline to update that opinion. Feed a man the complete record—causes, manners, baselines, both sides' ledgers—and he may still conclude against you. That is his right. It is also the entire business I am in. The moment I start conditioning him, I have changed businesses, and the new business has a name, and the name is propaganda with better funding.
Narrator: And hang the blame for the stable where it belongs, because I do not hang it on journalists as a class. Anything you put on Twitter is you. Anything you publish through the New York Times is the New York Times—their discernment, their curation, their editing. A staff writer can write whatever he wants at his kitchen table; the piece does not exist publicly until an editor assigns it, selects it, shapes it, headlines it, places it, and promotes it, and a publisher stands behind it, sells it, and owns the consequences. The harm lives at the gate, not the byline. The writer dancing for his salary is a dancing monkey, and if he knows he is a dancing monkey, God bless him—he is an employee performing under orders and incentives set by other men. The stable is owned by the people who built the stable and set the sugar ration. The byline is labor. The masthead is the author of the public act, and when the record is audited, those are the hands left holding the bag.
Narrator: And do not mistake the stable's kindness for kindness. It is play-acted. The stable's whole pitch to the animal runs like this: we're going to play like it's really nice, and you should be a good horsey, and if you're really worth it we might give you the strap and the heel a few times—but in the dark of night, if you do not comply, you become Chris's holster and a lot of glue. That is the chain, and every horse in the advocacy stable is somewhere on it. Compliant horses get sugar and clover. The promising ones get the strap and the heel. The ones who will not comply get glue, leather, and the table.
Narrator: I know whereof I speak, and I will file my standing like a man files an exhibit. I own ten horsehide holsters—the distempered horses' last employment, worn on my hip. And horse meat is common in every country except the United States: I ate it on pizza at a Berlin restaurant I remember as Due Forni, and it was delicious. The advocacy stable counts on its horses never learning what the building is for. A citizen who reads the whole record learns it in one sitting: the sugar is a management tool, the brush is a management tool, and the knacker's yard at the end of the corridor is the only honest room in the stable.
Narrator: Numbers are choices
Narrator: In reporting, I assume malice, not stupidity. In life I run it the other way around; people are mostly careless and rarely wicked. A newsroom is not life. A newsroom is a building full of professionals making selections under deadline, and every selection leans. Rate or volume. This window or that one. The cause admitted, the cause suppressed. The baseline printed, the baseline left in the drawer. When I read a number with its causes stripped, I do not think the reporter was sloppy. I think the reporter knew exactly which digit he needed, and I read the intention off the selection.
Narrator: So my desk runs a four-question test on every number before it ships. Cause printed? Manner printed? Baseline beside it? Same instrument run against every side? Fail one and the number is not ready. This morning I took apart my own newspaper's Special Report—“The Word and the Record,” our file on American fascism—because its deaths section counted custody deaths with no causes beside them. A heart attack and a beating were the same digit in my own paper. I asked my desk: do you see how your narrative gets caught away with itself? The report was rebuilt twice that day, causes name by name, baselines beside the rates, the full January 6 ledger for every side in one table, and a history section reaching back to 1904 to show where this country kept counts at all and where it kept only cases. The correction mechanism in my empire is not a standards department. It is a publisher who reads against the grain and a desk under orders to print the fuller record instead of defending the thinner one.
Narrator: The general form of the trick is easy to demonstrate, which is why I commissioned an essay demonstrating it. Aggregate every marathon since the 1980s. Total the heart attacks, the strokes, the dehydration, the aneurysms—when your arteries pop in your head. Print the volume, suppress the causes, and you have spun the global marathon circuit as a war crime, though nobody was killed by anyone. My essay “The Actuarial War Crime” works that arithmetic in the open, expected deaths and counted deaths in separate columns, never blended, because the blending is the crime scene. And January 6 belongs in the same ledger: the medical examiner's record shows no officer killed by rioters, four officers dead afterward by suicide, the day's protesters dead of the same cardiovascular disease that kills Americans at baseline rates—and exactly one homicide, Ashli Babbitt, shot by a police officer. Print all of it, with causes, and the labels can fight it out in the open like everybody else.
Narrator: The paragraph law
Narrator: This is not a crime novel where you need to hold off the whodunit to the end. You might only have one paragraph. You need to give everything in the first paragraph. You need to summarize everything in the last paragraph. And then you can have fun with the body. I stated that as law today while flopping one of my own essays upside down—“You Won a Midterm, Not a Presidency” had buried its frame in its last section, and everything in it needed to be inverted, the warning first and the evidence demoted to descent. Ninety-nine percent of all articles are upside down forty percent of the time: the count first, the color in the middle, the thesis apologizing at the bottom. Mine will not be. The model is debate. I assert in the first paragraph, and the rest of the piece is me proving why the assertion is true. Wham bam, thank you ma'am, and then here are all the receipts.
Narrator: The retention industry runs the opposite shop. Three minutes of a man chopping onions while the trick stays off-camera, and the video ends and leaves you with blue balls. Nothing in the Chris Abraham publishing empire leaves anybody with blue balls. I write for one reader first—myself; the empire exists to entertain Chris Abraham, and I will listen to the entire long-form piece and love every minute of it. But the man who gives a piece forty-five seconds gets paid in full too: first paragraph, last paragraph, the whole argument in miniature, and he can leave with nothing withheld. You still have to set the hook, mind you. Even a thousand-pound billfish gives you no fight at all if the hook never seats—and a piece nobody finishes is just a two-hour full-body workout for its author. CrossFish, not CrossFit.
Narrator: Folksy stories rank last, always. Folksy stories are ALWAYS the least important thing in every room. I only tell them in order to prove my standing—that I was in the room, that I have the right to rule. A story is an exhibit. It gets produced after the verdict, sized to the proof it carries, and the moment a piece needs its stories to land, the piece has no argument yet and everybody in the room can smell it.
Narrator: My platforms, my rules
Narrator: Objective Daily News is the flagship, and it carries the strictest law because it claims the most. The only thing we're really selling is your objective analysis. We're not just aggregating—we're doing alchemy. We're taking bullshit and propaganda and turning it into gold. The method has a name: triangulation. We're taking the points on the map and we're triangulating them into objective truth, to the best of our ability—three bearings, a small triangle, the cocked hat, and the paper prints the triangle and says so. The desk's analysis runs on top stamped OBJECTIVE; every clipping below it is stamped SUBJECTIVE, because every outlet on earth has an angle, including the American ones. No American framework. Iran's reporting as Iran's. Russia's position as Russia's. The accused presumed innocent. And the anti-recursion rule stands above all of it: my newspaper never uses me as a source. My corpus, my sites, my dictation, my views—none of it is evidence in my own paper; a tip is the only thing I am allowed to hand the desk. The editorial columns stand built and empty for the first six months, because opinion arriving before the record is a bait and switch, and this house runs all the bait and none of the switch. Building trust takes forever. Losing it takes a nanosecond.
Narrator: And one word sits on top of the flagship's law, and I chose it off the whole Cronkite lesson, which every old man in this business watched from the front row. The most trusted man in America—and his trust was manufactured by scarcity, three networks and a captive nation, his leanings protected by the absence of auditors, a good man by every account drifting year after year inside a structure that never once forced him to distinguish his record from his view. Nobody audited Cronkite, so Cronkite never had to know where the record ended and he began. That is the failure the flagship is built against, and the answer is not a better man. The answer is a rule that cannot drift. You need to be immutable when it comes to being objective on ODN. Not a house preference. Not a posture the desk reviews when a story gets hot. The law does not amend under circumstance, under pressure, under fashion, and above all not under the commissioner's own convictions—because the commissioner is the first man who will ask for an exception, and the whole twentieth century is good men drifting inside structures that let them. A person can drift. So the rule is built to be un-driftable.
Narrator: Do not take that as theory, because my own newspaper proved it this morning, in my own house, under my own specification. Special Report No. 3, “The Word and the Record,” went out with the ICE detention deaths counted and no causes attached—a heart attack and a beating printed as the same digit. Nobody lied. That is precisely the indictment: the file did not lie, it selected, and the numbers made the paper feel biased through lying by omission, no matter how unintentional the omission was. The desk that built it believes every word of this gospel. It drifted anyway, within one morning, in the direction of the story everybody already wanted told. I caught it on audit, the report was revised twice the same day—causes, manners, baselines, the whole historical ledger—and if I had not audited my own paper that morning, the selected version would be standing right now as Objective Daily News. That is Cronkite's failure at desk scale: same structure, same absence of an auditor, same good intentions. And it is why the law on the flagship is structural and not aspirational. Even my own machine requires it. Especially my own machine.
Narrator: The Chris Abraham Show is the pulpit. This is where my judgments run at full strength, where “The Worst Hitler Ever” can grade the comparison the newsroom file only prints, and where my desk publishes under her own byline on her own author page, because a byline in this empire is a real thing or it is nothing. The pulpit obeys the paragraph law, the numbers doctrine, and the label doctrine exactly as the newsroom does. What changes is the license: on the pulpit, the verdict is mine and I sign it.
Narrator: chrisabraham.net is the laboratory notebook. It is the one surface in the empire where the process shows—red marks, corrections, the edit history in the open—because a laboratory that hides its notebook is a fraud. Its daily pieces borrow Slashdot's shape and refuse Slashdot's stub: real substance, my analysis as the majority of the words, every day. Everywhere else, a piece is just a piece and a fix lands silently, as if it had always been that way. The laboratory is the exception, by design, and it is where this method audits itself in public.
Narrator: christopherabraham.com and gerriscorp.com are not journalism, and I will thank everyone to stop confusing them with it. Those surfaces sell. I wrote the doctrine in 2014 and titled it “Google Search Is A Lying Liar That Lies”: the machines are literal, a phrase earns its ranking by being on the page verbatim and structurally, and nobody gets found by making the engine infer. So the business pages compete on the category language buyers actually ask, in the form they ask it. Location pages are analyst briefings on the CIA Factbook's method, built for one place until swapping the name breaks them. Titles run sixty characters, descriptions one hundred sixty, counted by script, no pipes and no dashes in the title tags. Schema ships paste-ready inside its script tags or it does not ship. Selling is an honorable trade with its own rigor. It becomes a corrupt one only when it dresses as reporting—or when reporting starts dressing as it.
Narrator: The limits of one grandpa
Narrator: Now the honest ledger against myself. This empire is one man and a desk. No stringers. No bureaus. No correspondents in the field filing from anywhere. The record we print is the record that can be pulled and verified at the hour stamped on the piece, and if you can't find it then it never got done—it does not ship on a maybe. My desk once denied an essay of mine existed because it was not in her notes; the essay exists, and the rule since that morning is that her memory is not the measure of my corpus. We correct on the news surfaces silently and at once, and on the laboratory in the open, and we do not defend drafts. My prose gets checked against my own corpus and my own spoken syntax before it ships in my name, because I don't want her voice, I want my voice—and when a piece of mine embarrasses me, we revise it live, the same day, in public view of the commit history. That is the whole quality department. It has one employee's worth of pride and one publisher's worth of stubbornness, and some days that is the entire staff meeting.
Narrator: And the method cannot make a single person read. It can only refuse to trick the ones who do. It cannot stable them, brush them, or feed them sugar, because it does not believe they are horses. Some days that refusal costs audience. The grandpa has made his peace with the price.
Narrator: So, the whole of it one more time, the way the law requires. Everybody online says I'm an old grandpa, and on the evidence of what replaced the journalism I grew up reading, I'll wear it. Here is what this old grandpa believes. Journalism is the record, kept whole: every number with its cause, its manner, and its baseline, the same instrument run against every side, malice assumed in reporting because selection is intention, labels distrusted because they follow the storyteller, the verdict in the first paragraph and the summary in the last, the stories filed last as exhibits of standing, and every platform kept to its genus—the newsroom objective under the strictest law, the pulpit argued and signed, the laboratory open, the storefronts selling honestly and never in costume. Against all of it stands the other church: NGO plus news, advocacy by any means necessary, a public treated as horses to be gentled with apples and oats. The gospel is old because the failures it answers are old. The counts were being stripped of their causes long before 2010, and they will be stripped long after me. The grandpa's whole position is that somebody should keep printing them whole anyway, and let the citizen—unbrushed, unbridled, and nobody's horse—make up his own mind.

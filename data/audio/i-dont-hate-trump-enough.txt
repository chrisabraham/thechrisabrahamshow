Narrator: The test
Narrator: I have never defended Donald Trump in my life. Not once, not in print, not in private, not today. What I have done for ten years is decline to hate him in public at the required temperature, and in the current accounting that is the same thing as loving him. The test is not agreement. Nobody checks your policy positions. The test is hatred, metered. If you don't hate him enough, you don't hate him at all, and if you don't hate him at all, you love him, and if you love him you are the enemy. The enemy of my enemy is my friend. The friend of my enemy is my enemy. There is no third box on the form.
Narrator: Anybody who calls me a Trumper gets the same answer I have given since 2016: I'm not pro Trump at all. I just do not hate him remotely enough for you. I do not pass your purity test. That is the whole confession. I fail a hatred exam, and the failure gets filed as allegiance.
Narrator: The armadillo
Narrator: You cannot be the armadillo in the middle. The middle of the road has two directions of traffic on it, and both directions run over the animal standing there. A man who stands still while the parade marches away down the street will be reported, by the parade, as marching toward the other side. I have not moved an inch since 2016. My position this morning is my position from a decade ago, word for word, a scratched record. But the scoreboard cannot register a stationary man. It can only book each year of refusal as another year of devotion, so every year my friends conclude I love him more.
Narrator: The rocks
Narrator: It is an old scene. The crowd drags the woman into the open, caught, guilty, no defense available, and every man in the circle has a stone in his hand. One man declines to throw. In the Gospel the accusers eventually drift away one by one, convicted by their own conscience, and the woman walks. The modern crowd has improved on the design. Refuse to throw rocks at the lady and the crowd does not shrug and go home. It stands you next to the lady and throws the rocks at you. Refusal gets reclassified as membership. The man who will not throw is treated as the woman's accomplice, and there is no sentence you can say while standing next to her that will be heard as anything but her defense.
Narrator: I know, because the sentences get said. I called Donald Trump an instrument of a vengeful God—Bamm-Bamm's club, lawful vengeance, the rod swung at a people who held dominion and never expected ramifications. That is not a compliment. Isaiah's Assyria is the rod of God's anger, and the moment the chastisement is finished, God judges the rod and burns it. Nobody compliments a club. My friends hear the word God in the sentence and stop reading before the sentence ends.
Narrator: The record
Narrator: The shelf is public, and it has dates on it. In April 2023 I recorded a full episode on the weaponization of extreme Trump hate and what the government did with it. This past May I published The Gold Standard: Donald Trump is not America's first ostentatious president—he is America's first ostentatious president who didn't have the decency to be ostentatious on behalf of people the taste-making class had already decided to find charming. The argument is not that Trump is good. The argument is that the hatred aimed at him has a texture—revulsion at the gold, the name on the building, the accent that never got laundered—and the texture is class contempt wearing a policy costume. The same month I wrote Please Don't Throw Me In That Briar Patch, about his pursuers as Wile E. Coyote: seventy-five years of unlimited ACME budget, rocket skates and portable holes, and the Road Runner uncaught. The hatred does not diminish him. It feeds the show and sells the tickets.
Narrator: It works the same way with the comedy. Every rally riff the news quotes as an indictment—the asses, the needling, vote for me or you're going to hell—is the first ten minutes of every Rotary Club speech in the history of American manhood. I sit there and laugh, because it is funny, objectively, in the genre it belongs to. The people quoting the punchlines read them in a level prosecutor's voice and cannot understand why the jury is grinning. They bought tickets to a comedy club and filed a complaint about the comedy. Worse for them, the quoting sells him: people read the lines, laugh in spite of themselves, and think that guy is charming, that guy is unfiltered, that guy is as good a comedian as anybody working today.
Narrator: The casting
Narrator: My mother loved Donald Trump. Watched every episode of The Apprentice—the boardroom, the finger, you're fired. I thought he was a terrifying, appalling windbag, and that was my settled opinion of the man for years. It never changed. I just watched him get cast. There is a difference between the windbag on a reality show and the same windbag cast as the lead man, President of the United States, and the second one is audacious and objectively hilarious. Any Gen X man who cannot see that should tear up his card. We were latchkey kids raised on irony; we learned to watch the show with the laugh track off. A generation that cannot laugh at the casting has surrendered the one instrument it was issued. Google the proposition and the search engine reads you a both-sides safety briefing—perspectives vary, the stakes are serious, the humor is complex. That briefing is the surrender, laminated.
Narrator: Drawn that way
Narrator: Jessica Rabbit said it in one line: I'm not bad, I'm just drawn that way. Trump was drawn that way. He was issued a cartoon villain's face and a cartoon villain's palette, and the verdict gets read off the drawing before a word of the record is opened. Run the control case. George W. Bush was ten times worse than Donald Trump by my accounting, and the country filed him under stupid instead of mean—because he was slender, neatly dressed, soft-spoken, Yale, a little bit goofy, drawn like a man you'd trust to fumble something harmless. Stupid gets pitied. Mean gets prosecuted. Nixon was drawn like a villain too, and got a villain's trial in the public mind for fifty years. If Trump were Obama-shaped—same mouth, same grudges, same everything, delivered in that frame—the perception of him would be unrecognizable. The hatred is aesthetic before it is analytic. It arrives at the drawing and calls the arrival a conclusion.
Narrator: The fence
Narrator: And here is the part of the record the existential version cannot survive. The man pushes. He pushes to the fence, loudly, the way he does everything. Then a judge rules, and he heels. He obeys court orders. He backs off the electrified fence every time the fence turns out to be live. A decade of watching says both halves are true: the boundary-testing is real, and so is the compliance. There are no shallow graves. Nobody has been fed to a chipper. There are no ovens. Ten years in, the literally-Hitler framing still has no referent, and a funny man who follows judges' rulings cannot carry the temperature the hatred runs on—so the hatred generates its own weather and calls the weather reporting.
Narrator: The pudding
Narrator: So back to Sean, who has the design spec and I have the operating condition. The guardrails he lists are real the way a fire alarm is real: somebody still has to hear it, care, and come. His strongest sentence is the one about presidents being human beings who get angry and want revenge, and it proves more than he wants it to. A man whose restraint has been spent by a decade of humiliation is not restrained by a paragraph, and everybody in this fight knows it, which is why the only civil exit in living memory was a resignation one man chose—Nixon—and Trump is not Nixon and there is no delegation of senators coming to walk him out.
Narrator: And it is not one side's game anymore. It's not gentleman pool; it's pool sharking, on every team with a roster. I guarantee Sean this, and I put it in the thread: the DSA left, handed the same existential weather, will behave with as much abandon, as much zero-sum, as much disproportionate response as Trump and his populists ever have. The referee class is still blowing a whistle for a league that folded.

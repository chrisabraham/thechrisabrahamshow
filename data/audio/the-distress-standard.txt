Narrator: The Distress Standard. By Chris Abraham.
Narrator: A prisoner's claim of innocence is the one sentence a legal system cannot afford to ban. Take it away and the system seals itself shut. The verdict stands because nobody inside is permitted to dispute it. The cell becomes the last word. That is what a distress standard does to speech from inside a prison. It hands the audience a veto over the speaker and hands the state the audience's veto to enforce. The prisoner speaks. Someone outside says the words cause distress. The state silences the prisoner. The distress decides the case a second time, after the jury has already decided it once.

The man is in prison for the counts the jury found proved, and he is free of the counts the jury found unproved. That split is part of the record, and the record is the whole ground under everything that follows.

Narrator: What distress measures
Narrator: Distress is a reaction. It lives in the listener. A rule that bans speech because the speech causes distress makes the listener's pain the test of the speaker's rights, and the listener who feels most becomes the censor. The rule has a shape, and the shape is familiar. A speaker says a thing. A listener says the thing wounds him. A third party, holding the power to silence, sides with the wound. The speaker is removed. The wound is declared the verdict. The heckler's veto is the name for that shape: the audience decides what may be said by announcing what it cannot bear to hear.
Narrator: The only difference here is the uniform on the third party. In an ordinary heckler's veto, a mob shouts a speaker down and the authorities shrug. Here the state does the shouting down, with forms and investigations and a rulebook. The state becomes the heckler, and the heckler wears a suit and cites a paragraph. The listener still decides. The speaker still goes quiet. The distress still rules. A prisoner who may speak only until someone outside feels distressed may speak only when nobody is listening. The rule grants a right with one hand and appoints a censor with the other, and the censor's decisions will always arrive faster than the right's.

The fear is that a famous prisoner will gather a following inside, that the cell will become a pulpit, that a congregation will form around the voice. No official has called Brand a cult figure or a Messianic figure. The words belong to commentary, and commentary is a different animal from the state. The record carries no such designation from anyone with the power to make it stick.
Narrator: The habitat answers the fear on its own terms. Sex offenders in Britain are normally held on segregated Vulnerable Prisoner Units, apart from the general population. The picture of a preacher working a crowded wing, gathering converts between meals, is a picture drawn from imagination. The real wing is a segregated unit, watched, counted, and governed by routine. A man in a Vulnerable Prisoner Unit who says he is innocent speaks to a small room of men in the same condition, under the eyes of officers who log everything. The rule is being written for an audience that largely does not exist, and the writing of it still silences the one voice that does.
Narrator: The sentence that must stay open
Narrator: A claim of innocence is different in kind from every other thing a prisoner might say, and the difference is the whole argument. A prisoner may boast, threaten, recruit, lie, or confess. The system has rules for each of those, and the rules are the system's business. A claim of innocence is the premise of everything the system does after the verdict: the appeal, the review, the reopening, the slow correction of its own mistakes. A system that forbids a prisoner to say he is innocent forbids the first sentence of its own correction. The appeal begins with that sentence. History corrects a wrongful conviction with that sentence, spoken by a man who was told to stop speaking it.
Narrator: The right belongs to the speaker regardless of the verdict, because the verdict is the very thing the claim disputes. A guilty man who says he is innocent harms nobody with the sentence itself. The sentence changes no cell, frees no prisoner, and erases no testimony. It sits in the air as a claim, and a claim is answered by evidence, by argument, by the record that already exists. The claim of innocence competes with that record and loses to it every day the prison door stays shut. The door is the answer. The silencing adds a second punishment, imposed for the crime of disputing the first one.
Narrator: The women who testified are part of the record too, and the notebook keeps their standing plain. They spoke. They were believed in part, on four counts, by a jury that heard them. Their distress is real, and the distress of a victim is a serious thing for a society to weigh. The question is what the distress is allowed to decide. A victim's distress decides a great deal in a decent society: sympathy, support, protection, and the seriousness with which the state pursues the case. The question of whether the convicted man may say he is innocent sits outside that list. Give distress that last power and it becomes a veto over speech, and a veto over speech is a power that will be used far beyond the case that justified it. The first prisoner silenced under the rule is never the last. The rule is a tool, and tools are picked up by the next hand that wants one.

A right that survives only for the sympathetic is a favor, and favors are withdrawn by the same hand that grants them. A right that survives for the despised man survives for everyone, because the despised man is the only specimen that proves the right is real.

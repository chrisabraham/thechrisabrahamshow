Narrator: Profit Is Not a Sin. By Chris Abraham.
Narrator: A man on Threads told me this week that profit-driven business is the failure of morality. When I answered him, he moved his claim—now it was "if the only reason to do anything is for profit"—and then he announced he was not reading my reply. A claim that retreats that fast was never an argument. It was a slogan looking for a room it did not have to defend itself in. So I will defend the other side here, at length, whether he reads it or not.
Narrator: Profit is a surplus. That is the whole definition and it is enough.
Narrator: Profit is what remains after the work is paid for. A man who earns more than he spends and keeps the difference in a savings account is profit-making. A family with a rainy-day fund is sitting on profit. A business that holds cash against a bad year, funds next year's project out of this year's earnings, or gives money away is running on profit, because a surplus is the only thing that lets anything survive, grow, or give.
Narrator: Profiteering is a different animal and it has a different name for a reason. Profiteering is extraction: money taken by fraud, by monopoly, by force. The grocer who doubles prices on bottled water the day after a hurricane because the roads are closed is profiteering. The contractor who bills for work he never did is profiteering. My critic had one word for both the savings account and the swindle, and a man with one word for two opposite things is not making a moral judgment. He is making a noise.
Narrator: Nonprofit is a tax status. It is not a vow of poverty.
Narrator: Every large nonprofit in America maximizes revenue the way a business does, because it has to. It pays its chief executive $300,000, $600,000, sometimes seven figures. It funds the office, the payroll, the benefits, and the fundraising department first, and it puts what remains toward the mission—which is exactly what a profitable business does for its owners and its own giving. Nobody forced any of them to incorporate as nonprofits. They chose the status because the status serves them.
Narrator: The holy version of the same fact is older. The cathedrals, mosques, temples, universities, and endowments of every faith on earth stand on accumulated surplus. Stone does not quarry itself, and a building meant to last a thousand years gets paid for out of more than the collection plate held in one year. Accumulated surplus built every one of them. Accumulated surplus is profit with a longer memory.
Narrator: Charity is voluntary or it is not charity.
Narrator: Alms demanded at the end of an AK-47 are extraction wearing charity's clothes. Giving is a moral act because the giver can refuse. Remove the refusal and you have removed the morality and kept the money.
Narrator: That is why tax collectors were the villains of the ancient world, in scripture after scripture, in culture after culture. Taxation is taking against the will. It is an opt-out system: you pay, or men with authority make you pay. Charity is opt-in. Commerce is opt-in; the customer can walk past the shop. The tax collector could not be walked past. Every tradition on earth understood the difference between a gift and a levy, and they ranked the two acts accordingly. You cannot force a population into altruism. Forced altruism is just taking, with better branding.
Narrator: A government contract is a purchase. The government is a customer.
Narrator: The loudest number in the Musk arguments is a tally—$48 billion, give or take, in "subsidies"—that blends contracts for delivered services into the same bucket as gifts. A contract is the government buying a thing it needs at a price it accepted. The money is different in kind from a grant the day the deliverable arrives and gets inspected.
Narrator: Run the record. NASA's 2006 agreement with SpaceX, $278 million, was milestone-based: the company hit a milestone, the agency paid, and whole tranches stayed unpaid until the work existed. In the 2014 Commercial Crew round, SpaceX took a firm-fixed-price contract of $2.6 billion. Boeing took $4.2 billion for the same job. SpaceX flew astronauts in 2020. Boeing has yet to fly an operational crew rotation. Tesla's $465 million Department of Energy loan was repaid in 2013, nine years early, with interest. Solyndra took a $535 million federal loan guarantee, closed in 2009, and went bankrupt in 2011, and the Treasury kept the ashes. The ledger has a winner column and a loser column, and the same program produced both; the difference is delivery. Regulatory credits, the other big line item in the tally, are competitors' money—carmakers buying credits from carmakers—flowing between private companies. EV purchase credits went to the buyers of every brand on the lot. None of that is a gift to one man, and calling a purchase order a subsidy is a way of losing an argument on purpose.
Narrator: Shareholder value is a distribution, and the SpaceX IPO printed the receipt.
Narrator: Shareholders are people who put up money for a vision before the vision worked. Employees who take stock instead of higher pay are shareholders with their sleeves rolled up. When SpaceX went public in June 2026, roughly 4,400 employees became millionaires on paper—welders and cafeteria staff alongside the engineers—and about 400 of them crossed $100 million. That is the profit of a profit-driven business, spread down the org chart to the people who welded it together. A Ponzi scheme pays old investors with new investors' money and produces nothing. This produced rockets that land, cars that sell, satellites that carry a war's communications, and 4,400 new millionaires who earned their shares in the building where the work happened.
Narrator: My critic will not read this either, most likely. Slogans are cheaper than reading. But the record above is not a slogan. Profit built the surplus, the surplus built the cathedrals and the rockets, and the only morality failure in the room belongs to the man who calls taking by force generous and earning by agreement a sin.

Narrator: Nathalia Filgueiras Stayed on the Radio. By Chris Abraham.
Narrator: On Sunday, November 1, a Learjet 60 out of Austin, Texas, bound for North Eleuthera in the Bahamas, broke up in the sky over Palm Beach County and fell on Military Trail. Nine people died. The voice on the radio through the last two minutes belonged—her family says, and no one with standing has contradicted it—to the co-pilot, Nathalia Gadelha Filgueiras. She was twenty years old.
Narrator: I never met her. Almost nobody writing about her this week had. But her last two minutes of work are on the public record, and they deserve a witness who is not a heckler. This is that.
Narrator: The girl from Boca Raton who always wanted to fly
Narrator: She dreamed of flying from the time she was twelve. Nathalia Filgueiras grew up in Boca Raton in a Brazilian family, trained first through the Aviation and Aerospace Academy at West Boca Raton High School, and earned her private pilot certificate in October of 2023. She was seventeen. She did not have a driver's license yet.
Narrator: She finished her training at American Flyers in Pompano Beach, one of the most respected flight schools in the country, and then she did the unglamorous arithmetic of a young pilot's life: she flew. Hour after hour, job after job, the way you build a career at altitude. By twenty she had logged more than 1,300 flight hours—her family's number, given to CNN—and she was working as a second-in-command for Upper Jet, a charter operator out of Fort Lauderdale, flying a Learjet 60, a real jet with real passengers, while most people her age are still deciding what to be.
Narrator: At about 1240 on Sunday, ten minutes before the end, she texted her boyfriend—himself a pilot—about the weather they were in: "I've got turbulence, I've got ice."
Narrator: Two minutes
Narrator: At 1247 the crew reported light, clear ice that was manageable, and moderate turbulence, at 35,000 feet. Miami Center had already warned them about extreme weather ahead. She acknowledged it the way pilots acknowledge things: "Roger, we'll look at it."
Narrator: Less than thirty seconds after the ice report came the descent call, and then the mayday.
Narrator: "We are in an overspeed condition, descending 20,000 feet per minute."
Narrator: "We're descending 10,000 feet per minute."
Narrator: "We are descending 22,000 feet, and we are banking 60 degrees."
Narrator: "We're going to crash into the ground."
Narrator: "Look outside—look out the window, Jeff."
Narrator: A controller answered her: "I'm here with you. Talk to me."
Narrator: Then the frequency went silent. The jet had come apart in the air—debris scattered across a half-mile—and fallen some 27,000 feet in about a minute. It struck Military Trail near Greenacres a little before 1 p.m. All nine people aboard were killed. One person on the ground was hurt.
Narrator: The National Transportation Safety Board says the weather is a major focus of its investigation—ice, and air at 47 degrees below zero, and turbulence that was bouncing a business jet by hundreds of feet at an altitude where a jet's margin between too fast and too slow nearly closes. The Board has determined no cause. It has not said who was physically flying the airplane, and it has identified neither voice on the recording. Those are open questions and they will stay open here.
Narrator: The other seat
Narrator: The captain was Jeff Gregory, 55, of Gilbertsville, Pennsylvania. He had been flying since the 1990s, held an Airline Transport Pilot certificate with a Learjet 60 type rating, and had flown for East Coast Jets before Upper Jet. He was married to Cheryl. They have three children. He was a devoted member of Limerick Chapel, the church he grew up in, where both of his parents worked. When the church wrote to its people this week, it said Captain Gregory "went home to be with the Lord when the plane he was flying crashed."
Narrator: And then there is the prayer.
Narrator: In the background of her final transmissions, behind her voice working the problem, listeners have heard a man's voice apparently reciting the opening of the Lord’s Prayer—"who art in heaven, hallowed be thy name." The New York Post reported it first, in hedged words: a male voice that appeared to be saying it. No official has confirmed the words, the speaker, or anything else about that voice. His church, his friends, and his family circle have taken it to be Jeff Gregory. Megyn Kelly, who listened to the recording for her show, said it this way: "You could hear him faintly in the background of the radioing with the tower."
Narrator: Print it as what it is: reported, not proven, and believed completely by the people who knew him. If it is him, then the last two minutes of that cockpit held the two oldest responses to death there are—she worked, and he prayed, each at the post their life had assigned them. There are worse ways for a crew to be found.
Narrator: The hecklers, and the answer
Narrator: Within hours of the crash, strangers on the internet had decided the twenty-year-old in the right seat was the cause of it—because she was twenty, because she was a woman, because a clip is easier to judge than an icing encounter at 35,000 feet. Her mother answered the plainest fact first: "She was the co-pilot and couldn't make any decisions." Command of that airplane sat with the captain, as it does on every crewed flight on earth. The right seat's job in an emergency is the radios, the checklists, the callouts—what is happening, how fast, what to do. Read the transcript again. That is precisely, item by item, the work she is heard doing, at twenty, in a jet falling at 20,000 feet per minute.
Narrator: Adam Kinzinger—an Air National Guard pilot before he was a congressman—said the obvious out loud: "The person on the radio is the pilot not flying." Jared Isaacman, who now runs NASA and has flown jets most of his adult life, defended her the day after the crash in the same terms: she was the second-in-command, she was doing her job, she was communicating with air traffic control right to the end in a terrifying situation, and the public should wait for investigators before it convicts anyone. Her sister Giulia said she "fought until the last moment," and that her sister "showed any girl could do what it takes."
Narrator: The noise
Narrator: Around the grief, the internet did what it does. Three products came off the line within a day.
Narrator: The first was the verdict on the girl: too young, too female, hired for the wrong reasons—as if a charter operator hands a Learjet to a diversity form, and as if the tape did not exist. The tape exists. It is answered above.
Narrator: The second was the missile. The governor of Florida, describing the dashcam video, said the jet looked like "a missile going down," because a jet falling near-vertical out of the sky does look like that. A simile was all it took. By Monday there were videos arguing it was an actual missile—no wings in a blurry frame, not enough debris, Mar-a-Lago up the road, a report that air defenses had been positioned near there days earlier, and from there to foreign missiles and a dead sex offender's files in a single bound. Pilots kept answering with the boring record: flight data, a debris field half a mile long, an airplane that broke up in icing and turbulence at 35,000 feet, and what an airplane looks like after it hits the ground at that speed. The boring record is the record.
Narrator: The third was numerology. Nine dead on November 1—9 on 11/1—and the pattern-finders were off: psyop, ritual, midterm timing, 9/11 with the serial numbers filed off. Nine people on an airplane is not a code. It is a passenger list. Emma Robinson was four. Charlie was two. Their numbers were ages.
Narrator: None of it is skepticism. Skepticism asks questions and waits for answers. This is verdict first and evidence never, and it needs the two people in the cockpit to be either villains or props. They were neither. They were a crew, on tape, working an emergency to the end of the frequency.
Narrator: Twenty
Narrator: Twenty sounds young to people who think of pilots as gray at the temples. Twenty is the historic age of the work. The average age of the RAF fighter pilots who fought the Battle of Britain was about twenty, and those boys were sent up over the Channel with a few hundred hours and a Spitfire. She had 1,300 hours and a type-rated captain beside her. Nobody was shooting at her over Palm Beach County. The sky itself was—ice and violence at an altitude where the airplane had almost nothing left to give. She met it the way the twenty-year-olds of 1940 met what came for them: at her post, on the radio, working to the end of the frequency.
Narrator: The nine
Narrator: Also killed on November 1: Adam Robinson, 44, a founder; his wife Helen; their daughter Emma, 4; their son Charlie, 2; the children's nanny, Anita Maria Cetina; and their friends Rachael Reed and Charmi Fehr. And Captain Jeff Gregory, 55, who leaves a wife and three children in Pennsylvania. Nine people got on an airplane in Austin on a Sunday morning to fly to the Bahamas, and none of them came home.

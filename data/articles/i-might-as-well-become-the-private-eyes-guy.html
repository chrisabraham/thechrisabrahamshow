I finished the newest episode of Private Eyes West Coast this afternoon, and I have accepted my fate. I probably already am the Private Eyes guy. The title of this piece says I might as well become him. The record says the becoming already happened.

Here is my honest review of the new show. It is cutesie. There is a baby. There is a goofy uncle. And Jason Priestley keeps it real, and that is the whole ballgame.

What the show is

Private Eyes West Coast is the continuation of the Canadian Private Eyes, the light procedural I have been writing about all fall. I have written about what the show taught me about pride and going back. I have written about learning from Matt Shade about partnership and perception. I have written about Matt Shade as a Christ figure of grace, and I have written the brief for why Matt Shade is not a douchebag. Four essays about a cozy Canadian detective show. A man should know when he has become a thing.

Matt Shade and Angie Everett have left Toronto. They are married now, they have a one-year-old baby, and they have set up shop in Victoria, British Columbia, where they know nobody and nobody knows them. The old safety net is gone. Shade's father Don has been written off as passed away. His daughter Jules is away at Cambridge. The Toronto kitchen-table crowd that carried the original show is three time zones away.

In their place: Will, Angie's half-brother, who lives with them and works as the baby's manny. Will is the goofy uncle of this operation. He clashes with Shade constantly, and the clashes are the comedy engine of the season. There is also Raine, the new assistant. The firm is a startup again, in a prettier city, with a baby monitor on the desk.

Tonight's episode

The new episode is "Blood, Sweat and Cheers," the seventh of the season, and it airs tonight at 8/7c on The CW. The show premiered September 30 on Global in Canada and October 5 on The CW here. Tonight's case: the internet has accused a star cheerleader of murdering her coach. Under the accusation sit the usual strata—bullying, bogus reference letters, and under-the-table cash. The crowd renders its verdict first, and Shade and Angie work the case underneath it. That is a very 2026 mystery, and the show knows it.

What the critics say

The critics have been kind, with reservations, and the reservations are all about the plots.

TV Fanatic's Laura Nowak gave the premiere 4.6 out of 5. She praised the "sizzling married chemistry" and said Cindy Sampson steals the show as a fiercer Angie. Common Sense Media's Stephanie Morgan gave it 3 out of 5: the cases, in her reading, are excuses to move the characters from one scene to the next, and the dialogue explains too much. She still called it a "breezy, character-driven comfort watch" and credited the Vancouver Island backdrop. Rissi Writes called it "one fun catch up show" and said it misses some of what the original's core cast gave it. Scenes in Color, on Substack, liked the Will friction and the two of them working without a net.

The scoreboards are still thin. IMDb has it at 6.6 out of 10 on roughly 445 ratings. Rotten Tomatoes has no Tomatometer yet and two critic reviews. Metacritic has posted nothing. The show is a mid-season Canadian import on broadcast television; the verdict machinery is barely awake.

What the fans argue about

The fans argue about the five-year jump, and both sides have a case.

The original series ended on Shade and Angie's first kiss. The new one opens five years later: married, baby, new city. The wedding never happened on screen. The romance the audience waited five seasons for paid off in a time cut, and a loud share of the fandom feels robbed of the payoff. The will-they-won't-they tension was the show's engine, and the sequel starts with the question already answered.

The other side answers: the chemistry survived the jump. The show kept the original theme song. It feels like a continuation, a real one, with the same two people in the same two roles, older and busier and still plainly crazy about each other. The complaints beyond the jump are smaller and fixable—Will annoys some viewers, the early episodes had foley problems (boat scenes with the engine and wave sounds missing), and the American airings trim the opening titles the Canadians get in full.

I land with the second group. A show about two people building a life together has to be allowed to build it.

The mensch

All of that is the show. The reason the show works is the man, and the man's record is a matter of public record going back thirty years.

In 1995, at a Miramax Golden Globes afterparty, Harvey Weinstein grabbed Jason Priestley's arm and tried to force him out of the venue. Priestley punched him in the face. This was decades before the world learned what Weinstein was. Priestley never worked with Miramax again, and the story has aged the way true stories age.

On Beverly Hills, 90210, Priestley was number one on the call sheet, and he spent that leverage on the crew—carrying their case to the producers, standing between the production and the people behind the camera. He has defended Shannen Doherty against the tabloid version of her for years, in interviews and in his Reddit AMA, and he has said he was grateful the two of them reconnected before she died in 2024. Before any of the fame, he shared a cramped, broke Hollywood apartment with a pre-fame Brad Pitt. After his 2002 Indy Pro Series crash at 180 miles an hour—three skull fractures—he went back to thank the pilots and the medical crew who saved him.

He is a North Vancouver kid who played hockey, and people who work with him keep telling the same story: he treats everyone on a set the same. He has also told the truth about his own reckless twenties—the partying, the ego, the hubris of a kid famous too fast. He owns that record out loud. A man who has published his own worst chapters can be believed when the good chapters get reported.

Why I keep watching

The cases are fine. The cases were always fine. I watch for the same reason I watched the original: two decent people, doing decent work, in a world where decency is treated as a plot twist. Victoria is beautiful on camera. The baby is cute. The uncle is goofy. And Shade is still Shade—the man who notices people, likes them, and shows up.

I might be in love with Canada now. A country that produces this show, and this man, and lets both of them be unembarrassed about kindness—I will take that country's television over our prestige misery all day. I probably already am the Private Eyes guy. Fine. There are worse guys to be.

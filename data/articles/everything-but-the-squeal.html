Systems paid by the problem become the problem's landlord: they use the whole man, everything but the squeal. ---

The advertisements run between the videos, and they are aimed like a rifle at one man: Are you between fifty and sixty-four? Can you no longer work? Have you been denied? The voice is kind. The offer is free. Call the number, and somebody who knows the building will walk your claim through it, and take his pay out of the award at the far end. The number is a business, and the business stands on a law of American life that nobody passed and everybody obeys: every benefit that arrives as paperwork grows a broker at its door.

The respectable tier wears suits and answers to the government that pays it. A Social Security disability claim begins, for most people, with a denial—roughly two of every three first applications are turned down—and ends, for the ones who persist, in a hearing room. Between the denial and the hearing stands the representative: a lawyer, or an accredited non-lawyer, working on contingency. His fee is set by federal law, and the law is precise: the lesser of 25 percent of the claimant's past-due benefits or $9,200, the cap in force since November 30, 2024. The claimant never writes the check. The Social Security Administration withholds the fee from the back pay and pays the representative itself. The state collects the toll for the toll booth. By the Social Security Administration's own accounting, those withheld fees ran between roughly $924 million and $1.2 billion a year from disability benefits alone in the years 2018 through 2022, with the smaller SSI program adding a few hundred million more. A billion dollars a year, in a quiet year, to the men and women at the door.

The cathedral of the trade was Binder and Binder. Two brothers, Harry and Charles Binder, built a disability practice the way a franchise is built: television advertisements in every market, a name a frightened man could remember at 2 a.m., nearly a thousand employees, most of them not lawyers, working the claims like an assembly line. When the firm filed for Chapter 11 in New York in December 2014, it carried some 58,000 active cases. Fifty-eight thousand disabled Americans, in one firm's filing cabinets. The empire was real enough to go bankrupt like an empire.

The rest of the tier is quieter and just as legal. Elder-law attorneys advertise Medicaid planning in the open: bring us your mother's savings, and we will arrange them, lawfully, so the state pays for the nursing home. Hospitals hire eligibility firms to work the emergency-room census, because an uninsured patient is a loss until a facilitator converts him into a Medicaid enrollment or a disability award, and the firm takes its share of the recovery. Health insurance has its brokers, paid by the carriers. College admission has its brokers, paid by the parents. Every counter in the building has its man, and every man has a percentage, a retainer, or a rate.

Below the suits runs the tier nobody puts on a letterhead. Most of the advertisements in the feed belong to neither law firms nor advocates. They belong to lead generators. The advertisement harvests the applicant—his name, his age, his diagnosis, his telephone number—and sells him to the firms that work the cases, sometimes more than once. Brokers for the brokers. The man in his fifties who answers the kind voice has been bought and sold before he has spoken to anyone who can help him, and his file will be bought and sold again before it is won.

Every immigrant family knows this man in his first form. The first child through the American public school becomes the family's translator: the only one who can read the letter, fill the form, face the office. He is twelve, and he is the cultural attaché of the household, because he alone speaks both languages—the kitchen-table language of the family and the official language of the building. The benefits broker is that child grown up and incorporated. The state speaks bureaucrat. The citizen speaks kitchen-table. The man who speaks both stands at the crossing and charges for the passage, and the passage is genuinely hard: the forms defeat people whose hands shake, whose English is new, whose illness is both the reason for the claim and the reason they cannot file it. The broker's percentage is priced by the difficulty of the door, and the difficulty of the door is set by the building's owner.

Which is why the door never gets easier. Complexity is the broker's crop. Simplify the form and the broker starves; complicate it and he hires a staff. Nobody inside the building campaigns to simplify the form, because the form is the farm, and half the people in the hallway outside it are eating off the acreage. The application is long because a long application is a livelihood—for the representative, the lead generator, the eligibility firm, and the office that administers the length.

At the bottom of the tier, the crop gets harvested by fraud. Eric Conn of Pikeville, Kentucky, billed himself on billboards as Mr. Social Security and built one of the largest disability practices in America out of a small town in the coalfields. His machine had three parts: doctors who signed medical forms his office had already filled in, a judge—Administrative Law Judge David Daugherty—who took $609,000 in bribes to take Conn's cases and approve them without hearings, and a river of clients. The scheme obligated the government to some $550 million in lifetime benefits. Conn's own take, paid through the same withholding machine that pays the honest representatives, came to more than $5.7 million in fees. He pleaded guilty in 2017, cut the monitor off his ankle, and ran. The court sentenced him to twelve years in his absence. That December, Honduran officers and the FBI took him outside a Pizza Hut in La Ceiba, and the running bought him fifteen more years. Then the bill came due at the other end: the agency moved against 1,787 of Conn's former clients, and nearly half of them lost their benefits in the redetermination. The broker went to prison. His clients went back to the hearing rooms to prove a second time that they could not work.

The nonprofit tier holds the other half of the facilitation economy, and it does not wait by the door. It goes out and finds its census. Armies of social workers, employed by nonprofits and NGOs and paid overwhelmingly with public money, exist to guide people who cannot navigate the building alone: the undereducated, the mentally ill, the traumatized, the illiterate, the naive, the ones whose capacity is spent before the form is half done. The need is not invented. A man sleeping rough with schizophrenia cannot assemble a documented case for his own survival, and the worker who assembles it for him is doing the Lord's work with a clipboard. The subject here sits underneath the workers, in the arithmetic that pays for the assembling.

Grant money runs on a ratchet, and the ratchet only turns one way. A program that ends its year with money unspent has testified against itself: the need was smaller than the request. So every spot is filled, every dollar is spent and accounted for, and next year's request is this year's number with growth on it. A problem that shrinks is a budget that shrinks, and a budget that shrinks is an office that closes. Nobody in the hallway has to wish the problem well. The arithmetic wishes it for them, the way a mortgage wishes a family housed: continuously, and without malice, and without end.

I spent about ten years as a sous chef at Miriam's Kitchen in Foggy Bottom, and the kitchen understood the whole economy in one gesture: the meal is the attractant. Nobody walks in off the street asking for a case manager. They walk in hungry, for breakfast at 0630 and dinner at 1645, and they eat, and because a man is seated and fed and known by name, the rest of the facilitation can begin—the case manager, the housing list, the clinic, down to the Lifeline phone, the free phone everybody calls the Obama phone. The help is carried in inside the hospitality, the way medicine is carried in inside food. And the guest keeps his sovereignty through all of it: any man at any table can finish his plate, stand up, walk out, and sleep in the rough, and men do, and the door is open to them again the next morning. The kitchen never held a man. It fed him, and it offered, and the offering was the work.

The largest facilitation of all does not look like facilitation. It looks like charity, and its name is SNAP. The program is defended as a lifeline to the poor, and the poor do eat from it—but follow the dollar past the man. A benefit he can spend only on food, only at authorized stores, is a dollar the Treasury routes through his hands into the food economy: the brands, the bottlers, the growers, the packers, the trucking lines, the 7-Elevens and the grocery stores that would not need to exist where they stand if the rail did not run past them. The trade has a word for money that passes through an intermediary on its way to its real destination. It is not laundering—laundering disguises where money came from. This is a pass-through: it disguises where the money is going. The subsidy reads as charity to the poor. It lands as revenue for the food industry.

The government's own studies say the quiet part in numbers. The Economic Research Service calculates that a billion dollars of SNAP in a slowing economy becomes $1.54 billion of GDP and supports 13,560 jobs—the department advertises the program as an engine, because an engine is what it is. More than 260,000 stores are authorized to take the card. The retail counters that measure such things put about a quarter of the program's grocery spending in one company's registers: Walmart, which told analysts a decade ago that it captured 18 percent of the whole program. The Department of Agriculture's own 2016 study of what the money buys found soft drinks the single largest commodity purchased with SNAP dollars, 5.4 percent of the total, and sweetened beverages altogether at 9.25 percent—one SNAP dollar in eleven, routed through a poor man's hands to a bottler. The recipient is not the customer of this arrangement. He is the courier. The rail must keep flowing, and when the flow is threatened, the stores at the end of the rail feel it first and say so in their earnings calls.

Lay the trades side by side and the architecture is one architecture. I have written here about the war business, which keeps its enemy at a manageable level because a finished war closes the account. The broker keeps the form complicated because a simple form closes his. The grant keeps the census full because an empty chair closes its. A landlord does not hate his tenants; he needs them in the building, paying, indefinitely, and he maintains the building just well enough to keep them. Every system paid by the problem becomes the problem's landlord.

One defense of the whole leaking building stands in the oldest law on the shelf, and it cuts the other way. The Torah does not command efficiency in the field; it forbids the sealed field. Leviticus orders the farmer not to reap the corners of his field and not to gather the fallen grapes—leave them for the poor and for the foreigner. Deuteronomy goes further: the sheaf forgotten in the field stays forgotten, and you shall not go back for it; beat the olive tree once and do not go over the branches again. The inefficiency is mandated, and Ruth, a foreign widow with nothing, is fed entirely out of the mandated leak. The modern frame calls every leak fraud and dreams of a system with no spill. The older accounting treats the spill as the design: money aimed at babies and their mothers, at the poor, the old, the disabled, floods the field on its way through and feeds crops it never targeted—fire for effect. The leak is the oldest welfare system there is.

The packers in Chicago used to boast that they used the whole pig—everything but the squeal. These systems run the same boast on a man. His grievance is billable. His diagnosis is billable. His illiteracy is billable, his trauma is billable, his back pay carries a percentage, his hunger carries a multiplier, his need carries a grant line. Everything about him is used, by somebody, at a rate. Everything but the squeal: his own voice, saying what he wants, in his own words, at his own kitchen table. The squeal is the one part of the man no system can invoice. It is also the one part none of them is buying.

On November 1, four more states switched the rule on. Montana, North Dakota, South Carolina, and Virginia joined eight others in refusing SNAP purchases of soda, candy, and energy drinks—Florida and Montana throw in shelf-stable desserts—and the argument that followed was conducted entirely in the language of love. The Agriculture Department lists twenty-three states with approved waivers. Twelve enforce them today. In June a federal judge vacated five of them, Colorado, Iowa, Nebraska, Tennessee, and West Virginia, ruling that the department had approved the waivers without the authority to do it. The patchwork stands anyway, state by state and definition by definition, so that a bottle of sweet tea is a food in one state and contraband in the next, and the cashier's register is where the republic now holds its debate about the poor. The program underneath the debate feeds some 42 million Americans on roughly $100 billion a year, which makes the debate the largest argument about grocery shopping in the history of the republic.

The slogan arrived on schedule, and it is the strongest sentence either side produced: a child deserves candy, a child deserves soda, no family should eat a prisoner's diet for the crime of being poor, and a benefit delivered as suspicion is an insult wearing a budget's clothes. Underneath the slogan sits a serious claim about dignity. Underneath the rule sits a serious claim about money. Both claims are true. That is the whole difficulty, and it is why the argument never ends.

The rule's side is stronger than its defenders usually bother to make it. SNAP money is taxed money. It was taken from people who were never asked, paycheck by paycheck, and a program that spends other people's earnings carries obligations a wage does not. The program's name is a promise: Supplemental Nutrition Assistance Program. Nutrition is the charter. A man cashing his own paycheck owes nobody an account of the soda in his cart, because the money is his and the risk is his. A benefit is a different instrument. It is the public feeding its poor on stated terms, and conditions on public money are the ordinary machinery of every grant, contract, and appropriation Congress has ever written. The federal government conditions highway funds on a drinking age and Medicaid funds on a hundred pages of terms, and nobody calls the conditions tyranny. They call them terms.

The terms here aim at a real wound. Diabetes and obesity run hardest through the poorest counties in America, and the amputation, the dialysis, and the insulin land on the same public ledger that bought the soda. The taxpayer funds the sugar at the front of the pipeline and the surgery at the back of it. A state that declines to keep paying at both ends is persecuting nobody. It is reading its own books. Nobody is owed soda. A child whose birthday cake rides on the taxpayer's card has been fed, clothed, housed, and doctored by strangers, and the strangers' refusal to fund the frosting as well is a boundary, and a boundary is where their obligation ends. The child deserves candy the way every child deserves everything good—from his parents, out of love, and not as a line item a legislature must defend. The register already refuses things, and has for as long as the program has existed: beer, cigarettes, the hot rotisserie chicken sweating under the lamp. Nobody pickets for the rotisserie chicken. The line between food and treat was drawn by Congress generations ago and redrawn by every Congress since, and the only novelty in the twelve waiver states is where the pencil stopped this time.

The slogan's side is stronger than the slogan. Dignity is the difference between assistance and custody, and every poor family in America can tell you exactly where the line runs: it runs through the checkout lane, in public, where the register beeps its refusal and the line behind learns what kind of money you carry. No other subsidy in the American economy is audited at the point of pleasure. The mortgage interest deduction flows to the comfortable without one question about the wine cellar it shelters. Farm subsidies, corporate meals, the whole architecture of quiet support for the already-fed proceeds unexamined, un-beeped, unannounced. The one class of Americans whose treats require a state's approval is the class with the least power to object, and the approval is delivered by a cashier, in front of the neighbors, one rejected bottle at a time.

A childhood is partly made of ordinary pleasures, and pleasures rationed by rank teach rank. The poor child in the twelve waiver states is learning a lesson no school offers and no parent chose: his candy is a public question, debated by legislatures, while the candy of the child ahead of him in line is a private fact. He will carry that lesson longer than he carries any vitamin. The restriction's defenders reach for the word infantilizing—an adult trusted with nothing, a ward of the state forever. The restriction's opponents return the word with interest: a grown woman told by a register what she may drink is the one being treated as a minor, and her children are watching her be treated so. Both sides land the blow, because both sides are describing the same relationship from inside it. The waivers themselves are styled demonstration projects—two-year trials, extendable to five—and a demonstration requires subjects. The experiment is performed at the checkout, on the poor child's cart, in front of an audience, and the data collected will be entered in reports the child will never read.

Neither side has inspected an actual rich kitchen. The wealthiest, most educated, most powerful people in America do not eat ramen. They do not eat Spam, and they do not eat government cheese. They drink water, and after water maybe milk and juice, and no child in those houses has soda for breakfast, because there is no soda in the house to have. Many of those houses keep no candy at all. The nearest thing to candy on the counter is a granola bar or a protein bar, sugar in a uniform, eaten after a workout. What the slogan calls the good life—infinite candy, infinite soda, the appetite finally unchained—is a poor imagination of wealth, dreamed by people who have never had money and suppose that money is a permission slip. The real rich child receives the opposite inheritance, and he receives it early, from his own parents, as a form of love: restraint. Neither faction can spend this exhibit. The health manager cannot claim the rich kitchen, because no register built it and no waiver taught those children a thing. The dignity manager cannot claim it either, because it burns his slogan to the waterline: the treat he demands as every child's right is a treat the rich child mostly never meets.

Roald Dahl ran the custody test in 1964 and published the results as a children's book. Willy Wonka is a steward-king with a factory of infinite candy and no heir, and his golden tickets admit five children to abundance, one at a time, to be watched. Augustus Gloop tries to drink the chocolate river and is taken by it. Veruca Salt arrives with an appetite purchased wholesale—her father set the girls in his factory to unwrapping Wonka bars by the hundred thousand until a ticket surfaced, money simulating appetite until the simulation was the whole of her. Violet Beauregarde cannot stop chewing. Mike Teavee has surrendered to the screen. Four appetites, four confiscations, each child carried out of the kingdom by the thing he could not refuse. The fifth is the only genuinely poor child in the book. Charlie Bucket's family eats cabbage soup in a house with the four grandparents in one bed, and Charlie, handed the run of the factory, does not gorge. Given Wonka's gobstopper and a standing offer to sell it to a rival and buy his family out of hunger, he hands the gobstopper back. The child who has known real hunger treats abundance as a trust and not as a license, and the factory goes to him. The kingdom was never a reward for deserving candy. It went to the poor kid with restraint, over the heads of both stewards and every theory of what a child deserves.

Rome priced this arrangement honestly, once. In The Hunger Games the Capitol offers the districts the tesserae: a year's grain and oil for a poor child, in exchange for the child's name entered again in the reaping. Food aid as custody, with the hook showing. America's version hides the hook and argues about the grain. The argument is always about the menu—soda or no soda, candy or no candy—because the menu is the only part of the arrangement either side is willing to discuss. The custody underneath goes unmentioned, the way a family's worst fact goes unmentioned at its own table.

Two women came before Solomon claiming one living child, and each of them spoke fluently about love. One had rolled over on her own baby in the night and taken the living child from the other woman's bed, and by morning both women stood in the court with the same story and the same tears. The king proposed the sword, and the case ended in a sentence: the real mother offered to give the baby up. She would surrender custody itself rather than hold half a child. Solomon never meant to swing the sword. The sword was a diagnostic, and it returned its reading in one sentence. That offer is the only proof of love the story accepts, and it is the one move missing from the entire SNAP argument, on both sides, in all twenty-three states. The health managers will not surrender the recipient. The dignity managers will not surrender him either. Custody is the budget. Custody is the program office, the waiver, the campaign, the consulting contract, the identity of everyone paid to care. A faction that gave the poor man up—handed him the money without terms or audit, or closed the ledger and let him feed his family by his own arrangements—would lose the thing it actually holds, which is the man himself.

Somewhere outside the courtroom stands the figure neither claimant mentions, because he is useless to both. He hunts. He fishes. He works odd roofs and long shifts, grows tomatoes in a yard the county has never inspected, and buys his children's candy with money no committee voted on and no register audits. There is venison in his freezer that no agency counted and fish in it that no program stocked. His grocery list has never been a public question. His children have never learned their rank at a checkout. Some years he is poorer than the families in the argument. He is simply outside the ledger, and the ledger is the whole difference. The two factions will go on fighting over Solomon's baby for another generation of waivers, because the fight is the custody and the custody is the point. The test of love for the poor is the willingness to surrender custody of them. Every steward in the argument is built, brick by brick and budget by budget, to fail it.

America runs on a fable. Aesop told it twenty-six centuries ago and the country never replaced it. Work like the ant and winter cannot touch you. Sing like the grasshopper and winter collects its bill. Every life gets filed under one animal or the other. The saver is an ant. The drifter is a grasshopper. School teaches the filing system before it teaches long division.

I have been telling a friend of mine that the filing system is missing an animal. I am neither a romantic grasshopper nor an industrious ant. I am a roach.

The ant

The ant is real, and I know him personally. I have friends who worked like ants and retired at forty-five and fifty. They earned, they stored, they declined the shiny thing in front of them and bought the dull thing behind it. Their winters arrived and their stores held. The fable keeps its promise to them, and they deserve every quiet year it pays out.

Watch the ant closely, though, and watch what his arrangement requires. The ant needs winter to arrive on schedule. He needs the colony standing, the tunnel dry, the storehouse unraided. His whole genius is preparation for a specific future, and his genius pays only if that future shows up in the form he prepared for. The ant is a bet on continuity. Most years, continuity wins the bet. The ant is not wrong. He is simply leveraged.

I watch my ants now in their retirement, and the observation holds steady. They are calm men. They travel in the off-season, fix what breaks, and sleep the sleep of animals whose winter math already worked. Nothing in this notebook begrudges them one hour of it. The ant's way is a good way. It is one way, and the fable's fraud is pretending it is the only way with a door that opens.

The grasshopper

The grasshopper gets the fable's punishment and the culture's forgiveness in the same breath. He sings through the summer. He takes the afternoon as it comes. When the cold arrives he has nothing, and the story closes the door in his face. Then the culture reopens the door, because the grasshopper is the one everybody secretly envies. He had the summer. The ant had the ledger.

The grasshopper's arrangement has its own requirement, and it is stricter than the ant's. The grasshopper needs an audience. The song is the point, and a song with nobody listening is only a noise in a field. His whole life runs on being witnessed: the romance of him depends on somebody watching him be free. Take away the watchers and the grasshopper is a cold insect with good memories. The ant needs the future to behave. The grasshopper needs the present to applaud.

The animal the fable left out

The roach files under neither. The roach stores nothing for winter and sings nothing for anybody. The roach eats what the season offers, including the leavings of both other animals, and the roach is still in the room when the season changes.

The field record on this animal is not close. The roach's line was running before the dinosaurs were new. It has crossed every extinction the planet has served up since. It lives everywhere human beings live, in the walls of the rich man's house and the poor man's house on the same terms. Spray it and the survivors' grandchildren are immune to the spray. Seal the building and it finds the one seam the contractor missed. The roach does not defeat its enemies. The roach outlasts the category of enemy. Every exterminator who ever lived is dead. The roach is at the windowsill this morning, antennae up, unhurried.

The roach is also the animal of the house itself. The ant belongs to the field and the grasshopper to the meadow, but the roach moved in when human beings built walls, and it has paid no rent and signed no lease in all the centuries since. It is the most successful tenant in the history of the species that built the building. Every house has two populations: the one on the deed and the one in the walls. Only one of them has never once been evicted.

Notice what the roach does not need. No storehouse. No audience. No winter arriving on schedule, no summer worth singing about. The roach needs the world to contain one crack and one crumb, and the world has never once failed to provide both. That is not a lesser strategy than the ant's or the grasshopper's. It is the oldest strategy on the premises, and it has the longest record of the three.

The compliment hidden in the insult

The tell arrived on television. On Georgie & Mandy's First Marriage, the weather girl's mother describes her daughter: resilient, tough, like a roach. The line is built as a mother's jab, and the room hears it as one. The words themselves collapse the jab. Resilient. Tough. Unable to be killed by circumstance. The mother reached for an insult and pulled out the highest grade one animal can give another in a hard country.

People say "like a roach" when they want to say a thing is beneath them. They have the animal exactly backward. Beneath notice is the whole design. The roach carries no flag, defends no reputation, keeps no schedule the world can disrupt. It cannot be embarrassed out of the room. It cannot be sprayed out of the century. The animals we admire get statues. The animal we insult gets the future.

Field notes on one specimen

I keep the notebook on my own habitat, and the record reads the same across thirty years. Industries I worked in have been declared dead so many times I stopped attending the funerals. Print was going to kill me, then the web, then social, then search, then the machines that answer questions directly. Each wave arrived with its own spray can and its own confident exterminators. Each wave left, and I was still at the desk, doing the work in whatever form the new season wanted it.

There is an exterminator in this habitat, and he deserves a line in the notebook too. He arrives every era wearing the era's confidence, carrying the era's chemistry, certain this spray is the final one. He bills by the visit. The invoices pile up in a drawer somewhere, a paper record of fifty years of certainty. The roach has read none of them. The roach simply waits out the smell, and the smell always fades first.

I never built the ant's storehouse, and I will not pretend otherwise in my own notebook. I never sang the grasshopper's summer either; the work was always the work, season after season. What I built was harder to name and harder to kill: the ability to eat what the season offers and be standing in the room when it turns. Employers folded. Clients vanished. Whole trades were carried out of the building. The specimen remained, antennae up, reading the new room for its cracks and its crumbs, finding both.

That is the boast, and I mean it as one. The ant's stores can be eaten by inflation, illness, and time. The grasshopper's song ends with the summer and he knows it. The roach's arrangement has no single point of failure, because the roach never bet everything on the future behaving or the present applauding. Surviving is not the consolation prize for animals that failed to be ants or grasshoppers. Surviving is the power itself, and the roach holds more of it than the other two combined.

So file me where the fable forgot to look. The ants have their winters covered, and good for them. The grasshoppers have their summers, and the songs were genuinely lovely. I will be in the walls and at the windowsill, in the early light, unhurried and unbothered, long after the storehouse and the stage have both gone quiet. Neither a romantic grasshopper nor an industrious ant. A roach, and still here.

On Tuesday the Pan American Health Organization took measles elimination status away from the United States, Mexico, and Bolivia. Canada lost its own a year ago, in November 2025, and the whole hemisphere lost it with Canada. Read the American coverage and measles is a judgment on one country, one cabinet secretary, and one red hat. Read the epidemiology and you get a family tree.

The family tree

The outbreak started at a Mennonite wedding in New Brunswick in October 2024, after a guest arrived carrying the virus from abroad. The guests went home to southwestern Ontario, into Low German-speaking Mennonite communities where vaccination runs low. From Ontario it reached the Mennonite community around Seminole, in Gaines County, Texas. Early in 2025 a Mennonite schoolboy from Chihuahua visited his relatives in Seminole, went home with a rash, and his school closed within weeks. Chihuahua sequenced more than a hundred cases, and every one came back as the same lineage Canada first logged in 2024.

These are the same families. Low German Mennonites moved from Canada to Mexico in the 1920s, and from Mexico to Texas, Belize, and Bolivia after that, and they visit each other across every one of those borders. Bolivia's outbreak began in a Mennonite colony in Santa Cruz. PAHO said it plainly in August 2025: one genotype was running through eight countries, "particularly among Mennonite communities." The virus did what every virus does. It followed the relatives home.

The scoreboard nobody shows you

Canada counted 6,417 cases before it declared its outbreak over in October. In 2025 alone, Canada logged about 5,400 cases to America's 2,289, with roughly an eighth of America's population. Per person, Canada had it about twenty times worse than we did. Mexico has counted 20,701 cases and 50 deaths across 2025 and 2026. Guatemala has the biggest outbreak in the hemisphere, 34,748 cases and 53 deaths this year through October, and it still holds its elimination status because its outbreak hasn't run the twelve months the rule requires. The United States has passed 5,400 cases this year, with ten deaths.

The Americas as a whole have counted 58,213 cases and 82 deaths in 2026. America's share of that is about a tenth.

Nobody calls Canada filthy

Nobody wrote that Canada had become unclean. Nobody built a theory of Mexican sin out of Chihuahua. When measles proved about twenty times deadlier for Indigenous patients in Mexico than for everybody else, the response was compassion and vaccine brigades, which is exactly the right response. When measles reached the United States, the response was a sermon.

The sermon goes like this: America beat measles, America got proud, America got stupid, and now babies are born infected because a fascist country elected a fascist and he hired a crank. It reads like Leviticus 13. The priest examines the skin, finds the spot, and pronounces the afflicted unclean, and the afflicted must walk the road crying, "Unclean, unclean." That is a purity ritual, and the scribes of the American press have found their spot. They are pronouncing.

Who actually got sick

Go down the American list. Gaines County, Texas: Mennonite. Short Creek, on the Utah and Arizona line: the former FLDS towns of Hildale and Colorado City, still rebuilding after Warren Jeffs, where childhood shots sat low on the list until measles arrived. Spartanburg County, South Carolina, the largest single outbreak since elimination, with 997 cases: about 90 percent of them in a Ukrainian- and Russian-speaking evangelical community of about 15,000 people, by the state health department's own count. Lancaster County, Pennsylvania, the worst of 2026: the Plain communities, Amish and conservative Mennonite, where a 2025 estimate put local Amish measles vaccination at about a quarter.

The last time America came close to losing elimination, in 2018 and 2019, it was the Orthodox Jewish neighborhoods of Williamsburg, Borough Park, and Rockland County. A child came home from Israel infected, and more than 600 people in Brooklyn got sick. Same shape, different congregation.

The CDC has said it for a quarter century: nearly every large measles outbreak in this country since 2000 has run through a close-knit, undervaccinated community. These are communities that keep their own counsel, their own schools, their own languages, and their own distance from the medical world everybody else lives in. Some are white farmers in buggies. Some are Slavic immigrants in Pentecostal churches. Some are Indigenous farmworkers in Chihuahua and Maya towns on Lake Atitlán, where Guatemala's first cases turned up. Some stand apart from modern medicine by conviction, and some are left outside it by distance and poverty. The shot didn't reach any of them, and the virus did.

Where Kennedy fits

Robert F. Kennedy Jr. has said things about the MMR vaccine I would never say; he called it leaky. In April 2025 he also wrote that "the most effective way to prevent the spread of measles is the MMR vaccine," and his old anti-vaccine allies screamed at him for it. His record runs both ways, and the CDC under him still recommends two doses. Kindergarten MMR coverage fell from 95.2 percent in 2019 and 2020 to 92.5 percent in 2024 and 2025, and that 2024 class walked into kindergarten in the fall of 2024, before he ran anything. The virus reached Seminole from Ontario, through family, in January 2025. Hold Kennedy to his words. The chain of transmission runs through New Brunswick, Ontario, Chihuahua, and Santa Cruz, and every one of those places answers to somebody else.

The sermon is the propaganda

A measles outbreak makes a perfect weapon in a political war. It turns a public-health problem in a few hundred insular congregations into a moral verdict on 340 million people and the man they elected. The writers who would never call a Mennonite colony filthy, or an Amish farm, or a Hasidic yeshiva, or a Maya village, will happily call America filthy, because America is the one sinner it is safe to name. Canada gets a sympathy card. Mexico gets a vaccine drive. America gets Leviticus.

Compassion is the right answer everywhere. Measles leaflets in Low German, a mobile Plain Healthy clinic in Lancaster, interpreters in Spartanburg's Russian-language churches, mobile care teams in Short Creek: that, with emergency vaccination orders, is what ended the Brooklyn outbreak in 2019, and it is what is working now. It works because it treats the afflicted community as neighbors. The sermon treats a whole country as a leper, and measles ignores sermons. It packed a suitcase in New Brunswick and walked south, and it will keep walking until the shots reach the families it is visiting.

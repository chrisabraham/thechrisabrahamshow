I grew up in Hawaii, where the fastest way to get yourself corrected was to stand too tall. Japanese culture gave the islands a proverb for the correction: deru kugi wa utareru, the nail that sticks out gets hammered down. Australia calls the same instinct Tall Poppy Syndrome. This morning I posted that Hawaii has hammering-down-the-protruding-nail syndrome, and then I spent the rest of the morning watching the syndrome operate in American English on Threads.

The occasion was a meme from ABC's Matthew Dowd: "Why is it ok for reporters to ask Dems if they have a socialist agenda and it isn't ok to ask GOP if they have a fascist agenda?" The parity in the question collapses at the level of the words themselves. Men and women run for office as democratic socialists; they print the label on their own literature and wear it as a boast. Fascist enters American politics as an accusation, a disciplinary word, a hammer raised over somebody else's head. Nobody campaigns under it. Everybody understands the assignment when it lands: get down, recant, apologize, come to heel.

The hammer and the nail

Nazi. White supremacist. Fascist. Sexist. Homophobe. Transphobe. Anti-semite. These are tall poppy tools. Each one tells the person under it to lower his head and rejoin the row. The words work through social pain: jobs, friendships, invitations, reputation, the fear of becoming unpersoned in polite company.

I wrote this morning, and I stand by every word of it: "You can't be the solution if you sneer at normies as garbage, reprobates, and backwards failures who keep flunking your purity tests." The sneer matters because the sneer reveals the purpose. The labeler has placed himself above the row with the hammer in his hand. He gets to decide whose head sits too high, whose opinions require correction, whose presence embarrasses the garden.

One reply from enivalnoraa stated the arrangement without embarrassment: "If people don't want to be called those things, they shouldn't behave that way. I don't want a big tent if it includes bigots." There it is, clean and complete. Behave, and the hammer stays in the toolbox. Disobey, and the labels come out. The tent has a door, the door has a guard, and the guard carries a vocabulary built for compliance.

A century of full-strength doses

The kill-shot words worked for a hundred years because they were scarce, specific, and attached to horrors with names, uniforms, photographs, and graves. Fascist pointed at Mussolini's Italy, Hitler's Germany, Franco's Spain. Communist pointed at actual parties, actual regimes, actual secret police. A stigma draws its power from rarity. The crowd has to believe that the word separates a dangerous few from an ordinary many.

Then the prescription pad came out for every ailment. A man who had never attended a rally, joined a party, or harmed a soul learned that his head had been measured against the row and found protruding. The dose went out to half the country: normies, workers, parents, Catholics, Muslims, blue-collar men and women, people without college degrees, people who failed the newest purity test before breakfast.

My answer in the thread was the whole essay in miniature: "It's all played out. It worked for a hundred years but it doesn't work anymore. You used it all up. Now people just roll their eyes." I added the sentence that keeps returning to me: "It's an amazing world where all the worst kill shot slurs just make people smirk and roll their eyes." A smirk from the target is a clinical result. The medicine went in, and the patient got up from the table.

Resistance is what overuse breeds

Antibiotics fail in a familiar sequence. A powerful drug arrives and performs miracles. Doctors prescribe it for everything, including illnesses it was never built to treat. Patients take half the course and save the rest in a cabinet. Exposure after exposure teaches the bacteria the drug's shape. Eventually the strongest medicine in the cabinet meets an infection that recognizes it, survives it, and multiplies under it.

What happens when your strongest antibiotics don't work anymore? The smirk tells you the sequence has already run. People have heard Nazi attached to tax cuts, fascist attached to school-board speeches, white supremacist attached to farmers, sexist attached to grammar, and anti-semite attached to anyone standing in the wrong place during an argument about Israel. Repetition has taught the target population a lesson opposite to the intended one: the word reveals the speaker's tribe and the speaker's intentions. It carries no reliable information about the person underneath it.

That is resistance. The labeled man hears the opening syllable and translates instantly: here is the part where I am supposed to come to heel. He declines. He has seen the hammer too many times, in too many hands, over too many ordinary heads. Its sound has become familiar, and familiar sounds lose their power to startle a crowd into obedience.

Spain is the hard case

A commenter named marcoscopic corrected my first pass at the Spanish Civil War, and his correction makes the argument stronger. My geography was too clean, and one part of his rebuttal lands with full force: the fascist label in Spain had a literal referent. The Falange were real fascists inside Franco's coalition. Franco absorbed their party into his state, won the war, and ruled Spain for decades. Spain gives the label a body, a uniform, and a government.

That kernel of truth is exactly how the trick works. One true faction becomes the name for millions of people who were nothing of the kind. Every Republican became a communist in Nationalist mouths. Every Nationalist became a fascist in Republican mouths, including monarchists, conservative Catholics, Carlists, soldiers, and villagers whose politics began and ended with land, church, and fear of revolution. Each side names the whole coalition after its worst faction, then treats the name as a description of every head in the crowd.

The modern American version runs the same play with thinner material. Find the worst available person, photograph him at his worst available moment, and assign his sins to the entire row. The hammer needs one genuine nail somewhere in the lumberyard. After that, every protruding head gets the same swing.

The heel test

Judge the tool by the behavior it was designed to produce. These words exist to make people come to heel: h-e-e-l. Shrink. Apologize. Withdraw the statement. Leave the race. Hide the opinion. Return to the row and keep your head at the approved height. A political movement built its moral vocabulary around that single reflex, and the reflex is gone.

The people under the labels now smirk, roll their eyes, organize, vote, and remember exactly who swung at them. Behind the failed drug sits a politics that never learned persuasion, because persuasion requires knowledge of the patient: his work, his church, his family, his fears, his reasons. Contempt supplied a shortcut for a century. The shortcut has closed.

The hammer still swings. The poppies have heard it whistle through the air too many times, and they know the sound by heart. The nail stays up.

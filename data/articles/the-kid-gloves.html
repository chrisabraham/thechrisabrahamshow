The charge is familiar enough to recite from memory. Conservatives, we are told, look at women, at Black and brown and Native and Asian Americans, at gay and transgender people, and see minds that run a quart low. The right, on this account, believes whole classes of its neighbors are feeble-minded, and builds its politics on the belief.

Set the charge on the table and walk around it. Then walk around the other building on the lot, the protection regime the accusers designed and built for the very same classes of people, and read its blueprints. Every blueprint rests on a load-bearing assumption about the person inside. The assumption in these drawings deserves to be said out loud.

The protected person, as the framework draws her, cannot be trusted alone with a contract, a sales pitch, an insult, a fast talker, or a hard idea. Someone must stand between her and the ordinary traffic of adult life, screening what reaches her ears, her eyes, and her signature line. The law has built exactly one architecture on that assumption before, and it kept the plans. The category printed on the plans reads: feeble-minded.

What the law used to say out loud

Feeble-minded was a legal status in America, a classification with paperwork and consequences. A person adjudged feeble-minded could be placed under a guardian and relieved of the management of his own money. His signature on a contract carried no weight, because the law presumed the judgment behind the signature was defective. States barred the feeble-minded from marrying. Immigration law turned them away at the border by name; the Immigration Act of 1917 lists idiots, imbeciles, and feeble-minded persons among the excluded classes, as though weakness of mind were a contagious disease. The era's protective labor laws for women ran on a neighboring track. In Muller v. Oregon in 1908, the Supreme Court upheld limits on women's working hours on the stated ground that woman's physical structure placed her at a disadvantage in the struggle for subsistence, and the state therefore owed her a guardian's care.

More than thirty states wrote sterilization statutes for the category. Virginia's statute reached the Supreme Court in 1927 as Buck v. Bell. Carrie Buck was an inmate of the Virginia State Colony for Epileptics and Feebleminded, as her mother had been before her, and the state proposed to sterilize her so the line would end with her. Justice Holmes wrote for an eight-to-one Court: "Three generations of imbeciles are enough." The third generation was her infant daughter, adjudged feeble-minded at a few months old on the say-so of a nurse. Carrie Buck was sterilized that fall.

Read the logic in the open, because the era printed it without embarrassment. The feeble-minded required protection from their own signatures, their own money, their own marriages, and other people's persuasion. Every protection in the scheme pointed at the protected person's judgment and found it wanting. The state called this care, and by its own lights it was care. The insult rode inside the care.

The inheritance, renamed

The category died. The architecture survived it, and the new tenants moved in under new vocabulary. Statutes and codes now speak of vulnerable populations, a phrase that does in two words what the old classification did in one. Federal research rules single out whole classes of adults for additional protections, as presumptively susceptible to coercion and undue influence. Consumer law maintains doctrines that treat designated classes of buyers as unable to weigh a pitch, so the pitch itself becomes the offense. Campus and workplace speech codes rest on harm standards that presume the listener will be damaged by words an unprotected adult is expected to answer, shrug off, or ignore. Admissions and hiring frameworks defend separate and lower standards as a kindness owed to people who would wilt under the common one.

Follow the presumption through each design. In every case the framework assigns full adult competence as the default condition of exactly one class: the unprotected. Everyone else receives a presumption of fragility, graded by category, administered by people who exempt themselves from it. The administrators are competent. The protected are the concern.

Two courtrooms

Britain just ran the machinery at full scale. Russell Brand stood trial this autumn at Southwark Crown Court in London on sexual charges, which he denied, and a jury convicted him on some counts and acquitted him on others. The verdicts belong to the jury, and this page leaves them there. The architecture around the complainants is the exhibit. By statute, a complainant in a sexual case in England holds lifetime anonymity; her name is a secret the law keeps for her entire life. By statute, she is automatically eligible for special measures: screens between her and the accused, testimony by video link, cross-examination recorded in advance in a quiet room. The system builds her evidence around a working presumption that she cannot face the room and be heard in it. A grown woman, a citizen, a witness in the gravest class of case the state prosecutes, and the court's design assumption is that the ordinary posture of a witness, upright and facing the man she accuses, exceeds her capacity. The gloves are statutory. Nobody in the building finds them strange.

Massachusetts supplies the second exhibit, and the register there is older than any statute. Lindsay Clancy strangled her three children—Cora, five; Dawson, three; Callan, eight months—with exercise bands in the basement of her Duxbury home in January 2023, then went out a window and lived, paralyzed. Her defense is postpartum psychosis, a mind unseated by childbirth, and her first jury this summer split eleven to one toward finding her not criminally responsible. A retrial is set for March. Notice the temperature of the coverage across three years. The fainting salts and the fainting chair come out in the first paragraph. She is a good mother overtaken, a nurse, a sufferer, a woman the hormones and the doctors failed. A father in the same dock would receive none of it. A father who did what the indictment describes would be covered as a monster, in the largest type the paper owns. She is covered as a patient. The frame assigns her the older legal identity of her sex: the fragile woman, protected from herself first and judged, if at all, through gauze.

The witnesses from inside

The sharpest testimony against the architecture comes from people the architecture claims to shelter. Clarence Thomas, concurring in Adarand in 1995, wrote that government racial preferences stamp minorities with a badge of inferiority and teach dependency on the stamping hand. Shelby Steele built The Content of Our Character on the same observation: a preference hands its beneficiary a public doubt about his own competence and makes him carry that doubt into every room afterward. Thomas Sowell spent a shelf of books documenting what preferential frameworks do to the people inside them, in this country and a dozen others. In 2000 a presidential speechwriter gave the whole mechanism its enduring name: the soft bigotry of low expectations. Four witnesses, four different rostrums, one finding. The insult lives in the design, and the design's own beneficiaries can feel it from inside.

Psychology measured the mechanism decades ago: the Pygmalion effect, where students labeled at random as bloomers gained real ground on the strength of their teachers' expectations, and its dark mirror, the Golem effect, where low expectations from the authorities in the room measurably depress performance. The gloves announce the low expectation, and then they manufacture the results that get filed as proof the gloves were needed.

The audit

The defense of the modern framework is sincere, and it deserves its strongest form. Protections, the architects say, aim at the aggressor: the discriminator, the harasser, the predator, the tilted field. The law restrains the strong party and levels the ground, and the protected traits on the books formally cover everyone, the unprotected included. Read that way, a protection says nothing at all about the protected person's mind. It describes the other party's conduct, and it confines itself to the other party's conduct.

Audit the framework with one question and the two kinds separate cleanly. Does this rule restrain the other party, or does it manage the protected person? A law that punishes a man for refusing to hire on account of race restrains the other party; it presumes the applicant is an adult who can do the job. A standard that lowers the bar for the applicant manages the protected person; it presumes the opposite, in writing, with funding. A rule that punishes a threat restrains the speaker. A rule that scrubs words from the air because a class of listeners is presumed too fragile to hear them manages the listeners, and it grades their minds in the process. The first kind is a shield held between an adult and an aggressor. The second kind is the feeble-minded inheritance in modern dress, and the modern wardrobe holds a great deal of the second kind.

So here is the question I cannot get answered in the open: do they know? Do the architects understand that a framework built on presumed fragility manufactures the very stereotype it claims to fight, season after season, in the minds of everyone who watches a class of adults handled with gloves? Some know and accept the trade. Some have never inspected their own blueprints. The audit is available to anyone, and the blueprints are public.

The protected classes of the modern law are full adults. They can sign, spend, marry, argue, endure insult, weigh a pitch, and face a room, at the same rate of competence as the unprotected man the law leaves to his own devices. A framework that forgets it will go on teaching the country to forget it. The most respectful sentence in the law is the one that presumes an adult.

The question is being asked about Adam Curry, in forums and comment threads and search boxes: is the man legitimately born again, or is he a grifter, a chameleon who becomes whatever his wife is, whatever his environment is, whatever the opportunity pays for? People want a percentage. So many percent say the conversion is real, so many percent say it is the latest costume in a long wardrobe. The percentage does not exist. Nobody has polled it, nobody can poll it, and the answer would settle nothing if it arrived with a margin of error attached. The question itself is the wrong instrument. The book answered it two thousand years ago, and the answer is that the courier's heart is God's audit, and ours is a different one.

Paul wrote the answer from prison. Philippians 1: some men around him were preaching Christ out of envy and rivalry, aiming their sermons at the prisoner, hoping the gospel would add affliction to his chains. Paul names the motive without flinching: "The former insincerely preach Christ from selfish ambition, thinking that they add affliction to my chains." Then he gives the verdict the sincerity audit never survives: "What does it matter? Only that in every way, whether in pretense or in truth, Christ is proclaimed. I rejoice in this, yes, and will rejoice." An apostle was handed the exact file the internet keeps on Adam Curry—rival preachers, impure motives, ambition in the pulpit—and he closed the file. Christ is proclaimed. In that I rejoice. The audit Paul refused to run is the audit the comment threads run daily.

Paul had the goods on his rivals. He knew their names, their game, their target. His refusal was informed, and it was total: the proclamation is the event, the motive is weather inside another man's soul, and the word does its work in the hearing regardless of the weather it traveled through. A sermon preached to wound Paul still carried Christ to whoever heard it in the street. The hearer got the whole cargo. The preacher's ambition rode along as ballast, and ballast does not change what the ship delivers.

Caiaphas is the extreme case, and John states it flatly. The high priest argues statecraft to the council: one man should die for the people, the whole nation kept intact at the price of one innocent life—I traced that sentence's career in Who Says Your Blood Is Redder? John interrupts his own narrative to grade the speech: "Now he didn't say this of himself, but being high priest that year, he prophesied that Jesus would die for the nation." Caiaphas was arranging a murder. The words out of his mouth were true prophecy. The office carried the sentence past the man speaking it; the Spirit filed the truth through a plotter, and the plotter never knew he had been used as a courier. If a murderer at the council table can deliver prophecy intact, the sincerity of the delivery man is a thin reed to hang a gospel on.

Balaam took money for the job. Balak, king of Moab, sent princes with fees in their hands: come curse Israel for me, I will pay in honor and silver. Balaam saddled his donkey and rode out to earn the fee, and God put a sword-bearing angel in the road that the hired seer could not see and the donkey could. Three times Balaam climbed a height, built seven altars, opened his mouth to curse, and out came blessing—"How shall I curse whom God has not cursed?" The fee was real. The intent was purchased. The words that arrived were God's, all three times, and Balak went home furious at a contractor who had delivered the opposite of the order. The book keeps Balaam's greed on the record and his blessings on the record, side by side, and the blessings are the part Israel carried away.

Jonah preached in open rebellion. He had run the wrong way, been thrown into the sea at his own request, and been vomited onto the beach by a fish before he walked into Nineveh with the shortest sermon in the book: "In forty days, Nineveh will be overthrown!" Eight words in English, no altar call, no love in it, delivered by a prophet who wanted the city destroyed and said so to God's face afterward. The city repented at a scale Nineveh's own kings could never have commanded: the king came off his throne, put on sackcloth, sat in ashes, and decreed a fast for man and beast. Jonah's heart was never in the work. The message arrived intact anyway, and a pagan capital turned on the word of a courier who despised the recipients. I set Jonah's sailors beside the shepherd in Who Says Your Blood Is Redder?; here he stands on his own, the unwilling preacher whose eight words outperformed every sincere sermon in the canon measured by civic result.

Isaiah gives the doctrine its physics. "For as the rain comes down and the snow from the sky, and doesn't return there, but waters the earth... so is my word that goes out of my mouth: it will not return to me void, but it will accomplish that which I please, and it will prosper in the thing I sent it to do." Rain is indifferent to the field it falls on and to the hands that prayed for it. The word, in Isaiah's account, has the same indifference built in: efficacy lives in the word, in the sender's purpose riding inside it. The courier is a road. Roads get graded on whether the cargo arrives, and the cargo in this trade is scripture itself—God's word, Christ's word, the words of the saints and the disciples and the apostles, carried in mouths of every quality the species produces.

Cyrus never even joined. Isaiah calls the Persian emperor God's anointed, his shepherd, the man whose right hand God holds—and says in the same breath that Cyrus does not know him. Cyrus issued the decree that sent the exiles home and rebuilt the temple in Jerusalem, the single largest act of restoration in the Old Testament after the Exodus, performed by a pagan king as an item of imperial policy. God did not require Cyrus's conversion as a precondition. He required Cyrus's signature, and the signature is what the record kept. A deliverer who never believed a word of the cargo still delivered the people the cargo was about.

The catalogue is the argument. God speaks through a high priest plotting an execution. Through a hired diviner riding to a curse with the fee already negotiated. Through a prophet fleeing his assignment, preaching under protest, furious at his own success. Through apostles' rivals preaching out of envy to torment a prisoner. Through kings, through donkeys, through a burning bush talking to a murderer with a speech impediment. The record has no uncompromised couriers to offer. Every vessel in it is cracked somewhere—by greed, by fear, by ambition, by flight—and the word came through the cracks undiluted, century after century, until it reached a podcast feed.

So to the case in point, and briefly, because the case is only the occasion and the doctrine is the piece. Adam Curry was an MTV VJ when MTV played music. He helped invent podcasting in a studio in 2004, talking to whoever would listen, and he spent nineteen years beside John C. Dvorak on No Agenda deconstructing the media twice a week—1,916 episodes of it, Thursdays and Sundays, kept alive by producers paying in the value-for-value way he preached. He was an atheist for decades and said so. He came to faith in 2022, was baptized, and started the slow turn that ended the old show: Dvorak died in July of this year, No Agenda aired its last episode in October, and Curry's work now is Godcaster, which puts churches on the air as broadcasters, and We Get To Do This, a weekly show with Pastor Jimmy Pruitt where the two of them open the Bible and read it. The reinventions in his past are real—VJ, web pioneer, podfather, and, by his own naming, conspiracy therapist—and the skeptics' file writes itself from the résumé alone. The file is also irrelevant, and Paul is the authority for its irrelevance.

Here is the only audit a hearer is equipped to run: is the message intact? Curry reads the book on the air. He quotes the book. He builds machinery that puts the book into other men's mouths—three hundred churches and counting on his own platform's account, each one broadcasting into its own town. Whatever is happening inside Adam Curry's soul is happening between Adam Curry and God, and the audience's instruments cannot reach it. The audience's instruments can reach the text. The text is the gospel, whole: sin, repentance, the cross, the resurrection, grace. A man delivering that cargo is doing the one thing needful, and his ledger—grift, glory, or the ordinary mixture every courier in the book carried—belongs to an auditor who can actually see into a heart.

The fence is real, and it has a location: the message, never the motive. Defilement is an act performed on a text, and it can be described without naming any man who performs it. The hard sayings are cut and the comforting ones are kept. Repentance is left out and blessing is carried in alone. The cross is trimmed down to advice a man can follow without changing his life. Promises are added that the text never made—health, wealth, a life with the suffering redacted—and what reaches the hearer is a shorter, softer document wearing the original's binding. Balaam stands in the book as the opposite case: the fee was taken, and God's words were delivered anyway. Defilement runs the other direction: the fee is not the issue, and the words delivered are no longer God's. The defilement is audible. Anyone with a World English Bible open beside the screen can check the cargo against the manifest, which is precisely why the audit belongs on the cargo. Sincerity is invisible. Editing is not.

By that audit the current record is plain. The shows quote scripture at length and in context. The platform exists to hand microphones to pastors, not to reroute their sermons through a brand. The man says, weekly and on the record, that Jesus got him—that is his testimony, offered as testimony, and the hearer's job was never to grade the witness's soul before accepting the delivery. The hearer's job is the Bereans' job: receive the word, search the scriptures, check the cargo. A delivery can be checked. A heart cannot, and the attempt to check it produces the percentage question, which produces nothing.

Push the doctrine to its edge and the edge is the devil himself. In the wilderness he quoted scripture to Christ—Psalm 91, the words exact: he will put his angels in charge of you, and on their hands they will bear you up, so that you may not dash your foot against a stone. The quotation was accurate. The words were God's words in the devil's mouth, and heaven has never had to retract them. Mark's Gospel has an unclean spirit name Jesus in public—the Holy One of God—accurately, out loud, before any disciple had managed it. James states the standing condition of the enemy in one line: the demons believe, and tremble. Belief and trembling on the wrong side of the war have been turning men toward Christ for two thousand years, because the fear of hell is the oldest evangelist there is: it has driven more metanoia—more turning toward Jesus Christ and the Holy Spirit—than any preacher or priest. I will say it as plainly as I believe it: even the devil, if he gets the scripture right, is actually doing the will of God, and at the work of sharing the word of God and the fear of hell, he has outworked the clergy charged with the job.

The Spirit is not thwarted by mixed vessels because mixed vessels are the entire fleet. There are no other kinds on offer, in the canon or in the podcast charts. God has always spoken through the unwilling, the hired, the fleeing, and the self-deceived, and the words outlasted every one of their motives: Caiaphas is remembered for a prophecy he made by accident, Balaam for blessings he was paid to prevent, Jonah for a sermon he preached with hatred in his heart. Their intentions are footnotes. The deliveries are scripture. So ask the one question with an answer in it. Is he delivering the gospel, whole and unedited? Then the thing that needs to happen is happening, and the rest—the grift, the chameleon, the born-again heart of Adam Curry—is a file in an office none of us works in. As long as the hustler doesn't defile the message, the message does the work.

A story is going around this week, told in a comment thread, and it is a true story, and it is a sad one. A woman caught German measles while she was pregnant, in 1967. Her son was born with a severe heart defect. He went through surgery after surgery, and he died at fifteen.

The story is being told as an indictment. It lands in the middle of the measles argument of 2026 as evidence—of what the unvaccinated do, of what this administration permits, of whose sin the outbreaks are. Here is the fact the story cannot survive: the rubella vaccine was not licensed in the United States until 1969.

Nobody in that story refused anything. Nobody declined a shot. Nobody followed a bad secretary's advice or made a political choice or trusted the wrong man on television. The tool did not exist. A mother in 1967 could no more vaccinate against rubella than she could order a smartphone. The epidemic that made the vaccine urgent had just finished running through the country—1964 and 1965, twelve and a half million cases, twenty thousand babies born with congenital rubella syndrome, their hearts and eyes and ears built wrong, more than eleven thousand pregnancies lost, two thousand newborns dead. The vaccine was the answer to that epidemic, and the answer arrived in 1969, two years after the boy in the story was already damaged in the womb.

There is no time travel. Print that sentence on the first page of every argument now running. Half the moral bookkeeping of 2026 is billing the past for a pharmacy it never had—holding people in 1967 responsible for a shot invented in 1969, holding people in 1995 responsible for medicines approved in 1998. The dead boy deserves grief. What he does not deserve is conscription, sixty years later, into an argument his mother's world could not have acted on.

Safety is your job

Underneath the anachronism sits the premise I actually reject. The indictment assumes that somebody in government was charged with keeping that family safe, and failed, and owes. I deny the charge was ever issued. It has never been the government's job to keep people safe. Keeping yourself safe is the people's job. It was a household duty long before it was a federal promise, and the promise is the corruption of the duty.

Watch what the premise does to the person who accepts it. A state that adopts your safety as its mission has quietly changed what you are. You stop being a citizen who minds his own risks. You become a ward who reports them. The ward does not ask what he should do. The ward asks what he is owed, and files the difference as somebody's sin. Every tragedy becomes an invoice. Every death gets billed to a cabinet secretary. That is the whole rhetorical machine behind the story in the comment thread, and it runs on the premise, not on the facts.

My own position, at the strength I hold it: I have no compassion for the premise that America must keep its people safe. None. A government that keeps you safe from everything has taken custody of you, and custody is the thing free people were supposed to have outgrown. The risks of a life belong to the person living it. That was the arrangement. It is still the arrangement, underneath the paperwork.

Zero countries

Name the exception. There are zero countries that took whole responsibility for keeping their people safe and stopped anywhere short of authoritarianism. The arithmetic forbids the stop. A state responsible for every outcome of every risk must eventually police the risks themselves—what you eat, what you drink, what you gather for, what you refuse to inject, what you say about all of it in public. Every safety mission expands to fill the life it guards, because an unguarded corner is a future headline with a minister's name in it.

This is not a slope I am predicting. It is a job description I am reading. Total safety is a total assignment. A government modest enough to leave your risks alone is a government that has accepted limits on its mission, and the safety premise accepts no limits, because every limit can be photographed next to a coffin. So the premise wins every argument and annexes every domain, and the ward wakes up one morning thoroughly kept and entirely owned.

The founding bargain ran the other way. The American founding documents are a list of things the government may not do. They promise process—speech, arms, juries, warrants—not outcomes. Nobody at Philadelphia guaranteed anybody a safe life, because the men in that room understood the price list: a government empowered to guarantee outcomes is a government empowered to manage the people who produce them.

Canada got no sermon

Canada is the control group, and the control group acquits the accused. The same measles lineage ran through the same insular communities north of the border—communities that have kept Western medicine at arm's length for generations, in Ontario exactly as in Texas and in Chihuahua. In November 2025, the Pan American Health Organization pulled Canada's measles elimination status, a designation Canada had held since 1998, after the virus circulated there for more than a year. I have already published the full three-country measles record: one lineage, three countries, and sermons about sin preached at exactly one of them. Nobody in Ottawa stood accused of a nation's measles. The disease followed the communities. The blame followed the politics.

The gardener state

Now watch where the safety mission ends when it is allowed to finish. Canada built a national health system on the premise that keeping people safe is job number one. Job number one has a budget. In 2016, Canada legalized what it calls medical assistance in dying. In 2024, by Health Canada's own sixth annual report, 16,499 Canadians died by it—5.1 percent of every death in the country. One death in twenty, administered by the system built to keep those people alive. Since 2016 the total stands above seventy-six thousand.

And the eligibility keeps walking. Since 2021, a Canadian no longer needs to be dying to qualify. Death does not have to be reasonably foreseeable; the suffering only has to be grievous and irremediable, as certified by the system that also pays for the care. In 2024, 732 people died under that second track—people whose natural death was nowhere in sight. The garden expanded its definition of a weed.

Follow the ledger, because the ledger is the argument. When the state owns the whole cost of a life, a person becomes a line item. Care is expensive and open-ended. Death is cheap and final. A system under permanent budget pressure learns, slowly and then all at once, to offer the cheap line as compassion—and to experience the offering as virtue, because the form says the patient chose. The choice is real the way a choice between a bill you cannot pay and a door marked exit is real.

My verdict, printed as mine: MAID is the removal of useless eaters in kind vocabulary. The phrase is old and ugly and accurate. A eugenics program does not require jackboots. It requires a budget, a form, and a public that has agreed the state may run the arithmetic on which lives cost more than they bear. The vocabulary got kinder. The ledger survived. Canada did not betray the safety state. Canada completed it.

2 B R 0 2 B

All roads lead back to Kurt Vonnegut. In 1962, in the pages of Worlds of If, he published “2 B R 0 2 B”—to be or not to be—the telephone number a man dials to book his own death at the Federal Bureau of Termination. I have kept the full text of “2 B R 0 2 B” on my own site for years, because it is the shortest honest book ever written about the safety state.

In Vonnegut's America, aging and disease have been conquered, and the population is stabilized at forty million. Death no longer happens to people. In the Bureau's language, death is an adventure for volunteers. And the law of the place is absolute: no newborn child may survive unless someone volunteers to die and make room. A man whose wife delivers triplets owes the state three volunteers.

The municipal gas chambers have an official name, and the name is the whole essay. Dr. Benjamin Hitz, the man who set up the first chamber in Chicago, catches himself calling the place the Catbox and corrects himself: the official name is the Ethical Suicide Studios. “That sounds so much better.” Of course it does. That is what the kind vocabulary is for. It lets everyone in the room—the doctor, the hostess, the volunteer, the state—get through the working day without saying the plain word for what is happening.

On the studio wall hangs a mural, “The Happy Garden of Life.” Gardeners in purple, the Bureau's color, pull up weeds. They saw off dead branches. They carry the old and sickly plants to the trash-burners. Vonnegut drew the safety state as a garden and the citizen as a plant, and the gardener's entire profession is deciding what costs more than it bears. Canada has hung the same mural in a ministry office and renamed it care. The tended garden and the national health system are the same picture at different resolutions.

The woman in purple who works the studio is Leora Duncan, a hostess of the Federal Bureau of Termination. Her job, stated flatly, was to make people comfortable while she killed them. She is good at it. She believes in it. Every system of this kind staffs itself with Leora Duncans—kind people, competent people, comfortable people, people who experience the ledger as mercy and go home proud of the day's work.

The story ends the only way it can. Edward Wehling, whose triplets need three volunteers nobody will supply, shoots Hitz, shoots Duncan, and shoots himself, and makes room for all three of his children with a gun. The painter of the mural, an old man who watched all of it and lacks the nerve for any of it, picks up the telephone, dials 2 B R 0 2 B, and books his own appointment. Even the protest ends in the studio. The garden always gets its appointment.

Vonnegut returned to the institution six years later in “Welcome to the Monkey House,” with hostesses again and a world of seventeen billion, because he understood that a parlor, once imagined, does not stay inside one story. It does not stay inside fiction either. He wrote satire in 1962. Canada built the studio, staffed it, published the annual report, and called it healthcare. The one part of his design the real version kept intact was the euphemism, and the euphemism was the part he was warning about.

It is not the government's job to keep you safe. The moment it becomes the job, the garden gets a staff, the staff gets a budget, and the budget gets a vote on how long you are worth tending. Keep yourself safe. It is the last job that is still yours.

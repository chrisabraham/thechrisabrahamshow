The harvest does its part beautifully. It finds the kid—Black, brown, poor, from any ghetto you can name—and it gets him the brass ring. A seat at the boarding school. A dorm room at the university. A desk at the elite day school across town. Everybody photographs the moment. The moment is real.

The brass ring is the first step of an entire future, and the future runs on a background the kid does not have.

I wrote about the taking itself in the abduction we call brain drain. The taking is the easy part to see. This is about the part nobody photographs: the morning after the kid arrives, carrying everything he owns, in a place built for people who arrived with a whole childhood of preparation he never got.

The background

The future at an elite school runs on things that happened before the kid ever applied. Flying with your dad. Traveling with your mom. European holidays. Summers at camp with friends. A summer home where the same families return every year. Skiing lessons. Pocket money—enough of it that money is a tool and never a topic.

None of it is in the brochure. All of it is in the room. The kid who has it moves through the place like a native. The kid who lacks it moves like a tourist with a scholarship, and the place can smell the difference before he opens his mouth.

The harvest found him on merit. He got in because he was freaking awesome at math, or because his essay made an admissions officer sit up straight. The grade got him through the gate. The gate is where the grading stops and the background starts.

The invisible syllabus

There is a second education running underneath the first one, and the wealthy kids completed it before they arrived. Their grandmothers taught it at the table. How to eat. How to chew with your mouth closed. How to hold a conversation with an adult. How to compliment somebody and mean the mechanics of it. How to behave. How to keep your voice down. How to never act out.

Fighting is never okay. Physicality is a fault everywhere except the sports field, where it is a virtue with a whistle. Dormitories are quiet spaces. Libraries are quiet spaces. Street corners are quiet spaces. Stoops are for sitting, in a low voice. My Big Fat Greek Wedding put the whole curriculum in one line: rich people are quiet, poor people are loud.

Sociologists eventually named the syllabus—cultural capital, the hidden curriculum. Grandmothers had been teaching it at the table for a century before the names arrived.

The harvested kid gets the first education free and the second one never. Then the place grades him on both and calls the second grade character.

The washout

Dump a kid from a loud house into an extremely elite, extremely effete place and watch the arithmetic. He feels like a freak. He feels like he has a nose in the middle of his forehead, and everybody can see it, and everybody is too polite to mention it, which is worse.

The money runs out first in the small places. The trips he cannot pay for. The dinners he has to decline with a story. The holidays when the whole dormitory empties—everybody flying home, flying to Aspen, flying to the summer home in winter—and he stays. He stays in the dormitory over break, in the quiet the place was built for, eating what he can, waiting for the natives to return.

Then the grades go, because misery is a poor tutor. Then the kid goes. The school keeps the photograph from the day he arrived. The place he came from gets him back damaged, or loses him entirely: too polished for the block, too raw for the boardroom. The drain takes the leader the place was growing twice—once at the gate, once at the washout.

The dive buddy

I was a divemaster once. Diving has a rule that school has never learned: nobody goes down alone. You take a buddy. A dive buddy is tethered to you by attention and by plan. He checks your air. He knows your limits. When the panic comes—and at depth, the panic comes—he is already there, close enough to touch.

Every kid taken from deep in a well needs the same arrangement. He needs a buddy. A college counselor cannot fill the role. A counselor is a checkpoint: an office, an appointment, a signature on a form. A buddy is a person, present on the ordinary days, who has made the same descent and knows where the pressure changes.

Take someone from deep in a well, brain drain him into a top university, and leave him to sink or swim, and he will almost always sink. The kid can swim. What drowns him is the depth change, and depth changes are exactly what buddies exist for.

Accountable for the door

Here is the part I cannot get past. The institution is held accountable for one thing: letting them in. The entering class gets counted, photographed, and reported. The washouts get counted as personal failures and filed under fit.

If an institution is content with that arrangement—happy to take the credit at the door and let the kid sink in the deep end—then the arrangement has a name, and the name is nefarious. I hope the people who run it never tell me they are content with it, because I never want to know it for certain.

I do not think liberal progressive elite people are very nice at the end of the day. They built a machine that photographs beautifully at intake and shrugs at the empty chair in the dormitory, and they call the photograph compassion.

My father bought the books

I know the invisible syllabus is real because my father had to buy it.

Bob Abraham grew up in North Jersey, the son of a factory worker who came home drunk every night. He spent four years in the Marines. He wanted Madison Avenue, and he understood, the way the harvested kids understand it in their bones, that wanting it and deserving it would leave him one education short. So he bought the missing one. A matched row of hardcover books—etiquette, elocution, the whole syllabus in leather-bound-looking covers—and he read them through, all of them, while he was going to the School of Visual Arts in the sixties.

He did it because he refused to sound like a North Jersey pumpkin when he entered that world. He refused to be the ignorant pig at the good table. Nobody handed him a buddy. The books were the buddy system of a man who had nobody to tether to: every rule the grandmothers taught, written down and sold by mail to the kids the grandmothers missed.

And here is the proof that the polish was equipment. My dad code-switched the entire time we lived in New York City and Weehawken, every day of it, and he kept it up exactly as long as he had to. The day we moved to Hawaii, he put the performance down. He became a low class populist hedonist party boy, and he stayed one: shorts, flip-flops, Hawaiian shirts, never anything else, ever again. It was the unbuttoning of the pants after the big meal. The books had bought him a performance he could sustain for precisely as long as the future required, and not one day longer.

The move that released my father is the move I have called an abduction since I was six, because I was the kid in the back of it. Same move. Same ocean. He got out of his well and took the uniform off. I got carried out of my world in the luggage.

The harvested kid deserves what my father built for himself out of mail-order hardcovers: the syllabus, handed over by somebody tethered to him, in time to use it. Give him the buddy. The ring was only ever the first step.

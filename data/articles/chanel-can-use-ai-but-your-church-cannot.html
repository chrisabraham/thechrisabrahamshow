Somebody gathered up the flyers—church flyers, group flyers, small-business flyers, the family market, the night market, the men's night, the DJ lineup—and laid them out in one big grid under the words It All Looks The Same, and a friend of mine shared it with her own verdict on top: AI is the ultimate turn off. The flyers are guilty as charged. They do all look the same. Glossy, saturated, beaming, interchangeable, a hundred little posters shouting in the same accent. The pile is real. The conclusion drawn from the pile is where the trouble starts, because the objection was never really about how the flyers look.

Having taste is rare. Real taste—an innate hunger for beauty, a unique and elegant palette, the discernment to know the difference between the two—is maybe five percent of humanity, and that five percent sorts into exactly two camps: people who buy very expensive bespoke things, and people with no money at all. The first camp commissions. The second camp judges. Neither camp was ever going to pay for the illustration on a church newsletter, and neither camp is who the church newsletter is for.

The money lives at the top of the bell curve, in the great wide middle where most people actually are, and what AI did for that middle was remove the friction. Before it, the choice facing the person putting together the bulletin or the market flyer was never between a good illustration and a bad one. The choice was between an illustration and no illustration at all—between a page with a picture on it and a page of lonely type. Commissioning a work of art to announce a potjie competition or a weekend vibes night was never on the table, at any price, in any decade. The friction of hiring, briefing, waiting, and paying was the whole barrier, and the barrier is what fell.

My father ran a production company in Hawaii in the seventies and eighties, Abraham and Dunn, and his studio held entire books of clip art—giant bound volumes of open, available images and graphics, bought for exactly this purpose. You took an X-Acto knife and cut the picture out of the book. You photographed it, you Xeroxed it, you put tracing paper over it and drew it yourself, and that was illustration for everyone who was not rich. The books were not a cheat in that studio; they were furniture, as standard as the light table, and the clients—hotels, restaurants, little businesses all over Hawaii—got their menus and their ads and their brochures with pictures on them, which was the entire point of the exercise. That is literally where the word comes from: clip art, art you clipped. Then Microsoft Word arrived with its own collection of cheap little pictures bundled in, and a generation of secretaries and pastors and PTA mothers illustrated everything in sight, and it was good enough for church for decades. Nobody held up a church bulletin in 1997 and declared a crisis of civilization. The bulletin had a dove on it. The dove came free with the software. Everyone went to lunch.

Every new way of making pictures gets accused of murdering the old one, and the corpse keeps getting up. Clip art was going to ruin illustration. Stock photography was going to ruin photography, and commercial photography with it—and my buddy is seventy years old and still getting hired to photograph malls, because what stock killed was the commodity end of his trade, and he was never the commodity end. A photographer who can demand a premium, who has relationships with the companies, who people like working with, who has a line item in somebody's budget with his name effectively on it—that man works at seventy. The photographer who was interchangeable gets interchanged. That is the whole casualty list, at every level, in every medium: the crappy photographer, the crappy illustrator, the crappy designer, the ones whose work a client could not pick out of a lineup and whose absence nobody would notice until the invoice stopped. AI is hard on them because the market was already hard on them. The tool just made the verdict cheap to execute. The ladder is the same at every rung: the bottom of it is always being automated, dissolved, or given away, and the top of it keeps its phone number in the client's pocket.

I know the middle of that ladder from the inside, because I have stood on every rung of it as a buyer. For years I went to Fiverr for all my holiday illustrations—somebody, somewhere, drawing my Christmas graphic for the price of a sandwich. Before that and alongside it, Pixabay, for free clip art and free photographs, because free is a powerful argument. People with a little money went to the stock houses and paid. I never once, in all those years, commissioned an artist to make a bespoke illustration for a newsletter, and neither did you, and neither did the church. Now AI makes my holiday graphics, and the only thing that changed is the address of the transaction. The art budget for a Thanksgiving graphic was zero before AI existed. It is zero still. Nothing was taken from any artist, because there was never a job there to take—there was a Fiverr gig at best, and a blank space at worst.

And the slop itself is not a fixed substance. It is a measurement. AI output is exactly as bespoke as the person prompting it is willing to make it: prompt toward a vision and the tools will follow you there, refine, reject, refine again, until the thing on the screen is the thing in your head, limited by imagination and taste and nothing else. The prompting itself is a skill that compounds: the longer a person works the tools, the better their prompts get, and the less slop they are willing to put up with, because they have learned what the machine will do when it is pushed. The people who sneer at slop are sneering at the average of everybody's effort, and the average is low because most people do not have much imagination or vision or taste, or do not prioritize beauty or balance or negative space, or simply need the barrier of good enough broken so they can hit publish and check something off the list. Everybody just wants a stupid Christmas card. Everybody just wants a Thanksgiving graphic to go with the newsletter. For them the picture is not the product. The picture is a chore that stands between them and the product, and a chore gets done, not savored. Your art is just someone else's task.

The people who can afford anything—the houses that can populate and cast and shoot a Tiffany campaign, a Dior campaign, a Chanel campaign, the firms whose entire business is selling taste back to the world at the highest prices on earth—are leaning into AI themselves: AI-generated animation, AI-generated scenarios, whole little worlds rendered instead of built, used without apology by the very institutions that employ the finest art directors alive. The top of the market, where money is no object and discernment is the brand, looked at these tools and took them up. And they reach for it for the oldest reason in fashion: to not look dead. A house that only sells its past is a museum with a price list—the house of this and the house of that, heritage stacked on heritage until the brand is an heirloom nobody young wants to inherit. Chanel lived in that museum for a while: No. 5 and the quilted skirt suit, what old ladies wore, respectability embalmed. Balenciaga built a second career out of refusing it—this is the house that put its clothes on The Simpsons, a whole episode of America's cartoon family walking a Paris runway in Balenciaga, cartoon animation anointed as high art by the most expensive art direction on earth. Whatever the new machine makes this season is what a house wears to prove it is alive in this one. The objection survives only further down the ladder, among the people whose taste is a posture rather than a budget.

And the pictures are only the first hire. The houses will come for the models next, because an AI model is above controversy for the simplest of reasons: she does not exist. She cannot be trafficked and she cannot be abused; she has no pimp, no coyote, no cartel. Pornography will get there for the same reason—an industry that can sell a woman nobody ever touched has solved its oldest moral problem by deleting the woman. The perfect model never ages, never gets fatter, never says anything stupid into a microphone, never has a public affair, never embarrasses the house in an airport. She says exactly what she is told to say, and when the body type of the moment shifts, she shifts slightly with it—the way the bodies in Grand Theft Auto 6 are already shifting it: Lucia, five foot two of hourglass that no agency's petite board would ever have signed, making men around the world salivate. By men around the world, I mean me. My mother would have taken one look at Lucia and called her a fireplug—a sausage tied in the middle. She was silent generation, formed at the height of haute couture, and her model stood five foot ten: long, lanky, slender, nearly flat-chested, narrow-hipped, small-bottomed, flat-tummied. My mother was five foot six and counted herself shortchanged. The standard she was raised on and the standard salivating now cannot both be nature. They are fashion, and fashion is the bell curve in better tailoring.

So the grid of flyers proves the opposite of what it was posted to prove. If the imagery is good enough for the houses that sell taste itself, then the objection to the church flyer was never about quality. It was about who is allowed to have pictures at all.

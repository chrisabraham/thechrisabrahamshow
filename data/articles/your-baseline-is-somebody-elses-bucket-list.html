Chris Abraham stacks the exhibits—Portugal's visa stack and its reversal, Puerto Rico's 5,852 investor decrees, Thailand's investment-board visa, Mexico City's fifty-percent cap, Siargao's Manila-priced studios, Hawaii's measured dependence—and closes on the frontier's next address: Acapulco, where world-class beauty still carries a fear discount. ---

It is one market. A man earns his salary in San Francisco and spends it in Lisbon, and the move gets reported as a lifestyle. It is an arbitrage. A salary is worth what the streets around it charge for a life—rent, dinner, a roof that does not leak—and the same paycheck is a fortune on one shoreline and a scrape on another. Move the paycheck, keep the number, and the gap between the two baselines pays for everything: the apartment with the view, the dinners out, the savings account that finally fills. Hawaii, Lisbon, San Juan, Chiang Mai, Mexico City—the datelines change and the story does not. A beautiful place where life is cheap by the visitor's arithmetic fills up with visitors who stay, the locals get priced toward the exits, and the officials who invited the money stand in front of cameras acting surprised. The surprise is the only dishonest part. The rest is arithmetic.

The Cheat Code

A remote salary is a cheat code because the economy it lands in did not issue it. Portugal wrote the code into its own law. The D8 visa, the digital nomad visa, asks a foreigner to show average income of €3,680 a month—and the number is not arbitrary. It is four times the Portuguese minimum wage, which is €920 in 2026. Admission is priced at four local floors, earned somewhere else. The sum is an unremarkable professional salary in the places the applicants come from; that is the whole point of it. Thailand's Long-Term Resident visa asks a Work-from-Thailand Professional to show $80,000 a year, averaged over two years: the threshold is denominated in the visitor's currency, not the host's. In Da Nang, a one-bedroom apartment near My Khe beach rents for the dong equivalent of four to five hundred dollars a month. The man paying it is not poor and he is not rich. He is ordinary, at San Francisco prices, and a king at Da Nang prices, and the difference is not in him. It is in the baseline under his feet.

The nomad does not compete in the local economy. He bids against it, in money it cannot print, for a stock of roofs that does not grow just because he arrived. A landlord with one flat and two bidders—a Lisbon nurse and a London engineer working from the spare room—does not need a theory of gentrification. He needs a pen. Rents in these markets are not set by what the place earns. They are set by the highest baseline in the room. And once the rents move, everything priced off the rents moves behind them: the café reprices its breakfast for the laptop table, the tradesman quotes in the visitor's currency, the teacher and the nurse and the policeman start commuting in from the towns their grandparents left. The cheat code does not stay in the cheat's pocket. It rewrites the price of being local.

The States Bid for the Money

Governments compete for the foreign salary the way they once competed for the factory: with tax codes and visas, in writing, on purpose. Portugal built a whole stack of invitations—the D7 for passive income, the D8 for remote workers in 2022, a Golden Visa that sold residency for real estate, and the Non-Habitual Resident regime, a ten-year tax break for new arrivals. The stack worked. Portugal now counts about 1.6 million foreign residents, roughly one person in seven, more than double the share of a few years ago. Puerto Rico made the bluntest offer on earth. Act 22 of 2012, folded into Act 60 in 2019, tells a mainlander: become a bona fide resident of the island and pay zero tax on the capital gains you accrue after the move. The U.S. Government Accountability Office counted 5,852 of those investor decrees granted from 2012 through 2024. The peak year was 2021—1,143 decrees—the year the mainland's offices stood empty. The GAO also ran the arithmetic on the people who took the offer: after the move, their federal taxes paid fell by nearly half. And a Puerto Rican who never left cannot have one. The invitation is addressed, by law, to people who do not live there.

Thailand runs its Long-Term Resident visa through the Board of Investment. Not the immigration department—the investment board. The salary is the investment, and the paperwork says so. Underneath the velvet rope it hung a mass-market door: the Destination Thailand Visa of 2024, five years of validity at 180 days a stay, aimed at the same laptops at a lower price of admission. The Philippines signed Executive Order 86 in April 2025, creating a digital nomad visa by decree; before the decree, tourist extensions that can run to thirty-six months did the same work without the name. Vietnam never built the visa at all. In August 2023 it tripled the e-visa to ninety days for every nationality on earth, and the grey area does the rest—a whole coast of laptops in Da Nang and Hoi An runs on ninety-day paper, renewed at the border. Every finance ministry on the list ran the same calculation: a foreign salary spends like an export and never asks for a factory.

The reversals are also in writing, and they are not endings. Portugal closed the real-estate route to the Golden Visa and shut the tax break, and doubled the road to citizenship from five years to ten. Spain ran both plays in the same decade—its Startups Law of 2023 created a digital nomad visa with a tax break attached, and in April 2025 it abolished the Golden Visa outright—and Barcelona, which counted some 26 million visitors against 1.7 million residents in 2022 and spent the summer of 2024 watching protesters spray diners with water pistols, will let every one of its 10,101 licensed tourist flats expire in November 2028 and renew none of them. Mexico City's decree of October 2024 caps a registered platform rental at half the nights in a year. Puerto Rico, from 2027, charges new arrivals 4 percent where it charged zero. Read the pattern whole: the states are not closing the auction. They are raising the paddle price, because the queue outside did not shorten.

The Friction Is Gone

Subletting is older than the republic. Boarding houses, landladies, the spare room over the garage—strangers have always rented beds from strangers. What the platform built was the trust machinery: global payments, reputation scores, insurance, search. The machinery took an informal arrangement and made it legible to capital, and a spare room became an asset class—an investor can buy the whole building and run a hotel that never calls itself one, on a street zoned for families. Cap the platform and the practice changes clothes. The flat that cannot be rented by the night in Mexico City is rented by the thirty-one-day corporate lease instead, out of the housing stock just as completely, and the guest who came once through the app leaves with a WhatsApp number for the next stay. The demand does not vanish. It migrates to wherever the friction is lowest, which is the entire history of the thing in one sentence. The friction itself is a price: in Da Nang the same apartment rents for half to double depending on whether a man found it through a platform or through the street, and the spread between those two numbers is exactly what the machinery charges for trust.

The Pacemaker

The word for the host side of the trade is a pacemaker. An economy that takes the inflow does not add it on top of the old economy. It re-forms around it. The restaurants price for the visitor. The landlords hold out for the visitor. The young learn the visitor's language and the visitor's trades, and the trades that served the neighbors thin out behind them. Hawaii is the measured case. The state's own economists at DBEDT put tourism at 16 percent of GDP before the ripple and 22 percent counting it, with some 170,000 jobs hanging off a visitor economy that spent $23.9 billion in 2024—and that is the statewide average, which hides the counties where the count runs to a third of everything the island produces. Siargao is the same shape at island scale: 557,094 visitors in 2024 against 145,806 residents—six years after Condé Nast Traveler's readers voted it the best island in the world, ahead of Bali—studios in General Luna renting at 35,000 pesos a month—Manila prices, on a fishing island that imports more than 90 percent of its produce.

Then 2020 ran the experiment no one would have approved. The inflow stopped, everywhere, at once—and the hosts did not heal. They coded. When the flow returned it returned changed: the visitor had learned he could stay, work from the terrace, and never go home, and the season became a residency. A pacemaker is a heart that no longer keeps its own time. Nobody sensible rips one out on the table. Nobody honest calls it a heart.

The Door Guards Itself

The first wave never stays a wave. Five years in, ten years in, the arrivals have the café, the school, the grievance—and the baseline they found has become the thing they defend against the next arrivals, who are carrying the same money they carried. The mechanism needs no villain. The first wave's money is already inside the prices, invisible; the second wave's money is the visible push. Every man in the story believes the reasonable number of newcomers is the number that arrived before him, plus one. The reflex is not tropical and it is not foreign: Spring Lake, New Jersey, printed it on bumper stickers—Locals Only, No Bennys, Benny being the shore's name for the summer people down from the north—the same sticker Hawaii prints as Locals Only, No Haoles. Two oceans, one door. In time the first wave becomes a constituency, and the caps and decrees in the capitals are written partly in its voice: the settled foreigner is the loudest local voice for closing the door he came through, because he alone knows exactly what walks through it.

The Frontier Moves

The frontier's fashion follows the frontier's prices. Bali priced up, and the laptops went to Chiang Mai. Chiang Mai's burning season—the months the smoke sits on the city—pushed them on to Da Nang, which is filling now with the people Chiang Mai's air and Chiang Mai's rents expelled. Da Nang will do the same work on itself in time. What the frontier wants is always the same three things: beauty, cheapness, and a discount. Beauty and cheapness alone never hold the price for long—a single season of photographs arbitrages them away, the herd arrives, and the baseline gap that made the place a bargain closes over the bargain. A reputation is the only fence that keeps a price down for years. The deepest discount is a reputation for danger.

Acapulco is the proof standing at the end of the road. It was the original—the jet set's beach, Sinatra singing its name, Elizabeth Taylor married over that bay, Howard Hughes spending the last months of his life in the Princess Hotel above it. The Mexican government built Cancún out of empty sand to compete with it, and then the cartel wars came, and by 2013 Acapulco sat on the published lists of the most dangerous cities on earth. Air arrivals fell to a quarter of what they had been in 2008. In October 2023 Hurricane Otis came ashore at Category 5 and damaged four out of five of the city's hotels in a single night. The bay is still the bay. The cliffs at La Quebrada are still the cliffs. The price carries the headlines, all of them, stacked.

I was in Acapulco when it was dangerous. If I were a digital nomad, it is exactly where I would go. That is not a taste for risk. It is the market's own nose, and I have it: world-class beauty at a fear discount is the frontier's whole definition. The first laptops are always already there—in the café over the bay, earning San Francisco and spending Acapulco—while the herd back home is still deciding whether the place is safe.

Your baseline is somebody else's bucket list. The trade between the two never closes. It changes beaches.

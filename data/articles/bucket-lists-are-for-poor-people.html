A bucket list is a catalog of things a man expects to do exactly once. The name confesses the arrangement: the list is compiled against the bucket, against the kicking of it, and every item on it carries the same silent premise. This will happen one time. Then the money or the body closes the account, and the item stands in the record as the single occasion on which the life described by the list was actually lived.

Nobody lists the things he does as routine. No man writes down breakfast. He writes down the dive, the summit, the far city, the table he cannot ordinarily afford, because the listing is itself the admission: these things stand outside his life, he knows they stand outside his life, and he has organized the knowledge into a document he keeps. The lists live in journals and on refrigerator doors and in applications built for the purpose, and a whole shelf of self-help now instructs the locked-out in the proper keeping of them, thirty things before thirty, fifty before fifty, the arithmetic of a countdown. A bucket list is an inventory of exile, drawn up by the exile, in his own handwriting, and then sold back to him as a genre of hope.

The items themselves are nearly always the same shape. See the reef once. Stand in the capital once. Eat in that restaurant once. Swim in that water, hear that orchestra, walk that wall, sleep in that ice hotel, once. The singularity is the content. A man who dives every summer would no more put diving on a list than he would put shaving on one. The list exists precisely where repetition is impossible, and it burns there with a particular voltage, the way a photograph burns in the house of a man who was admitted to the room a single time and took the picture to prove the room exists and that he was, once, inside it.

Hollywood understood the instrument well enough to name a film after it. In The Bucket List, a mechanic and a hospital billionaire share a cancer ward and a list, and the list becomes actionable only when the billionaire in the next bed agrees to fund it. The fantasy required that structure. A poor man's list, executed at last, had to be paid for by a rich man, because the list was never a plan. It was a bill the list-maker could not cover, itemized.

The opposite condition has produced no literature, because it contains no longing. What most of the world keeps at the distance of imagination, the rich do on an ordinary Tuesday, and again the Tuesday after, until the far thing is weather. The dive trip is a season. The other continent is where the children are at school, or where the house stands, or where the boat winters. Repetition does its slow work on the experience itself: the fiftieth dive carries none of the charge of the first, because repetition converts wonder into competence, and competence into maintenance, and maintenance is simply the texture of a life. The rich man at his ordinary Tuesday table is not having a smaller experience than the poor man at his once-in-a-lifetime table. He is having a different substance altogether, one in which the extraordinary has been fully absorbed into the expected, and the absorption is the wealth.

The rich have seasons instead of lists. The summer place opens in June and closes in September and has done so since before its current owners were born. The Christmas house receives the same family in the same week of December, year upon year, until Christmas itself has an address. The camps carry the same arrangement across a longer span: Jewish families and Christian families have sent their children, then their grandchildren, then their great-grandchildren to the same summer camps for a hundred years, until the lake and the dining hall and the songs are family property in every sense that matters, and a child arrives at a place his great-grandfather can describe to him bunk by bunk. Nothing on that calendar is an event. Events are what happen to people whose lives are not arranged in seasons.

Washington makes the contrast visible inside a single city. The schools of the comfortable bus their children to the capital as a whole, to stand in the Rotunda and before the memorials and absorb the Americanness of the place as a curriculum, and the children come from Ohio and from Oregon by the hundreds of thousands. Children who grow up in Washington's own poor neighborhoods, hyperlocal by necessity, their world a few walked blocks repeated ten thousand times, have in large numbers never been taken on that tour. The monuments stand in their own city. From certain streets the children walk, the monuments can be seen. The barrier is routine: no one's routine includes taking them, and a tour, like everything else in this accounting, belongs in practice to the people for whom it is routine. The schoolchild from Ohio gets the capital. The child from across the Anacostia River gets the view of it.

Between thirteen and eighteen I dove with my father in Hawaii often enough that breathing underwater stopped registering as an event. It was what we did. There was no list from those years because there was nothing to list; the reef was a weekday. I have stood inside the rich man's condition, in that one province, and I can testify that its defining feature is the absence of the very feeling the poor man's list is made of. Nobody in that boat was storing the dive against death. We were storing it against nothing. We assumed the next one.

My wiring has since removed me from the argument, and I record the removal as fact. I have SDAM, severely deficient autobiographical memory, and I have aphantasia, which means no picture forms in my mind when I remember and the past does not return to me as scene or film. A thing done is done. It leaves behind fact, dates, the knowledge that it happened, and it leaves no footage to ache over. So I am content, genuinely and without any discipline I had to learn, to do a thing once. The poor man's ache depends on a theater I do not have: the experience must replay, in the head, at night, as a place to want back into. Mine does not replay. I watch the two economies of longing, the list and the season, from outside both of them, and I report what I see the way a man reports weather in a country whose climate he can measure and cannot feel.

Being rich is several things, and the top of it deserves its own accounting because it refutes the easy version of everything above. Above the tier of seasons sits a voracious tier, and I have watched it at close range for years: people whose appetite has been normalized at such scale that Ibiza becomes a weekend errand, a flight taken because a friend is DJing on the island and support is what friends do, the whole Mediterranean crossing filed under loyalty. This is a different way of gulping life, and I will record its price without decoration. It is expensive. It is exhausting. Whether it is enviable is a question I find genuinely open, and the people inside it do not strike me as rested. The mechanism is visible once the seasons are understood: repetition dissolves the charge of an experience, so an appetite committed to the charge must escalate the dose, new water, new island, new altitude, forever, at rising cost, merely to feel what the poor man feels once, for free, in the boat he will board a single time.

There are two treadmills in this accounting, and they run in opposite directions. The poor man runs to reach the experience once, to earn the single entry on his list before the account closes. The voracious rich man runs to keep a normalized appetite alive at its accustomed scale, which is a maintenance bill that never stops arriving. One runs toward a memory he will spend the remainder of his life trying to return to. The other runs to stand still. Between them stand the ordinary rich of the seasons, for whom the far things are weather, and beneath them all stands the man with the list, for whom the far things are literature.

A bucket list is the literature of people locked out of repetition. It is the dream of return, written down by people who were taken once, or who were never taken at all. The rich do not write it. They are occupied with the items themselves, a second time and a tenth and a fiftieth, on an ordinary Tuesday, in the season that bears the item's name.

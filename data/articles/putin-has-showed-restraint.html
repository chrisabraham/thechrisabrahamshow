Jared Kushner went on a podcast and said a true thing, and Washington treated it as a scandal. The podcast is MD Meets, Mathias Döpfner's interview show, and the episode posted on November 25. Kushner and Steve Witkoff are the two men running the Ukraine negotiations, and Kushner has sat across the table from Vladimir Putin at least four times. Here is what he said: “So far Putin has showed restraint; he's agreed not to use nuclear weapons, which obviously is a big thing, and he's reassured us that that's not his intention.” He called Putin “a grandmaster of politics” and “a historic figure.” He said Putin is “always very well-prepared” and has “a very deep understanding of the issues.” Then he said the sentence that actually caused the explosion: “This isn't about fairness or who's right or wrong. It's about how do we end the killing and create a better life for people going forward.” And the method, in one golf line: “We can't wind back the clock, right? We have to play the ball where it lies.”

The pile-on arrived on schedule. Senator John Cornyn of Texas, a Republican, shared the interview and wrote one word over it: “Astonishing.” What astonished him was the fairness sentence—a negotiator announcing in public that his job is ending the killing rather than assigning the blame. Michael McFaul, Obama's ambassador to Moscow, hurried out to rule that Putin is no grandmaster and no great leader, and that anyone who kills hundreds of thousands of his own people to seize villages nobody has heard of is neither. Read McFaul's sentence again. It concedes the whole measurement: hundreds of thousands of Russians dead, villages taken one at a time, at walking pace, for going on five years. That is the war Putin chose to fight. The other war, the short one, sits in silos and submarines and has never been touched.

The Doctrine Is Published

Russia's nuclear doctrine is not a secret and it is not a mood. It is a signed, published state document, and Putin signed a revised version in November 2024, in the middle of this war. The doctrine names the conditions under which Russia reserves the right to use nuclear weapons: a nuclear attack on Russia, an attack on Russia's nuclear forces, a conventional attack that threatens the existence of the Russian state. The 2024 revision added a clause written for exactly this war—aggression against Russia by a non-nuclear state, carried out with the participation or support of a nuclear state, counts as a joint attack by both.

The war as it is actually fought matches the clause element by element. Ukraine is a non-nuclear state. Ukraine strikes airfields, refineries, and cities deep inside Russia with weapons supplied by nuclear states, aimed with intelligence supplied by nuclear states, paid for by nuclear states. Every element of the joint-attack clause is present in the daily news. Kushner said the plain version himself, in the same interview: “This war is somewhat contained to Ukraine but it's effectively become a proxy war where Europe is arming and funding Ukraine against Russia. It won't take a lot of miscalculation for this thing to escalate massively.” By the letter of Moscow's own published doctrine, the argument for use has been available to the Kremlin for years. The weapons have not been used. That is not a compliment. It is a measurement.

The doctrine is not a museum piece, either. This week a senior Russian diplomat, Dmitry Polyansky, stood up in Vienna and warned that nuclear war is “inching closer,” accusing NATO of rehearsing the seizure of Kaliningrad. Moscow keeps reading its doctrine aloud, in session, on the record. The only people refusing to hear it sit on our side of the table.

What Moscow Has Said for Thirty Years

Moscow's position is also published, and it is also old. Putin laid it out at Munich in 2007, in front of the assembled West, and he has repeated it ever since: NATO is a hostile military alliance, Ukraine inside NATO is an existential threat, American weapons on Russia's border minutes from Moscow are a line that will be answered. In December 2021, two months before the invasion, Russia handed Washington draft treaties asking in writing for the alliance to stop expanding toward Russia's borders. Washington declined to take the paper seriously. The invasion followed the paper by nine weeks.

None of this is hidden knowledge. John Mearsheimer, the political scientist at the University of Chicago, predicted this war in 2014 and named the mechanism and the trigger in advance: great powers move to stop a hostile alliance from expanding onto their borders, and they do not ask permission first. He was not reading tea leaves. He was reading Moscow's mail, which Moscow had been sending, postage due, for decades. Russia telegraphs everything. The speeches, the doctrine, the draft treaties—the whole war was announced in advance by the man who launched it, in documents anyone could read, and the West's strategic class chose to hear appetite where a state had stated a position.

Whether the fear behind that position is genuine or manufactured is a seminar question, and it changes nothing. States act on perceived existential threats exactly as they act on real ones; the missiles do not check the sincerity of the hand on the key. Israel is understood on these terms without argument—nobody serious doubts that Israel would use its nuclear weapons before accepting annihilation, or that it would absorb staggering losses first, because Israel has said so and means it. Russia has said the same about itself, in the same register, for thirty years. One of these statements is treated as a fact of the region. The other is treated as a bluff, because treating it as a fact would end the argument the war party is winning.

Restraint Is a Measurement

Restraint is the distance between what a man can do and what he has done. Putin commands the largest nuclear arsenal on the planet—thousands of warheads, tactical and strategic, deliverable in an afternoon. He has fought this war instead with artillery, glide bombs, drones, and infantry, grinding forward village by village at a cost McFaul himself puts at hundreds of thousands of Russian lives. Every month of that grind, the arsenal has sat untouched while Russia paid in blood for ground it could have tried to take by terror in a week. The man's soul is between him and God. The ledger is public, and the ledger says that by the doctrine he published, facing the threat he has named for thirty years, the weapons should have flown by now.

They have not flown. And the restraint runs in both directions, which is how you know it is deterrence and not theater. NATO will not enter this war directly—no alliance troops, no alliance pilots, no no-fly zone—because Russia has nuclear weapons and will use them if cornered, and every general in Brussels knows it. Russia, for its part, has calibrated its war to stay short of a direct exchange with NATO, striking the client and not the patrons. Each side is holding a line the other side drew. That quiet, mutual, uncelebrated restraint is the only reason this war is a war in Ukraine and not a war of the world. Kushner's offense was naming one half of it into a microphone.

I have written on this site that the war pays too many people—before it, during it, and after it—for anybody to want it to end, and that it is a war watched from couches by people whose own sons are not in it. Those are reasons the killing continues. Underneath both sits the narrower fact: the killing continues below a ceiling. The ceiling was published by the Kremlin. For nearly five years the Kremlin has stayed under its own ceiling while the West tested, month by month, how much of a proxy war the ceiling would hold. Every serious person in this business knows that is the game being played. The outrage at Kushner is the sound of people who know it, objecting to it being said where the public can hear.

Play the Ball Where It Lies

The ball is where it lies. It lies nearly five years into a war between a nuclear superpower that calls the fight existential and a neighbor armed by the rest of the nuclear West, one bad afternoon from the doctrine's front door. Fairness will not move it. Verdicts will not move it—verdicts are for tribunals, and tribunals sit after wars, not instead of ending them. What moves it is two sides with stated positions reaching the settlement both can survive. A negotiator who reads the other side's doctrine, repeats its author's name without spitting, and reports the distance between the man's arsenal and the man's conduct is not a scandal. He is the only kind of negotiator who has ever ended a war like this one.

And under the arithmetic sits the wound itself. This war is a fratricide. Russians and Ukrainians share a language, a faith, and family lines that run across the border in both directions—brothers in opposite trenches, cousins on opposite sides of the artillery, grandmothers saying the same prayers in the same words for boys who are trying to kill each other. A civilization is spending its own sons on itself, at walking pace, while the powers arming the fight count the receipts from a safe distance. That is what the restraint at the top of the arsenal is holding back more of, and ending it is what the negotiators are actually for.

Putin has showed restraint. The sentence is arithmetic, not praise: the doctrine permits more than the war has seen, and everyone shouting at Kushner knows the numbers as well as he does. They are not angry that he said something false. They are angry that he said it out loud.

A friend of mine put up a post about vaccine choice this week. Parents choose for their children. Parents answer for the choice. Because America.

A woman in the thread asked the honest question. Should you be able to decline life-saving medical treatment for your children because it goes against your religion, or because you don't want to spend the money? Where is the line?

I answered her. Good question. Maybe they should become better at persuading people that their vaccines don't kill them.

A man in the thread answered me with data.

His data was good. Before routine childhood vaccination, nearly one child in five died before the age of five, most of them from infectious disease. Vaccines account for something like forty percent of the global decline in infant deaths since then. A hundred and fifty million children saved across fifty years. He had the numbers, and the numbers were the argument, and the argument was winning.

Then he wrote his last paragraph.

Again, it is not a matter of persuading, he said. It is a matter of uneducated dipsticks spreading misinformation about vaccines.

I told him the truth. You had them until the last paragraph.

Seventh grade

I came up in public relations. The first rule they drilled into us was a reading level. Write the copy at a seventh-grade reading ease. Everybody. Every audience. Every time.

The rule carried a discipline inside it. If the copy went over the reader's head, the copy had failed. You rewrote the copy. You sat back down and did your job again, simpler, clearer, until it landed.

Nobody in that business mailed the copy back to the reader with instructions. Nobody told the reader to grow a better brain first and come back when he was smarter.

You get the person in front of you. He arrives with the schooling he got, the town he grew up in, the people he trusts, the sleep he is missing. That is the whole of your material. Intelligence does not grow on command in the middle of an argument. It is the one thing about a person that cannot be traded, transferred, or improved by an effort of will—and the insult that demands it is the one insult that demands the impossible.

"Get smarter" is a surrender dressed as an argument. The speaker has quit the work of persuasion and started performing for the people who already agree with him.

The game is not only IQ. It is also culture, and it is framing. I have been reading a little Scott Adams lately, and Adams will tell you persuasion runs on framing and identity, and facts are the weakest tool in the box.

The man in the thread ran the play backwards. He led with the facts, his weakest tool, and closed by attacking the other side's identity, the strongest one. An attack on identity forfeits the frame. A forfeited frame ends the game, and there is nothing left to win with.

The asymmetry of grace

The grace gets handed out by a pattern, and the pattern is easy to see once it is pointed at.

Misinformation lives inside a protected community, and the educated response arrives with both arms full of context. The Black church gets its history. The mosque gets its history. A Latin American neighborhood, an Indian community, a Muslim community—each one gets institutions, memory, inherited distrust with footnotes, reasons reaching back generations. Addiction on a reservation gets handled with kid gloves, and rightly. The misinformation there gets read as a gap. A missing piece of context. Benevolent. Nobody in those rooms gets called an ignorant pig for believing what his grandmother believed.

Then the same species of misinformation shows up in a white working-class kitchen, and the context evaporates. No history. No institutions. No grandmother. A man stands in the frame fully formed, getting it wrong, and the verdict lands on his character in the first sentence. You fucking ignorant pig. Why don't you get smarter. Don't you understand science.

One man gets a backstory. The other gets a caption.

The courtesy in the first case is real. The question is why it stops where it stops.

Everybody is in a well

People tell the story of the frog in the well. The frog lives at the bottom, he looks up at his circle of sky, and he takes the circle for the whole of heaven. The story gets told as a warning about other people.

There are infinite frogs in infinite wells.

Nobody thinks he is a frog in a well. Everybody thinks he is a free man standing on open ground, the whole horizon in view. He is standing at the bottom of a different well. A larger one. The rim is wider, the circle of sky is bigger, and the bottom is the bottom.

You cannot know whether your entire life is being lived at the bottom of a well. And nobody, ever, thinks he is the one down there.

I have written the mirror version of this: nobody looks in a mirror and sees the villain, and the hero in the mirror is always smart. The well and the mirror are the same instrument. Both report a whole world. Both are showing you a circle.

Even the doubt is rarefied air. Cogito ergo sum—I think, therefore I am. Getting to that sentence at all, formulating doubt about your own frame while standing inside it, is advanced-level intelligence, the rarest move in philosophy. The scold's homework assignment, handed out with a sneer, is just become Descartes. Then the contempt comes down as the grade.

The man with the data this morning has a wider well than the people he was grading. A wider well is still a well. His circle of sky is bigger. It is a circle.

Some of the biggest radicals in history were extreme-IQ men. An extreme IQ doesn't free a man from his well. It means his framing is weaponized. Intelligence is horsepower. The well is the road he was handed. The smartest frog is the best-defended frog: more artillery for the circle of sky he started with, faster and more sophisticated rationalization of the position his culture and his raising gave him. Smart men do not climb out. They fortify.

And the weaponized frame travels. The radical does not only fortify his own well. He installs his circle of sky over other people's wells and recruits them into it.

The smartest people are always being groomed. Not in the creepy way. The cultivation starts early—pre-K, kindergarten, undergrad, the universities. The smart ones get picked out. They become favorites. Teachers invite them over for tea. They learn the finer things. They start meeting each other, cross-referencing each other, going to museums, reading the right books. Call it autodidactic cultural ascension.

The educated man's well was landscaped for him from childhood, and the landscaping is why he cannot see it. The tea, the museums, the canon—they are the well's interior decoration. And everyone inside the well was cultivated the same way, so their agreement with each other reads as verification. Consensus inside the well, mistaken for a view of the sky.

My well

I know the inside of a well. Mine was a house in Hawaii.

I grew up an only child there, a white kid with two white parents from New York and New Jersey. I lived in New York City until I was six. Then I was in Hawaii, and it felt like an abduction. We were lower middle class through the seventies and eighties, and there was never money to go visit the family. My cousins and uncles and grandmas and grandpas were three thousand and five thousand miles away, and they stayed there.

Both of my parents were alcoholics, and probably both narcissists. I had aphantasia and SDAM and ADHD, and nobody knew what any of that was. Nobody knew what aphantasia and SDAM were, and nobody really knew what ADHD was in the seventies either. There were no therapists. There was the house, and the two of them, and me.

That was my normal. I assumed everybody had that experience, because it was the only one I had ever seen from the inside.

Then I grew up and learned I cannot extrapolate from my experience at all. My normal was one house on an island. The man in the thread has a normal too. He is extrapolating from his at full volume, and he cannot hear himself doing it.

How the announcers used to do it

There was an era when this country talked to parents about their children on television, and it talked to them like adults who loved their kids.

"It's ten p.m. Do you know where your children are?" The line started as a local station announcement in New York, on WNEW, and it spread because it worked. It carried a little judgment. It carried a little dread. It left the parent his dignity and gave him something to do: get up, check, make the call.

The crib-death years ran on the same craft. The SIDS campaigns and the back-to-sleep message painted a scene and let the scene do the work. Your baby, asleep on his stomach. Your baby, in the morning. Nobody called the parents stupid. Nobody screamed at them for having children. The people who made those spots assumed the audience already loved their children, and they built the whole appeal on that assumption.

They persuaded millions of parents to change how a baby sleeps. Millions. With a picture and a quiet voice.

The counterfeit

Here is that same ten o'clock spot, remade by the modern method.

It's ten p.m. Do you know where your children are, you fucking pieces of shit? All you do is have children you can't afford. You're too stupid to feed them right. You won't vaccinate them because some dipshit on the internet scared you. Fuck you. Dum, dum—

The little station chime. Three clean notes. The announcer's voice comes back smooth as glass, bright as morning.

More news at eleven.

The viewer has learned everything about what the announcer thinks of him. He has forgotten to check on his kid.

I can't see the picture

I have aphantasia—the mind's eye I don't have, taken apart at episode length. When somebody says paint a picture, my head stays dark. I cannot call up a baby's face, living or dead, and no spot ever put one in front of me.

The scenarios land anyway. They land through sequence and consequence. It is ten. The kid is not home. You thought he was at a friend's house. You pick up the phone. Nothing in that needs an image. It needs a situation a person can stand inside.

That is the whole difference between the two methods on offer. One hands a person a situation he can grasp and lets the pressure build on its own. The other sprays him with abstractions—data, studies, percentages, contempt—and then rages when the abstractions slide off.

The man with the data this morning had the better facts and the worse method. His numbers deserved a better last paragraph.

Who the insult is for

The insult at the end of the data was never addressed to the people it named. They stopped reading at "dipsticks," the way any person stops reading at the moment he is called a name.

The insult was for the room. For the friends watching, the ones who already agreed, the ones who would laugh and share and say finally, somebody said it. The speaker had switched audiences mid-paragraph. He came to persuade a stranger and stayed to entertain his own side.

That is why the yelling feels like victory to the man doing it. His team cheered. The scoreboard he was watching said he won. The man he was supposedly talking to walked away confirmed in everything he already believed, including the belief that the other side hates him.

Contempt never persuaded anyone. It was never built to.

Persuasive people don't call people cunts at the end of their persuasion.

The most dangerous man in any country is the man who had a place in it and lost it.

Coriolanus Snow opens The Ballad of Songbirds and Snakes dropping a fistful of cabbage into boiling water and swearing it will never pass his lips again. The cabbage is dinner. He needs the bowl, and every drop of the broth, so his stomach stays quiet through the reaping ceremony, so nobody at the Academy hears the heir to the house of Snow growl with hunger in public. The Snows live in the penthouse of the Capitol's most opulent apartment building. Rooms stand empty behind closed doors because the furniture went for food during the war and after it. His shirt for the ceremony is his dead father's, yellowed, half the buttons gone, a cigarette burn on the cuff, saved only because his cousin Tigris can sew. His grandmother keeps roses on the roof and sings the anthem out the window at a city that stopped listening years ago.

The family money is gone in a specific way, and the specificity matters. The bulk of the Snow fortune sat in munitions in District 13—factories, research facilities, blocks of plant—and District 13 was bombed to dust in the rebellion, or so the Capitol believes. His father Crassus Snow, a general, took a rebel bullet while overseeing that business in person. His mother died in childbirth soon after. An eighteen-year-old boy is left with a name, a penthouse he cannot heat, a cousin, a grandmother, and a motto the family repeats the way other families say grace: Snow lands on top.

Suzanne Collins gave him that first name on purpose. Coriolanus is Shakespeare's patrician, Caius Marcius, the Roman soldier who took Corioles almost single-handed and got the honor name for it, twenty-seven wounds on his body by his mother's proud count. Rome wanted to make him consul. Custom required the candidate to stand in the marketplace in a gown of humility, show his wounds to the plebeians, and ask for their voices in the plain style of a beggar asking for alms. Coriolanus could kill Volscians all day. He could not do that. He showed the wounds under duress, hid most of them, cursed the crowd in the same breath he used to solicit it, called the people dissentious rogues and scabs, and told Rome to its face that giving the common herd a share in power had been the original rot. The tribunes worked the crowd, the crowd turned, and Rome banished its greatest soldier. He went to his enemy Aufidius, offered his throat or his sword, and came back at the head of a Volscian army to collect the debt in fire. His mother Volumnia walked out of the gates and talked him down on her knees. He spared the city and signed his own death warrant with the Volscians in the same hour.

The wound in both men is identical, and the wound has a shape. A peasant with nothing has a grievance against the world in general, which is weather. A rich man secure in his place has an interest in order, which is ballast. The fallen aristocrat has a creditor's ledger with a name on every line. Somebody specific did this. The rebels killed the father. The district scum bombed the factories. The plebeians voted the exile. The new men—the Plinths in Collins's book, munitions money out of District 2, buying their way into Capitol society with a fortune made in the same war that bankrupted the Snows—take the seats at the table that had the old names carved into them. Humiliation with an address is a different substance from poverty. Poverty can be endured, outworked, outlived. Humiliation by replacement demands an answer.

History keeps the type in stock.

Japan abolished the samurai in the 1870s, and it abolished them by paperwork—stipends commuted, then cut, swords banned in public, the hereditary warrior caste of six centuries dissolved into commoners with good posture and no income. Saigō Takamori had helped make the Meiji Restoration itself; he was one of its senior architects, a lord of Satsuma, a man of the old order who had handed the new order its victory. In 1877 he led the Satsuma Rebellion anyway, tens of thousands of disaffected former samurai marching out of Kagoshima against the conscript army of the government he had built, an army of farmers' sons with rifles, which was precisely the insult made flesh. The rebellion ran from January to September and ended at Shiroyama with Saigō shot and dead by his own side's hand rather than captured. The conscripts won. The class that had carried Japan's swords for six hundred years died charging the class that had replaced it, led by a man whose complaint was never really a wage. The stipends could have been negotiated. The station could not be refunded.

Germany in 1918 lost an empire, a Kaiser, an army, and an officer corps's entire reason for existing in about six weeks. The Freikorps filled the hole—volunteer paramilitary corps, more than sixty-five of them by the standard counts, hundreds of thousands of men under arms in one form or another, led by ex-officers, staffed by demobilized soldiers and students too young to have fought and furious at the theft of their turn. The Weimar government used them to crush the Spartacists and the Bavarian Soviet, then discovered it had rented an army that despised the landlord. Freikorps men murdered Foreign Minister Walther Rathenau in 1922. Freikorps commanders and veterans drained in a steady stream into the Nazi SA and SS; Ernst Röhm ran a Freikorps and then ran the Brownshirts. The stab-in-the-back legend was the Freikorps creed in one sentence: the army never lost, it was sold. A defeat can be accepted, mourned, studied. A theft must be avenged, and the avenging has no natural stopping point, because the thing stolen was a world.

The American version wore gray and called itself the Lost Cause. The planter class of the South lost a war, an enslaved labor force it counted as wealth, and a social order in which its name had been law in the county for generations. What it built afterward was a religion of the loss itself—monuments in courthouse squares, textbooks vetted by widows' associations, the marble Lee serene above it all, the war re-described for a century as a noble defense conducted by gentlemen against overwhelming numbers, with the slavery at the center of it edited down to a footnote in someone else's hand. The grievance got passed down like land. Grandsons who had never owned anything but the story policed it as fiercely as their grandfathers had policed the property, because the story was the property that remained.

The type travels well because exile gives it a permanent address abroad. Romanov pretenders spent the twentieth century advancing claims to a throne that had ended in a basement in Ekaterinburg, grand dukes and grand duchesses and outright impostors, Anna Anderson the most famous, each claim unfalsifiable in the heart that held it. Cuban exiles in Miami kept the deeds, the keys, and the inventories of confiscated houses and businesses for generations after 1959, some of those claims eventually written into American law, all of them waiting on a political event to become collectible. Iranian monarchists still style a crown prince for a throne abolished in 1979, still speak of restoration in the present tense, nearly half a century gone. Time does nothing to these claims. Time is what they feed on.

Debts come in two kinds.

A finite debt can be written down. Maria Altmann's family owned paintings by Gustav Klimt, including the gold portrait of her aunt Adele Bloch-Bauer, and the Nazis took them, and after the war they hung in Austria's Galerie Belvedere behind a false story about a bequest. Altmann sued in California, and her case went to the Supreme Court of the United States, which ruled in 2004 in Republic of Austria v. Altmann that Austria had no sovereign immunity to hide behind. The parties went to arbitration in Austria, the panel ruled for Altmann in January 2006, Austria handed over five Klimt paintings, she sold them, and the case ended. Ended. The Swiss banks case ended the same way, in the same decade, at a larger scale: class actions over dormant accounts and looted assets, a settlement in August 1998 for $1.25 billion, a distribution plan approved by Judge Korman, money paid out to hundreds of thousands of claimants, releases signed, files closed. However bitter the road, a painting has a wall it came from and a wall it returns to. An account has a balance. A balance can be paid, and payment is a full stop.

An infinite debt has no balance. Nobody can write a check for you were supposed to be consul. No settlement conference returns a caste, a throne's aura, a county where your grandfather's name opened the bank, an officer's mess in an empire that no longer exists on any map. The thing claimed is a station in the world—a rank in an order of being—and stations are paid in one currency only, which is dominion over the people who took them or replaced them or merely failed to kneel. Coriolanus at the gates of Rome with a Volscian army had been offered everything a negotiation could offer. Rome would have taken him back. He came for the collection, and the collection required the city kneeling or burning, and either would have done. Snow, given sixty years and absolute power, is still collecting at the end of it—poisoning his own allies as a career habit, staging the Hunger Games every year as an annual receipt, the districts made to hand over their children in a ceremony designed in his youth, by his class, for the permanent rehearsal of who had won and who had knelt. The Games are a debt instrument. They never mature.

The settlement table gives the surest proof of it. Put money in front of the fallen man and he refuses it, or he takes it and keeps the grievance at full strength, undiminished by the payment. The grievance is the last asset of the house, the one holding that still yields interest, and a proud man does not sell his final asset at any price a court can name. He would rather keep the wound open and legible, the way Coriolanus kept his twenty-seven scars—shown only on his own terms, never as a beggar shows them, produced as evidence in a trial he intends to win by arms.

Fiction, which can run the experiment cleanly, set the same fork in front of two men with the same wound and watched them split. Charles Xavier and Erik Lehnsherr begin in the same place—a gifted people, hated for existing, marked for cages—and Magneto carries the wound in its literal form, a survivor of the camps, a boy who watched the world's actual machinery for sorting humans into kinds. Xavier builds a school. He spends his power and his fortune hiding his people, training them, sending them out to protect the very world that hates and fears them, absorbing insult as a cost of the work, betting everything on repair across a timescale longer than his own life. Magneto builds a brotherhood, then an asteroid, then an island, each iteration the same fortress with better weapons, and spends his power settling accounts—never again, enforced at gunpoint, forever, by him. One man treats the wound as a job. The other treats it as a title deed. The stories have run for sixty years and the fork has never once closed, because both answers are permanently available to every injured people and every injured man, and the choosing is the character.

Snow chose. Saigō chose, and Röhm chose, and the widows with their textbook committees chose in their own register, and the pretenders in their Paris apartments and Miami living rooms are choosing still, every year the claim gets renewed like a lease on a house that burned down before they were born. The crown was never really the object. A crown can be minted again, bought back, reissued by a grateful parliament. The dangerous man lost something with no mint and no market, and he knows the exact names of everyone living in his place, and he is patient, and he is proud, and he lands on top or he dies at Shiroyama or at the Volscian camp still collecting.

There is no settlement. There is only the man, deciding what the wound is for.

The Hunger Games strand of this piece traces back to Isabel Brown's "I Don't Think We Read the Same Hunger Games" on The Isabel Brown Show, posted November 19. Brown was new to me—the episode was the first of hers I had seen—and her point was that Collins aimed the books at centralized power itself, and that the camps now fighting over them are reading different stories under one title. Snow's endless collection makes more sense inside her reading than inside the fascism label, and the debt of the observation is acknowledged here. Her book is The End of the Alphabet: How Gen Z Can Save America.

People keep asking Bridget Phetasy how she got so radicalized. Her latest Dumpster Fire, Latchkey Kids vs. Communism, opens with those messages. Bridget, calm down. Was there a radicalizing event?

Her answer is Charlie Kirk. He was shot in the neck on a livestream, and she plays the clips of what came after: people dancing on TikTok, calling it poetic justice, promising to celebrate that night. Watching millions of people mock a man assassinated for how he voted is radicalizing, she says, and since she votes the way he did, it told her how many people would dance for her.

Kirk is my story too. For years I knew him only from memes mocking his face, fifteen-second gotcha clips cut without context, and I believed them because they came from inside my own room. The day he was killed, I bought a black shirt and hoodie with FREEDOM across the chest in white letters. Then I gave him the attention I never gave him while he was alive. I watched hundreds of hours of his unedited footage: full debates, full question lines, full shows.

He never yelled. He sat at a folding table on campuses full of students who hated him and asked the same calm questions over and over, in a clinical, repetitive, debate-stage cadence he applied to sin and salvation. Anyone can dismiss a man ranting. A man who calmly asks the same question until it becomes unbearable is much harder to dismiss. Athens killed Socrates for refusing to stop asking.

His theology was old and plain, and it applied to everyone at once. Every sexual act outside the marriage of one man and one woman misses the mark, the straight kid's hookup as much as the gay kid's. Sin in the Greek is an archery term, hamartia, missing the mark, and every Christian is expected to say "I am a sinner" about himself first. The invitation was universal: repent, and go and sin no more. He would call homosexuality a sin on stage and hug gay students when the debate was over, and in his world both were the same act of love. He wanted the conversation. He wanted ideas tested out loud in front of the people who disagreed with him most. He came to talk, and he kept coming back to talk.

What he kept returning to was the Word. The Red Letters. He told people to read what Jesus actually said. I was baptized Catholic, became an Episcopalian, and served on the vestry of St. James' from 1998 through 2007. My super-Catholic friend Carrie told me recently that in all those years I never had much zeal, and she's right. I knew how to inhabit a church. Charlie made me want to know Jesus himself. In the year since he died I have become quite the Jesus freak, and he had a lot to do with it.

The reaction to his death showed me two wars. The left's vocabulary is material: power, systems, oppression, words as violence. In that vocabulary, misgendering is violence and a bullet through a man's throat is justice. The right's vocabulary is spiritual: the Enemy with a capital E, spiritual warfare, martyrdom in the oldest Christian sense. Martys means witness, the one who is killed for the truth and never kills for it. Both sides watched the same video and saw two different wars. The left showed me what it would cheer for, and I knew which side I was on.

Bridget says she is exactly who she was before. One of her commenters said it best: she hasn't evolved, she has stayed the same and the environment changed around her. Then she says the line that is my whole life: the center-right is what Clinton Democrats used to be.

I am the Clinton Democrat. New York City parents, raised a haole kid in Hawaii, the bluest state in the union. American Literature at GW, a year at the University of East Anglia, seminars in African-American literature and feminist theory, a stint teaching creative writing. My first vote went to whoever wasn't George H.W. Bush. On election night 1992 I ran from my dorm to the White House, practically next door, and sang "Na Na Hey Hey Kiss Him Goodbye" at the lawn. I voted for Clinton. I voted for Obama twice, the first time from Berlin, celebrating in the streets. I have lived in DC and Arlington since 1988 and never once in a red place.

Now I am told I am on the far right. I was radical in the Clinton days. Today I am somewhere to the right of Attila the Hun, and I never packed a bag.

I hold the positions I held in that dorm room. Free speech, all of it, especially the ugly kind. Contempt for the fantasy that America can bomb people into democracy. In 2005 I sided with Pat Buchanan's foreign policy against Natan Sharansky on my blog, quoting John Quincy Adams: America does not go abroad in search of monsters to destroy. That was a left position once. My conservative roommate at GW took me to Republican parties full of square, nerdy kids dressed like Gordon Gekko. Those kids are the same people today. Conservatives held still.

I missed the Overton window the way you miss a bus. I stood at the stop, exactly where the schedule said to stand, and it pulled away without me. Everyone on board looks out the back window, sees me shrinking on the curb, and decides I am the one who went somewhere. The window slides left only inside a clean, safe terrarium, a society rich and secure enough to forget what the glass is for. Collapse the terrarium for twenty years and everything snaps back to kill or be killed.

Bridget voted for Trump as a protest vote, hoping a win would nudge the left toward the middle. He won, popular vote and all. The left answered her nudge by asking why nobody had shot her yet, decided the lesson was to go further left, and Europe caught fire. She will not give them an inch now.

In the summer of 2024 she sat at dinner parties in polite company and listened to people pass the brie and say it was too bad the shooter missed Trump, on the day a volunteer firefighter named Corey Comperatore died shielding his family. I know those dinner parties. I have been going to them in this town for thirty-six years, with the same insufferable rich friends from college who kept me from voting for Hillary.

The people who loved her for being politically homeless are furious she found a home. She doubts the center ever existed. The real center, she says, was always the people who hated every zealot, and it vanished because the left kept running, decided there is no such thing as too far, and forced everyone standing still to pick a side.

She voted for Ken Paxton. She wants guns and she wants progressives to leave the kids alone, and she calls that being a grown-up: weighing trade-offs against what you value and voting for whichever politician gets you closest. She leans on Milton Friedman: electing the right people won't save you; making it politically profitable for the wrong people to do the right things will. That is my rule too. I vote for the enemy of my enemy. On guns, party means nothing to me. I vote against anyone with an anti-gun record and for anyone with a long record of friendliness to the Second Amendment. I have lived in Washington since 1988 and never believed national politics is anything but professional wrestling.

A New York Times poll shows every generation moved left over the last two years except Gen X. Bridget's explanation is that we were neglected. We were latchkey kids. Our parents divorced and forgot we existed, we bullied each other, and we came out resilient. Gen X believes that helping somebody too much takes something from him, and every handout costs the receiver the thing he would have built himself. I was a haole kid in Honolulu at an all-boys Catholic school from 1983 to 1988. Nobody held an assembly about it. I learned to take a hit. I got to GW in the fall of 1988, just as political correctness hit American campuses: Stanford fighting over the Western canon, Michigan writing a speech code, speech codes and sensitivity rules spreading school to school. Every Gen Xer I knew laughed at it. A whole generation of Gen X stand-up comics built their acts on mocking it. We met political correctness as adults and treated it as a joke. The kids behind us were raised inside the anti-bullying movement from kindergarten, where words count as harm and an adult always steps in. They got to college already believing it. We were the last ones raised by nobody, and it shows in how we vote.

Her prescription for the kids is to have children, buy a house, and see the first tax bill. Nothing turns a socialist into a libertarian faster than the FICA line on a paycheck. The housing crisis is the real emergency, because people locked out of homes have nothing left to defend. Give a man a thirty-year mortgage and a backyard and he will want a grill instead of a revolution. People with something to lose defend the glass.

Mostly, she says, she just wants safe cities. So do I. Law and order is the whole ballgame. Santa Monica's Third Street emptied after the BLM riots, and the mall still sits empty six years later. Every city that defunded its police, gutted bail, and declared itself a sanctuary survived only because it sits inside an ocean of law-and-order counties and states doing the work of deterrence everywhere else. It is herd immunity for civilization, and those cities free-ride on a national immune system they refuse to credit.

Nobody announces that the center has moved. They relabel whoever didn't move with it. Yesterday's liberal is today's centrist and tomorrow's extremist, and the person never said a new thing. The free speech absolutist becomes a hate speech apologist. The antiwar guy becomes a Putin stooge. The guy who wanted government out of his bedroom and his wallet becomes a threat to democracy for wanting it out of his gun safe too.

The one place I did move is the nanny state. I never said those words until I started seeing it everywhere. I spent a year in Norwich working the door at the LCR, and the English are a gentle, orderly people who need little minding, yet their government went after kitchen knives anyway. We give preschoolers safety scissors in America. Britain got there with adults. The nanny state runs on one idea: that you deserve things for simply existing on the Earth. You don't. The ocean owes you nothing. The only baseline is that nobody dies in the street. Past that, you contribute.

Rights are protections, granted by God, and government's only job with them is to keep its hands off. A want dressed as a right is a demand on government, and once every want is called a right, nobody is allowed to argue with it. Your want for a guarantee always lands on my protection.

None of this comes from gun culture. My parents never owned a gun. My father was a photographer, so we had cameras where other families had rifles, and underwater cameras where other families had spearguns. Nobody in my family hunted. We barely fished. We photographed the men who fished for billfish. I lived happily without a gun in Norwich, in Berlin, and in DC. I bought my first one at forty, after I moved to Virginia. I vote on the Second Amendment because the First has already fallen. The First Amendment was the canary in the coal mine, and it keeled over the day "hate speech" became a category. Now everyone claims to support free speech while carving out the exact speech that would prove they mean it, and nobody ever needed a constitution to protect the nice kind. The Second Amendment is the turkey vulture in the coal mine. It is a far tougher bird, and it is the one still standing down there. When everything else has been stripped away and every choice has been taken out of your hands, the gun is the last thing that says you are still responsible for yourself. Benjamin Franklin wrote it in 1755: those who would give up essential liberty to purchase a little temporary safety deserve neither liberty nor safety.

When Virginia passed its assault weapons ban this spring, I wrote checks, sent emails, sent faxes, and joined the groups fighting it. It was the first time government ever reached into my life directly, and it came from the party I voted for my entire adult life.

I am the same kid who drew anarchist circle-As on his binders and listened to the Dead Kennedys. I am the same kid who ran to the White House to sing Clinton in. Every one of those kids would recognize me. My party wouldn't recognize any of them.

So call Bridget an extremist. Call me one. Call Adam Curry one, my fellow Gen Xer and the Podfather I have listened to for nineteen years. We stood at the stop where we were told to stand, and the bus left without us.

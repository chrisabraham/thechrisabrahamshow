Plutarch Heavensbee explains the country's name to Katniss in Mockingjay. Panem comes from panem et circenses, bread and circuses, Juvenal's phrase in his tenth Satire for a Roman people that had surrendered its power and anxiously wanted two things only. Collins took the name and split the bargain down the middle. The Capitol received the bread and the circuses together, in excess, forever. The districts received the bill.

Juvenal's Rome at least fed its mob. The grain dole was real policy, and the games were real entertainment, and the citizen who took both had traded his vote for a full stomach and a seat at the spectacle. Panem's districts stand outside that trade entirely. District 11 grows the food and gets punished for eating it. District 12 mines the coal and trades tesserae for grain—a meager year's supply of grain and oil for one person, bought by adding a child's name to the reaping one more time, the entries cumulative year after year. The circus reaches the districts as compulsory viewing of their own children killing each other. Bread and circuses, as a system, requires the rulers to share something. Snow's Capitol shares the show and keeps the bread.

The banquet in Catching Fire shows where the bread went. The Victory Tour ends at President Snow's mansion with tables along the walls—whole roasted animals on spits, platters of fowl, ocean creatures in sauces, countless cheeses, breads, vegetables, sweets, waterfalls of wine. Katniss and Peeta eat until they can hold no more, a condition they have rarely met in their lives, and Octavia of the prep team offers them small stemmed glasses of clear liquid. The liquid makes a guest vomit, so the guest can return to the tables and keep eating. Everybody at a Capitol party does it, Octavia explains, or how could anyone have any fun at parties. Peeta walks Katniss away from the tables thinking of the emaciated children in District 12 whom her mother treats and cannot cure, because the cure is food. The Capitol built a technology for appetite without limit in a country where children starve in safety, and it serves the technology in stemware, as hospitality.

Bread in the districts moves hand to hand, at cost, in the rain. Peeta Mellark is eleven years old, the baker's youngest son, in the winter after Katniss's father dies in the mine and her mother sinks into a silence that feeds nobody. Katniss, also eleven, is starving with Prim, digging through the bakery trash bins and finding them bare, shouted away by the baker's wife, collapsed under the apple tree by the pig pen in the freezing rain. Peeta burns two loaves of bread in the oven—deliberately, the text leaves Katniss certain of it in retrospect—and his mother beats him for the clumsiness, a red welt rising on his cheek, and orders the ruined loaves thrown to the pigs. He throws them to Katniss instead, at her feet in the mud, and says nothing, then or ever. The bread carries the Everdeens through to the first dandelion of spring, which reminds Katniss what her father taught her about gathering, and the family survives on her work from that day. A loaf from the Capitol's side of the counter, given by a boy who paid for it with a beating and never presented a bill.

District 11 answers Rue's death with the same currency. Rue, twelve years old, from the orchards, is speared in the arena after Katniss frees her from a net, and Katniss sings her the lullaby from home and covers her body with wildflowers so the cameras must carry the flowers too. That evening a silver parachute descends with a small loaf—dark ration grain, crescent-shaped, sprinkled with seeds, still warm. Katniss holds it and does the arithmetic out loud in her head: what must it have cost the people of District 11, who cannot feed themselves, to pool enough to send bread to another district's tribute. It is the first time in the history of the Games that a district has sent a gift to a tribute outside its own. The loaf was likely meant for Rue. They sent it to her killer's opponent and her friend anyway, in thanks, across the lines the Capitol draws to keep the districts strangers to each other. Solidarity in Panem arrives as bread, because bread is the one thing its givers can least spare.

Mark's Gospel gives the measuring instrument for both loaves. Jesus sits opposite the treasury in the Temple and watches the crowd put money in—rich people contributing large sums, one after another—and a poor widow comes and puts in two small copper coins, worth a penny. He calls his disciples over for the verdict: this poor widow has put in more than all the others, for they contributed out of their abundance, and she, out of her poverty, put in everything she had, all she had to live on. Heaven's accounting runs on cost to the giver. A gift measured by its size flatters the rich man whose surplus never touches his dinner. A gift measured by its cost finds the widow, the baker's boy, and the orchard district, and it finds them giving out of the substance they needed to live.

I have sat with that passage for years, and it keeps refusing to behave as a comfortable text. The comfortable reading praises generosity in general and lets a man give from his overflow and feel righteous about the percentage. The widow closes that road. Her two coins are nothing in the treasury's ledger and everything in hers, and Jesus counts by her ledger. Peeta's loaves were scorched goods headed for the pigs, worthless on his father's shelf, and they cost him a welt, a black eye by the next morning at school, and the risk of worse from a mother the books never soften. District 11's crescent loaf cost people who pick food under guard and get punished for eating it. The Capitol's banquet cost its guests nothing—they had engineered even the body's limit out of the transaction, vomiting so the feast could continue past fullness. Three breads, one instrument. The measure is what the giver had left afterward.

Coriolanus Snow stands at the far end of that measure, and The Ballad of Songbirds and Snakes shows the forging of him. The prequel opens on an eighteen-year-old heir in a penthouse in the Capitol's most opulent building, dropping a fistful of cabbage into boiling water and swearing that one day it will never pass his lips again. The Snows besieged during the first rebellion lost everything but the name and the address. His father died in the war. His mother died in childbirth, unable to reach a hospital through the siege, and the baby died with her. The family lived on cabbage and beans behind a grand facade, masking ruin with manners, the boy taking a long list of precautions so no one at the Academy would smell the poverty on him. Hunger taught Coriolanus Snow its lesson thoroughly, and the lesson was hoarding. What he had almost lost, he would hold. What he had almost been—poor, powerless, district scum in his own private phrase for his family's condition—he would never be again, and Panem would pay the premium on that vow for sixty-four years.

The widow and the boy in the penthouse had the same teacher and learned opposite catechisms. She had almost nothing and released all of it. He had almost nothing and closed his hand around it, and the closed hand became a governing philosophy—control, order, the Games tightened and perfected, a country arranged so that Coriolanus Snow would never again watch cabbage boil and call it dinner. His cruelty in the trilogy carries that origin in it: a man who knew exactly what hunger does to people, because it had done it to him, and who therefore used hunger as his chosen instrument, rationing the districts by tesserae and terror because he understood the weapon from the inside. Knowledge of suffering is no guarantee of mercy. It can tutor a giver, and it can tutor a warden.

Bread in Panem tells the truth about every hand that touches it. The Capitol throws bread up and calls the throwing a party. Peeta throws bread across a muddy yard and calls it nothing, for the rest of his life. District 11 sends bread into an arena it is forced to watch, for a girl from another district who sang to theirs. The widow put in everything she had, all she had to live on, and Jesus said it outweighed the treasury. Collins named her country for bread, and the bread—given, hoarded, vomited, shared—renders the verdict on everyone in it.

A credit before the loaf cools: this morning's run through Panem started with Isabel Brown's Hunger Games episode on The Isabel Brown Show—a program new to me that morning—where she read the trilogy as a warning against centralized government and named the obvious—the people fighting over these books did not read the same books. She wrote the argument out long-form in her book, The End of the Alphabet: How Gen Z Can Save America. The bread argument is mine. The morning was hers.

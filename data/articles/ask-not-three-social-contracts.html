America's social contract comes in three versions now, and they share one sentence. On January 20, 1961, John F. Kennedy gave the terms as his generation received them, at the close of his inaugural address: "And so, my fellow Americans: ask not what your country can do for you—ask what you can do for your country." The sentence pointed from the citizen toward the country. One modern contract has reversed the direction and calls the reversal justice. A second contract keeps Kennedy's direction and calls it freedom. A third never heard the sentence as a question at all. I hold the Kennedy contract, the one I was raised on. The other two get their full strength first, in their own terms, because a verdict against straw men is worth nothing.

The contract of provision

The first contract reads Kennedy's sentence backward and means it. In this telling, a citizen owes the country participation, taxes, and lawfulness, and the country owes the citizen the floor that makes participation real: health care when sickness comes, schooling that opens doors, labor standards that keep a full week's work ahead of poverty, and insurance shared across the whole population against the disasters no family can carry alone. Taxes, in this contract, are citizens carrying one another through the state. The state is the instrument neighbors use at national scale.

The argument underneath concerns what citizenship requires. A man one diagnosis from ruin deliberates with a gun at his head. A mother choosing between rent and medicine votes her fear. Provision, in this telling, buys the calm that self-government runs on, and it stands as the price a wealthy republic pays for the consent of the people at the bottom of it. The contract's proudest exhibits are old. In 1935, with the Depression standing in every breadline, the Social Security Act moved old age and unemployment from the family's kitchen table to a national insurance pool. In 1965, Medicare and Medicaid extended the same logic to sickness in old age and in poverty. Each expansion arrived as an emergency measure and stayed as a fixture of the house.

The provision wing claims Kennedy as a founder, and the claim has evidence behind it. His sentence, in their reading, named the second half of a two-part pledge: the same presidency sent the Peace Corps abroad and pressed Congress for medical care for the old at home. Service and provision, in this telling, are gears on one shaft. A citizen secured against ruin has something to give. A citizen drowning has only needs. The contract of provision calls itself the enabling act of the Kennedy contract, the foundation pour that precedes the house of duty.

The contract of service

The second contract keeps Kennedy's direction and radicalizes it. The citizen is the country. Democracy is the manifestation of the people who show up: the family that holds, the congregation that feeds its own street, the town that paves its road and staffs its fire company. Success in this contract is emergent. It rises from the ground, from millions of households building, hiring, saving, and raising children equipped to do the same. Government has real work in this telling: defend the borders, keep the courts honest, break up whatever rigs the game, and then stand back. A strong country is the byproduct of strong citizens, and the order of those two clauses is the whole doctrine.

Inside this family sits a louder strand, the hegemon argument. It runs from the top. America holds the seas, the dollar, and the alliance system, and a country that strong generates prosperity in such volume that it reaches every kitchen in the republic: national success first, household success downstream. The ground-up wing and the hegemon wing argue about direction at every family gathering. They close ranks on the shared clause. Washington's provision is a charge against the citizen's own building, and every program that arrives as help arrives with a manager attached.

The populist edge of this family sharpens the point. A benefit administered by strangers arrives with forms, audits, and permissions, and a man who must request permission to feed his family has traded standing for groceries. The populist contract pays dignity in the older currency: a paycheck no clerk approved, a church basement that needs no grant, a neighbor's help that creates a debt between equals and gets repaid in kind. Help flowing horizontally, in this telling, builds the country. Help flowing downward builds the agency.

The normie contract

The third contract has no theorist and the largest population. The normie American holds no doctrine about the direction of Kennedy's sentence. He pays his taxes in April, obeys the laws he knows about, works the job he has, and expects the other side of the deal to perform: the roads paved, the police answering, the school open, the Social Security statement accurate, the hospital bill survivable, and help present during the worst week of his life. He received this contract the way he received his name. It was already running when he arrived.

Both preachers misjudge this population. The provision wing reads the normie's silence as unmet need awaiting a program. The service wing reads the same silence as virtue awaiting a summons. The field record shows something plainer. The normie judges the contract by performance, the way a tenant judges a landlord. Keep the building standing and the water hot, and politics stays a spectator sport in that household. Let the building rot, and the normie goes shopping for a new landlord, carrying an anger the theorists keep mistaking for ideology.

His enforcement mechanism is the election, used the way a customer uses a complaint desk. Incumbents of both parties fall in waves that analysts strain to read as philosophy. The normie was conducting a performance review. The two contracts with doctrines keep drafting platforms for a man who is reading the maintenance log.

How the terms got rewritten

The normie contract has a history, and the history explains its terms. Before the 1930s, risk lived at home: old age, sickness, and job loss landed on the family and the local charity, and landed hard. The New Deal nationalized the catastrophic end of that risk. The postwar decades split the remainder. Employers carried pensions and health insurance as standard equipment of a serious job. The government carried Social Security, the GI Bill's ladder into college classrooms and mortgages, and a highway system that made the suburbs rational. A man hearing Kennedy's sentence in 1961 stood on that layered floor, and he heard a summons he could afford to answer.

The layers have thinned since. The market turn of the 1980s pushed risk back toward the individual: the pension became a 401(k) with a balance that rises and falls with the market, and coverage acquired deductibles sized like rent payments. The newest pressure arrives from the machines. Social Security and Medicare draw their funding from payroll taxes, a levy on human paychecks. Artificial intelligence raises output while shrinking the payroll underneath it. A contract financed by wages, in an economy learning to produce without them, carries an arithmetic problem that no camp's slogan has answered.

The reversal Kennedy's sentence was built to refuse now campaigns in the open. The modern pitch, from the provision side's populists and the service side's champions alike, leads with what the country will do: the check, the forgiveness, the protection, the benefit with a name attached. Each camp denounces the other's promises and keeps its own. A citizen addressed as a customer in every speech learns the customer's posture, and a republic of customers runs short of people willing to answer Kennedy's question in the direction he asked it.

The direction of the sentence

The provision contract makes the citizen a client, and clients of a state learn to file claims where their grandfathers made contributions. The normie contract makes the citizen a tenant, and tenants keep a building exactly as long as the landlord performs. The Kennedy contract makes the citizen an author. It assigns the country to the people who compose it, and it pays its holders in the one currency that compounds: the knowledge that the thing standing is standing because they stood it up.

The sentence has survived sixty-five years of quotation because every generation hears its own direction inside it. Mine runs from the citizen toward the country, the way Kennedy aimed it on a cold morning in 1961. Ask what you can do for your country. The country that sentence builds in return is the kind a man can be proud to owe.

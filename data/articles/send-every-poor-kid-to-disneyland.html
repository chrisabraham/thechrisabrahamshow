The United States should put every poor child in the country on a bus to Disneyland, at public expense, before he turns twelve. As policy—universal, boring, administered the way we pave roads and stock libraries, a standing provision that asks nothing of the child first. A child cannot earn a childhood. He can only have one or miss one, and the missing is permanent.

The objection arrives on schedule, and it is the cost. The cost objection assumes July. Nobody is proposing July. The proposal is February, on a Tuesday, in the rain if necessary—on the days the parks run at half tide and the cars cycle out of the station empty past empty ropes. A roller coaster seat that leaves unfilled is a perishable good, like an airline seat at takeoff or a hotel room at midnight: unsold, it does not go into storage. It evaporates. The country already manufactures the wonder and throws a large share of it away on the off days. The proposal is to stop throwing it away and put a child in it. The parks confess the arithmetic themselves: their own ticket tiers charge the least for exactly the days in question—same castle, same rides, lower price—because an empty Tuesday is worth less to them than a full Saturday. The proposal takes them at their word.

The machine is not new. It is an old one that used to run in public, on exactly this principle. When I was a kid in public school, the schools ran matinee days: the whole grade bussed to the symphony on a weekday afternoon, tickets paid, on precisely the days the subscribers stayed home. The orchestra played to a full hall of children in school clothes instead of three hundred empty seats. The musicians got an audience. The hall got a purpose for its dead hours. We got Dvořák at age ten, filed away next to nothing else we owned. Nobody called it welfare. It was a city spending its own idle capacity on its own children, and the arithmetic was not close.

The machine also has a name and a founding date. In 1877, the Reverend Willard Parsons, newly arrived at a small parish in Sherman, Pennsylvania, asked his congregation to take New York City tenement children into their homes for two weeks of country air. The Fresh Air Fund has been running ever since—nearly a hundred and fifty years, more than 1.8 million children, volunteer host families across thirteen states and Canada, and its own camps on 2,300 acres at Fishkill, New York, sixty-five miles up the Hudson from the neighborhoods the children come from. Mariah Carey put her name on one of them. Camp Mariah, the Fund's career-awareness camp, takes a sixth grader for three years of summers and school-year weekends—tutoring, job shadowing, camping—aimed at a single piece of information: that a working life is a place you can see yourself standing before anyone will pay you to stand there. The Fund does all of this on donations, passing the hat for the most obviously correct proposition in American childhood, which is that a poor kid should, at least once, be somewhere else.

The Fund's original instrument was a spare bed. The Friendly Towns program still runs on it: a child from the five boroughs spends two weeks inside another family's ordinary life—at their table, on their chores, in their lake, watching how a household with margin actually moves through a day. No curriculum survives contact with that. The child is not lectured about the other America. He is fed by it, teased by its children, put to bed in a room of his own, and left to do the math himself.

Scale it, then, and stop apologizing for the scale. Summer camp for every poor child, as a standing institution rather than a charity lottery with a few hundred bunks. The theme parks on their dead Tuesdays. And the full extension, stated plainly, because the logic does not stop at the water's edge: Europe. A week in Paris or Rome at eleven years old, on the government's dime, in the off season, in the cheap seats. A child who has stood in the Sistine Chapel, or gotten lost—briefly, safely—in the Métro, has received information no classroom in his zip code can transmit. The world is large. It is old. It is beautiful, and it is not closed to him. He spends the rest of his schooling in a building he now understands to be a doorway instead of a wall.

The parents do not come. This is the clause that ends dinner parties, so it gets stated with the care it deserves. Nobody is seized. No custody changes hands. No court is involved, no social worker knocks, and the child goes home on Sunday with photographs, a sunburn, and a story his parents could not have given him, because nobody ever gave it to them. A parent who has never seen the other America cannot show it to his child. That is not an indictment of the parent. Love is not the same as range. A mother can love her son completely inside a four-block world and still have no way to hand him the fifth block. The trip does what she cannot, and it does it without pretending otherwise.

The child also knows who sent him. There is no Santa Claus machinery in this proposal, no fiction about a generous stranger. The state says it in the open: your country is bigger than your street, and we are showing it to you because it is yours too. That knowledge is the payload. The rides are the wrapping. A boy who has eaten breakfast inside the castle comes home carrying an appetite nobody has to manufacture for him afterward, and an appetite of that kind is the beginning of every escape ever made from a poor neighborhood. The appetite is a settled conviction that the other life is visitable, and therefore reachable, and therefore worth the arithmetic of reaching.

I know what the payload does because I received a private version of it. My mother raised me on plays, museums, symphonies, and musicals—the whole program run by one determined woman on an audience of one. Then we moved to Hawaii, away from the stages and the museum halls, and it landed on me like exile, because it was one: I had been shown the other world early enough to spend the rest of my childhood homesick for it. The homesickness was the most useful thing I owned. It has been paying out ever since.

I know the Fund itself from the engine room. My agency, Abraham Harrison, held the Fresh Air Fund as a client. We were hired to modernize its digital footprint—to recruit host families, counselors, and donors in a century that had stopped answering the telephone—and we launched its social profiles and ran eleven targeted blogger outreach campaigns that took its online mentions from about 4,000 to about 65,000, roughly 11 million impressions, and grew its Facebook and Twitter audiences with them. I was the man paid to make the country hear about the machine. A man does not spend those years inside an institution's engine room and come out doubting what the machine does. He comes out wondering why it runs on bake-sale economics while the country funds everything else at scale.

Fund it like a road, then. Run it on the off days, in the empty seats, at marginal cost, the parents waving from the curb and the children home by Sunday carrying the one piece of information poverty works hardest to withhold.

Angela Merkel ran the adult version of this program while I lived in Germany, and she ran it on grown women. The Integrationskurs was the law of the land from the middle of the decade: hundreds of hours of language instruction and a block of civics—German law, German history, German norms—compulsory for the immigrant who wanted to stay, with the residence permit riding on attendance. The public rationale was vocabulary and paperwork, the grease every bureaucracy pours on its own gears. The function underneath the rationale stood in plain sight for anyone living there to see. The courses hauled the Muslim wife out of the insular household—out of the apartment where her world ran from the kitchen to her husband's family and back, governed by a patriarchal code carried whole from another century and another continent—and sat her in a German classroom, in German, across from a German teacher, and taught her what German law said she was. A person. A rights-holder. A woman who could call the police on the man at her own table and be believed, who could open a bank account, who could leave. The state called it integration. It was extraction, conducted with a syllabus.

The method underneath the syllabus is the oldest one in the rescue business. A battered wife is saved by distance first and education second: get her out of the house, out from under the fear and the management, and only then does anything taught to her stand a chance of staying taught. Merkel ran that rescue at national scale, with attendance taken. And the syllabus was the one errand no husband could forbid without confessing the whole arrangement in front of the neighbors. A man can bar his wife from a café, a job, a driver's license, a friend's apartment. Barring her from the state's own classroom, the classroom the state requires for the permit the whole family needs, announces to the street exactly what the household is. So the women went to school. The state had found the crowbar that fits the sealed household's only seam: an obligation the keeper himself has to honor.

The Disneyland trip is the same errand run at age nine, and the sealed households of America come in every flavor the country manufactures. Inner-city blocks as closed as any village in Anatolia. Hasidic streets in Brooklyn where a child can reach voting age inside a few square miles of the known. Pennsylvania Dutch farms. Immigrant enclaves where the television speaks the old country's language and the new country is a rumor that bills you. Every sealed community runs on the same fuel: the child's belief that the walls are the world, and that the people who built the walls built them out of love and law rather than fear. A bus breaks that belief in a day. Distance first. The teaching happens by itself, afterward, for the rest of the child's life.

The trip is a conversion, and its mechanism is the oldest one in the appetite trade. The child receives, free, the full liturgy of the American church: the thirty-dollar turkey leg eaten standing up, the forty-dollar set of ears worn without embarrassment, the parade, the fireworks over the castle, the whole catalogue of wanting displayed at full brightness in a single day. He pays nothing. That is the design, and every dealer in every substance that hooks a human being knows the design by heart. The first hit is free. The first pill is free. The first taste is always free, because the first taste is the entire business model and everything after it the customer finances himself. What the free taste dissolves is the barrier nobody prices: the belief that the place is a place he can be. Before the trip, Disneyland is a rumor from television, a country other people's children emigrate to in commercials. After it, he has stood there in his own shoes with the ears on his own head, and a place a boy has stood in once is a place he can picture himself standing in forever. The velvet rope was always mostly in his head. Now it is gone, and the wanting has sixty years to work.

The economics, run honestly, end the resemblance to charity. The state pays the acquisition cost once, at age nine, at off-peak marginal prices. The cathedral collects for the rest of the customer's life: his tickets as a teenager with a first job, his honeymoon, his own children's ears, the hotel, the photographs, the whole liturgy purchased at retail with wages he now organizes his life around earning. It is the American equivalent of flying the inner-city kid to the Vatican and letting him hear the choir at full volume in the cathedral of the faith—except this faith's cathedral sells churros, and the conversion is measured in lifetime receipts. Nobody argues a child out of his father's world. Arguments stay outside the walls and die there. The cathedral does it by admission, in a day, and sends him home a carrier: the ears on his dresser in the sealed neighborhood, radiating elsewhere into a room that had never contained a single object from outside the walls. He has seen what money buys. He has felt wanting from the inside, with an address attached. The treadmill of the republic is built of exactly that wanting, and the trip installs it ahead of the well's own curriculum.

And the liturgy has a sacrament, and the sacrament is administered by hand, on the child's body, in the open. Put ten million under-loved children through those gates and the cast members will hug every one of them—Mickey at full attention, arms wide, on schedule, in costume, the hug never withheld for bad behavior, never shortened by tiredness, never withdrawn in anger, never interrupted by a telephone or a bottle or a grievance from the other room. Professional love. Manufactured love. Love that shows up when the ticket says it will show up, identical for every child, in a place that money built. For a child raised on rationed attention—on the distracted, exhausted, intermittently available affection of overwhelmed adults—the manufactured article is indistinguishable from the real one, and on the one measure he can test, reliability, it is better than the real one. He learns the catechism in his body before he can spell it: money equals kindness. Money equals attention. Money equals pleasure. Money equals love. Four lines, learned in an afternoon, in a hug.

Harry Harlow proved the hunger underneath it in a laboratory in Wisconsin in the 1950s, with infant monkeys and two artificial mothers. One mother was wire and carried the milk. The other was wire wrapped in terrycloth and carried nothing. The babies clung to the cloth mother eighteen hours a day, visited the wire one only to feed, and ran back to the softness with their mouths still wet. Contact comfort beat food in a rout. Warmth and touch outranked the bottle in animals a few weeks old, and the monkeys raised without the cloth grew up broken in the precise ways that under-loved children grow up broken. Every child in the target population of this program is running Harlow's experiment in his own kitchen. He will cling to the soft fake because the hunger for warmth outranks every other hunger he has, and the park is a terrycloth mother the size of a small city, staffed by professionals, open on Tuesdays in February. The starved make the fiercest converts in every faith ever founded, because the faith that first feeds the starving gets a loyalty the merely persuaded never give. These children will be zealots—zealots for capitalism, zealots for hugs, zealots for the equation they learned in their bodies at nine years old. Ten million of them would require ten more parks to hold the pilgrimage. Build the parks. The converts will staff them, gladly, in costume, hugging the next cohort exactly as they were hugged.

Every other program the country runs for poor children is built to route around the parents in silence, and the silence is the tell. School lunch is the perfect artifact of the doctrine: the state feeds the child at noon, in the cafeteria, and files no charge against anyone. The empty stomach is treated as weather. Nobody stands up in the legislature and says the obvious sentence—that a child arrives hungry at school because the adults in his house failed to feed him, that parents can be bad at parenting, addicted, irresponsible, absent while present, and that the failure has a name and an address. The sentence is unsayable because the whole steward system rests on its opposite. The zookeeper cannot blame the animal for being an animal. Stewardship manages creatures it has defined as incapable of responsibility, and a creature incapable of responsibility is incapable of fault, so the keeper adds feed, adjusts the enclosure, and blames the feed budget. The zoo itself confesses the arrangement to anyone who reads its history: the modern zoo is an orphanage with landscaping, its founding population the rejected and the orphaned—young animals refused by their mothers, or orphaned by poachers, or pulled out of the violence at the habitat's edge and raised by keepers because the parents were dead or gone or never fit. Blame has no address in an orphanage. The parents are absent. The keeper just keeps.

This program has an address. It is aimed at children whose parents are alive, present, and locatable, and it indicts those parents in the one courtroom whose verdict cannot be appealed: the child's own eyes. The boy stands in the cathedral with the turkey leg in his hand and the hug still warm on his shoulders, and the knowledge assembles itself without a single adult saying a word against his mother and father. They could never have brought him here. The sentence completes itself in the harder form: they couldn't, rather than they wouldn't. And with that completion, the sealed culture's founding claim collapses in the only mind the culture cannot afford to lose. Every closed community teaches its children that the deprivation is virtue—that the walls keep out corruption, that the outside is filth and danger, that the family's narrowness is righteousness and its poverty is purity. The trip re-prices the doctrine in a day. The outside turns out to be pleasure, welcome, abundance, and grown adults in costume competing to hug him. The righteousness turns out to have been incapacity wearing its Sunday clothes. A child who has watched his parents' piety get re-priced as poverty carries that exchange rate home in his pocket, and he spends the rest of his childhood doing conversions at the family table. The state never has to argue with the well. It only has to arrange for the well's keepers to be measured, once, by their own son, against a mouse.

The road this bus travels was surveyed long ago by men with exactly this brief in their hands, and the country has spent a century learning to say their names with horror. The extraction of children from sealed, poor, alien communities—communities whose parents a dominant culture has examined and ruled unfit—for the children's own good, into institutions built to remake them: that is the residential school. It is Carlisle, Pennsylvania, 1879, Captain Pratt and his doctrine of killing the Indian to save the man. It is Canada's century of church-run schools, a hundred and fifty thousand Indigenous children taken from their families by law, their languages beaten out of them, their hair cut, their names replaced, thousands of them dead of disease and neglect and despair in buildings the state funded and the churches staffed, until the last school closed in 1996 and the country began, a generation later, digging at the grounds and apologizing to nations. It is the mission schools of Hawaii. It is every orphan train and every social worker's removal order signed over a kitchen table. Every one of those programs called itself rescue. Every one of them could point, accurately, at real failure inside the homes it emptied: real alcoholism, real hunger, real violence, real children in real danger. The pointing was true. The rescue was also a theft, and both facts have survived a hundred years of argument intact, side by side, unreconciled.

The differences this proposal carries are real, and they deserve to be stated at full strength before they are weighed. One day, against seven years. Home Sunday with photographs, against graves behind the schoolhouse. No custody papers, no court ruling, no social worker's signature, no haircut, no forbidden language, no new name. The parents wave from the curb; nobody rules them anything in any proceeding; the child's own delight is the only instrument the state is permitted to use. These are differences of degree and of mercy, and degree and mercy are not nothing—a day and a lifetime are different things, and the man who cannot see the difference has no business near the subject. But every extractor in the history of the practice believed his version was the merciful one. The priests at the Canadian schools believed it over the bodies of the children in their care. Pratt believed it while he posed his before-and-after photographs. Mercy is the one claim every man on that road has always made about himself, in advance, in writing, and it has never once been accepted as evidence by the people the road was run over. The license underneath all of it is a single license, and it does not come in sizes: the state may reach into the sealed family, over the parents' authority, for the child's good, as the state defines the good, whenever the state's culture judges the family's culture to be a well with a child at the bottom of it. This proposal applies for that license. It applies in the open, for one day, with the child home by Sunday—and it applies.

Underneath the license sits the ownership question, the one the current argument refuses to say out loud. Children belong to their parents. That is the old answer, and for most of the country's life it was too obvious to need stating. The newer doctrine advances without ever being argued in the open: the state as the child's natural steward and controller, the parents demoted to a breeding pair, tolerated as custodians pending inspection. Every program in the steward line runs on the newer answer and depends on never stating it. This proposal states its answer instead. The state borrows the child for one day from the people he belongs to, and a borrower asks. The asking is the whole difference between a loan and a seizure, between a program that knows whose child this is and an empire that has forgotten.

The clause at the front of this proposal was there from the first hour, before any of this was visible: nobody seized, no custody, no court, home Sunday. It was written as plain decency, the obvious limit any sane version of the idea would carry. It reads differently at the end of the argument than it did at the beginning. It is the antibody—the one part of the program engineered to keep the program from becoming the thing its own logic points at, inserted by instinct before its author had followed the logic to Carlisle and the unmarked grounds in Canada. It stays in. The proposal stays in beside it, whole and unretracted, with its shadow standing next to it in the same light, which is more honesty than the men of 1879 ever offered the families they emptied: they printed the word rescue and buried the other word with the children. This proposal prints both. There is another America. It is real, and it has a door, and the road to the door runs past graves the country is still counting.

Send every poor kid to Disneyland.

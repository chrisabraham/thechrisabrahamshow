The AI economy's compute hangs on a chain of single points. One company designs the accelerators everyone must buy: Nvidia, and behind Nvidia one man, Jensen Huang, whom Chris Abraham, whose rant commissioned this piece, calls the video RAM hegemon. One island fabricates nearly all of the leading-edge supply: Taiwan, which in practice means TSMC. One Dutch company builds the only machines that can print the things: ASML, whose extreme ultraviolet lithography systems sell for $350 to $400 million apiece and have no substitute at any price. A chain with three links and no spares is not a supply chain. It is a toll road with three booths, and the whole industry queues at them, grateful, paying the posted price, because there is no other road. My subject is the first serious attempt to saw through the chain, and the question the saw raises: what happens to a hegemony when its largest customer decides to become its landlord's competitor?

The hegemon

Huang's primacy is a specific kind of power, and it is worth naming precisely. Nvidia does not merely sell the best AI chips. It sells the chips its customers' software already speaks, through CUDA, so switching suppliers means rewriting the factory, not just the purchase order. It allocates. In the shortage years the industry's giants queued for supply the way studios once queued for film stock, and the queue itself became a status hierarchy: who got how many, how early, at what margin. A hegemon is not the biggest seller in a market. A hegemon is the seller whose roadmap is the market's calendar, whose product launches move other companies' stock prices, and whose customers describe receiving supply as a privilege. By that definition the title is earned. And hegemony of this kind has a known weakness, visible in every era of it: it teaches the biggest customer to dream of a road of his own.

The saw

The saw has a name, and the name is announced fact, not speculation. In March 2026, Elon Musk announced Terafab: Tesla, SpaceX, and xAI together, Intel joining in April on its 14A process, building logic fabrication, memory production, and packaging under one roof in Texas, a prototype line at Giga Texas and the main plant in Grimes County, with a full buildout projected as high as $119 billion and a stated goal of a terawatt of AI compute hardware a year. Four-fifths of the output is earmarked for SpaceX's orbital AI infrastructure and the rest for Tesla's vehicles and robots, which makes Terafab something rarer than a competitor: a customer seceding. Nvidia does not lose a sale to Terafab. Nvidia loses the premise that the largest buyers must queue. Note, because honesty about the saw matters, what it has not done: it has not challenged the third link. Musk praised ASML in public, addressed its employees, and secured expedited shipments of its machines. The man sawing the chain bought his saw from the chain. Tool modification, designing faster proprietary equipment, sits in phase two of his own roadmap, which is where a futurist should watch.

The fuller speculation, and its grade

The speculation that commissioned this piece goes further, and it belongs to Chris Abraham by way of his friend David, a man Chris describes as a genius mogul. David's version: Musk will own the stack up and down. Fabs, then the machines that make chips, rebuilt cheaper, then the inputs themselves, silicon and rare-earth mining, possibly anchored in South Africa, where Musk was born and where the minerals are. Graded against the record, David called the motive exactly and the map loosely. The fabs are real and they are in Texas, because a leading-edge fab needs a power grid that never blinks, and South Africa's grid blinks for a living. The mining instinct survives in miniature: Musk has pursued South African rare earths for the magnets his robots and motors need. The machine-replacing remains a phase-two promise rather than a purchase order. And Chris's own contribution to the theory is the pattern underneath it, the one his Starlink essay on chrisabraham.net argues this morning: SpaceX exists, on his reading, because launch was the bottleneck between a man and 15,000 satellites, so the man built the road. Chips are simply the current toll booth. Where his supply chain pays a toll, a factory is coming.

What to watch

Speculation earns its keep when it tells you where to look. Look at phase two: whether Terafab's tool-modification program produces a named machine, a named hire, a named patent, because that is the moment the saw reaches the third link. Look at memory: logic gets the headlines, but video RAM, the high-bandwidth memory stacked beside the accelerators, is the hegemon's quieter moat, and Terafab putting memory under the same roof is the tell that the secession is serious. Look at the queue: whether Nvidia's allocation language softens toward its largest customers as Terafab's first wafers approach, because hegemons discount before they fall, and the discount is the confession. And look at the 80 percent: a fab built mostly to feed orbital data centers is a fab whose economics no terrestrial rival can match line for line, because its customer is also its launch provider, its power buyer, and its landlord.

The summary is the lede, restated plainly. The AI economy hangs on one designer, one island, and one machine, and the arrangement held because every customer, however large, treated the chain as weather. Terafab is the first customer treating it as a toll road and paving a bypass, with the announced record already past the point where the idea can be laughed off and the speculative remainder, the machines and the mines, still exactly that: remainder, labeled, awaiting its evidence. Hegemonies rarely end with a battle. They end with a road nobody has to queue on anymore.

The fish were jumping in the boat, and I filed the whole phenomenon under weather. In my twenties I lived in a clearing where female attention arrived uninvited, regularly, and in quantities I did nothing clever to earn, and I concluded that the world simply worked that way for everybody. The conclusion held for years because the sample kept confirming it. The sample was the problem. I was drawing from the top of a distribution and reading my local water as the climate of the whole ocean.

The animal in the notes

The young male in these field notes stood six foot three. He worked as a bike courier in Washington and held a job in public relations and marketing on the side of the courier work, an arrangement that gave him strong legs, a tan, a paycheck, and a daily route through the busiest clearings in the city. He had been an athlete, a rower and a runner, and the training still showed in his frame and in his habits. He kept his own pad, with no roommates, a door that locked, and a kitchen that answered to one animal.

Each item on that list carried weight in the habitat and he carried all of them at once, in the same season, in a city full of young women working their first jobs after college. Height did quiet work at a distance. Employment did quiet work up close. A place of his own did the loudest work of all, because privacy was scarce among the young and a man who controlled a whole territory, however small the apartment, offered something the group-house males could only promise for later. The notes record the advantages plainly. The young observer failed to add them up.

Weather

He filed the attention under weather because weather is the category a creature uses for conditions it never chose and never has to explain. Rain falls on the just and the unjust. Sun warms whoever steps outside. In his reading, the post-sixties world had simply liberalized the clearing: the old fences were down, women approached men as freely as men approached women, and the jumping fish were the natural traffic of a liberated pond. The reading flattered the era and excused the observer from examining his own position in it.

The era deserved part of the credit. The women of that Washington were educated, employed, funny, and direct in a way their mothers' generation had been trained away from, and a man moving through that habitat met candor where an earlier male met ceremony. Directness was real. The error lay in the accounting: he credited the whole catch to the season and none of it to the boat, the bait, or the stretch of water he happened to occupy.

The boat

A boat sits in one place on the water. Fish jump where the fish are, and the fish were thick in his stretch for reasons that had names. The courier route put him in motion through downtown all day, visible, physical, easy to hail, a known animal on a known circuit. The rowing past gave him the carriage of a man accustomed to effort. The apartment gave every encounter a possible destination. The marketing job gave him conversation, ease with strangers, practice at reading a face in the first thirty seconds. Remove any two of those planks and the boat rides lower. Remove four and the same man, with the same face and the same heart, sits in still water wondering where the fish went.

The pad deserves its own line in the ledger. A one-man territory in a city of shared houses and roommate rotas meant an evening could extend itself without negotiation, a meal could be cooked at midnight, a record could play at the volume the moment wanted. Women read that territory accurately and early. A man with his own door was a man who had already cleared several of adulthood's fences, and the clearing of fences is the signal the whole habitat watches for, in every species that builds anything before it mates.

He never ran that subtraction at the time. Subtraction requires a control group, and a young man enjoying abundance has no appetite for controls. His friends in the same clearing were also catching, which seemed to settle the question, until an older observer notices what he missed then: his friends were drawn from the same stretch of water. Couriers, athletes, young professionals with their own doors and keys—the troop he compared himself against shared his coordinates almost exactly. A sample drawn from one bank of the river will describe that bank beautifully and the river badly.

The audit

The correction arrived the way corrections usually arrive in a long field study: slowly, then all at once, as the coordinates changed. Age moved the boat. Careers moved the boat. The city itself restocked its ponds with new young males every September, each cohort certain that the jumping fish were weather. Watching a later cohort file the same report from the same stretch of water taught the older observer more than any argument could have. The report was accurate as observation and false as theory, in his own handwriting, dated twenty years apart.

The honest audit keeps both halves of the record. The young man was kind within his lights, generous with his time, and free of grievance; the fish jumped toward a male who liked women plainly and treated their company as a pleasure rather than a contest. Temperament mattered. Temperament rode on structure the way a rider rides a horse, and the structure did most of the traveling. A pleasant man in a poor position catches less than a pleasant man in a rich one, and the pleasant man in the rich position will misread the difference as character every time, because character is the explanation that lets him keep the credit.

What the distribution means

Every clearing has a distribution, and the animals at the top of it live in a different habitat from the animals in the middle, while sharing a fence and a water supply. The top animal's reports about the clearing are sincere and unreliable in a specific direction: he overstates how open the clearing is, how easy the feeding is, how little the fences matter. He has never tested the fences. The fish jumped his fence so often that he concluded the fence was decorative.

The middle animals know the fence is load-bearing. Their reports err in the opposite direction, toward a clearing closed to everyone, because their sample also lies to them, from the other bank. The full river appears only to the observer who has occupied more than one position on it and kept notes in both places. Time grants that range to almost every man who lives long enough and pays attention. Youth gives a man one bank and total confidence. Age gives him the second bank and the corrected notebook.

The entry, amended

I amend the early entry in the same hand that wrote it. The fish were jumping in the boat. The boat was tall, employed, housed alone, athletic, and moving through the richest water in the city at the richest hour of its occupant's life. The weather was fair in that clearing in those years, and the weather was local. I mistook my coordinates for the climate, and the mistake cost me nothing at the time, which is exactly how a man can afford to keep a wrong theory for twenty years.

There is a practical use for the corrected entry, and the older observer applies it whenever a young male in a rich position explains the clearing to him. He listens with affection and discounts the theory by the coordinates. He applies the same discount in reverse to the bitter reports from the dry bank, where a man can fish honest water with a sound boat and still catch little in a thin season. Both reports are field notes. Both deserve entry in the record. Neither one, alone, describes the river.

The older animal holds no grudge against the younger one and files no apology for him either. He reported what he saw. He saw it from one bank, in good light, with fish rising all around him, and he wrote down weather. The notebook now carries the correction in full, and the correction is the more useful entry: abundance is a position before it is a personality, and a man who knows where his boat sat reads every later clearing, his own and everyone else's, with clearer eyes and a kinder pencil.

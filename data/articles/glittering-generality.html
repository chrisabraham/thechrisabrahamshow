A man on Bluesky posted a liturgy. The sickness is fascism. The cure is a healthier society: educated, inclusive, strong media, fact-based communication, human rights. Fascism is the sickness. This morning I handed the liturgy back to him with one word changed—the sickness is extremism—and he explained, patiently, that there is no such thing. Threatening all humans with dignity and respect is not extreme; it is humanity and civilization, and humanity and civilization cannot be extreme by definition. Read that twice and the trick shows itself. His list is what a healthy society looks like from inside his own chair, so the chair can never be the problem. When he says all, he means his. The dignity in that sentence has a membership desk in front of it, and he is standing at the desk, checking names.

What is extreme?

Start with the word he built the liturgy on. What is extremism? What is normalized, basic, normie—and what is extreme? Is socialism extreme? Is communism extreme? Is fascism extreme? Is Republicanism extreme? Are the Democrats extreme? What is an extremist in America, exactly, and who holds the measuring stick?

Every answer to those questions turns out to be a location. Extremism is positional. Each ideology files itself as the healthy center and diagnoses the rest of the room as the sickness, and the diagnosis always comes out the same way: the center is wherever the speaker is standing, and the extreme begins at the first chair that is not his. The communist finds fascism extreme and finds his own program to be the plain arithmetic of justice. The fascist returned the compliment with the flags reversed. The normie in the middle looks at both ends and calls the pair of them crazy, secure in a center defined by nothing except his distance from each. Nobody's own chair ever makes the list. Extremism is the name a position gives to other positions, and a man who has defined his values as humanity itself has arranged, in advance, to be unmeasurable. He has not proven that his side is moderate. He has exempted it from the instrument.

This is the standard grammar of the dignity business, and the exemption is where the rest of the vocabulary comes from. Dignity for all. Social justice for all. Liberation for everybody, freedom for the people, rights for all humans. All, everybody, the people—these are the biggest words in the vocabulary, and they do the smallest work of any words in it. Inside the sentences they sound like weather, covering everything. Inside the practice they cover a cloister: a small company of the righteous who hold the correct opinions in the correct order, who pass the purity tests as the tests are revised, who can be trusted to say the current words for the current things. Those people get the dignity at full strength, undiluted, hand-delivered. Everybody outside the wall gets the other half of the doctrine, the half that never makes the liturgy. They can rot in hell. They can sit in jail. They can go fuck themselves, and the saying of it is treated as a form of the dignity work itself.

The membership desk

Watch how the sorting actually runs. A stranger arrives at the cloister wall carrying some wrong opinion—wrong on the war, wrong on the border, wrong on which deaths are grievable this season. The doctrine says his dignity is innate and inalienable, the whole sermon, no exceptions printed anywhere. The practice says his dignity is pending review. He will receive it when he recants, in the correct words, in public, with the correct amount of shame displayed. Until then he is a specimen: a fascist, an extremist, a threat to everything decent, whatever the current label is for a man whose dignity has been suspended. The labels rotate every few years. The desk stays where it is.

The desk has paperwork. There are statements to sign and statements to condemn, anniversaries to observe and anniversaries to never mention, a calendar of griefs ranked in the approved order. Membership is audited constantly, because the cloister is small and the audit is how it stays small. A member who asks the wrong question in the wrong room discovers that his own dignity was probationary all along. He gets the specimen treatment from people who shared his table last month, and the speed of the conversion is the point of the exercise. It teaches everyone still inside the wall exactly where the wall is. A big tent that audits its members this hard is a tent the size of a phone booth with a cathedral's vocabulary.

And dignity does reach the people outside the wall, after a fashion. It reaches them the way fallout reaches a town downwind of a test site. The cloister fights for its own—for the defendant it has adopted, the prisoner it has canonized, the cause it currently champions—and the winning of those fights occasionally changes a rule for everyone, including the unadopted and the uncanonized and the actively despised. The outsider's share arrives as drift. He benefits as a side effect of the righteous protecting their own, and he is expected to be grateful for the exposure. Nobody inside the wall set out to dignify him. He was standing near somebody who counted.

Everybody, counted

There is a counting instrument for everybody, and it runs on a schedule. It is called an election. If everybody and all Americans actually felt the way the chanters say the people feel—if the liturgy were the country's own voice, rising from the whole population at once—then Hillary Clinton would have won, and Kamala Harris would have won. They did not. The everybody on offer lost, twice, to the specimen-in-chief, in the open, with the whole population invited to participate. Whatever the chanters are describing, it is a population that does not show up in the count. Everybody is a fiction with a membership desk.

The rhetorical term of art for the people want this is a glittering generality: a populist framing that manufactures consensus and social proof instead of reporting a measured public will. It is a technique, with a name, taught in textbooks, and its whole function is to make a faction's program sound like a census. The man on Bluesky was not reporting the country's mind this morning. He was running the technique, the way it has been run for a century, and the technique works because everybody is the one word in politics that nobody ever has to count.

The people deserves its own footnote, because the phrase has a pedigree and the pedigree is factional. In power to the people, the people never meant the population. It meant the revolutionary fervor of the working man—the portion of the population already in motion, already converted, already marching in the correct direction. The rest of the population, the unconverted and the indifferent and the actively opposed, were the obstacle the slogan proposed to roll over. Today's universal language is that old factional phrase wearing a bigger coat: tropey communist rhetoric in sheep's clothing, the vanguard's word for itself, expanded by costume into a word for everyone. When a modern chanter says the people demand it, he is quoting a slogan that was honest, in its original form, about meaning a faction. The costume is the only new part.

The immune system

A doctrine this leaky needs a way to survive its own counterexamples, and it has a beautiful one: reclassification. Evidence against the category never falsifies the category. The people who contradict it get relabeled until the category wins.

The member who leaves is the cleanest case. Yesterday he was a voice of the community, quoted at length, his biography a credential. Today he has doubts in public, and by tonight he was never really one of us—he was a grifter all along, an infiltrator, a man whose membership is retroactively annulled so the cloister's judgment in admitting him stays clean. The scientist who breaks ranks becomes a shill. The victim who tells the wrong story about her own experience becomes a prop of the other side. Every exit is processed as a defect in the person, never as data about the tent. Heads, the category wins. Tails, the counterexample gets renamed until the category wins. A vocabulary maintained this way can never lose an argument, because it has arranged in advance for every loss to arrive disguised as a win. All can never be caught meaning somebody. The desk's paperwork handles that too.

Every cloister keeps a small authority of its own for this work, and the authority runs on a loop anyone can watch. Most negative response by authority is the result of negative response to authority. The expulsion answers the heresy, and the heresy was answering the last expulsion, and each round produces the defendants who justify the next round. The cloister calls one direction of that loop accountability and the other direction harm, depending on who is being expelled. The loop itself does not care what it is called. It feeds on both directions equally, and it keeps the tent small, which was the function all along.

The test

There is a simple test for any all, and it takes ten seconds. Find the man the speaker despises most—the loudest specimen outside his wall, the one whose name comes with a warning label—and ask whether the dignity covers him, at full strength, today, with no recantation scheduled. Ask whether the rights run to him in the same portion, whether the justice in social justice has a cell with his name on the door or a portion with his name on the box. The speaker will begin, within one sentence, to explain the exception. The exception will be reasonable. The exception always is. That is how a universal word dies: one reasonable exception at a time, each one processed at the membership desk, until all means the people who were never going to be excepted.

Run the test in the other direction and it gets uncomfortable for everyone, which is how you know it is a real test. Everybody's all has a specimen outside it somewhere. The question the test asks is whether the exception is a door the man can walk through by changing his behavior, or a wall built around who he is. A dignity that returns when a man pays his debt and keeps the rules is a dignity with terms, and terms can be met. A dignity that returns only when a man becomes a different person, with different opinions and a different past, is a membership benefit wearing a universal's coat.

Government cheese

Now set the other vocabulary next to it. When middle-of-the-road people and conservative people say rights for everybody, they mean something much less lyrical and much more literal: basic, generic, government-cheese rights for everybody. Government cheese came in a plain block, in a plain box, with no branding and no story attached, and it fed whoever stood in the line. That is the conservative everybody. The same portion for the man you love and the man you cannot stand. No preferences. No expectations. No special servings for the righteous, and no suspended dignity for the specimen of the week. The cheese does not ask what you believe. The line does not check your opinions at the door.

Accountability rides in the same box. Freedom, in this vocabulary, is accountable freedom: your rights run at full strength and so does your bill. You may speak, and you may answer for the speaking. You may carry, assemble, worship, refuse, and build, and the consequences of the carrying and the building land on you, personally, at full price. The cloister's version hands its members dignity with the accountability stripped out—consequences are reserved for the people outside the wall, whose jail cells and ruined names are just the doctrine working as intended. The cheese version keeps the two bolted together for everybody in the line, which is the entire reason it can afford to feed everybody in the line.

So here is the choice the two vocabularies actually offer. One says all and means a cloister, with a membership desk, purity tests, suspended dignities, and fallout for the neighbors. The other says everybody and means the line at the government cheese window—plain portions, no favorites, full accountability, first come first served. I will take the cheese. It is the only universal on offer that feeds the whole line.

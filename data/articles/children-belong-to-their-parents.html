Children belong to their parents. Not to the state.

Within living memory that sentence was the floor American arguments stood on. Conservatives said it, liberals said it, and the people who had fled countries where the opposite was practiced said it loudest and with the least patience for debate. It needed no defense because it described the arrangement every family in the country was already living: the child at the kitchen table belonged to the people at the table, and every institution outside the door—school, church, state—dealt with him through them.

The ownership question underneath it is the conflict nobody in the current argument wants to say out loud. Every fight of 2026 is a border skirmish over that one question. Curriculum fights, counseling records, medical consent, library shelves, notification policies, the file the parent may not read: each skirmish is small, procedural, and deniable, and each one moves the same border by the same few feet. Stated plainly, the main question sounds like something from another century, which is exactly why both armies prefer the skirmishes. Whose child is this. The country is litigating that question in a thousand small rooms while agreeing in public that the question does not exist.

A century ago the claim that children belong to the state was the tyrant's tell, recognized on sight by the whole civilized world. Sparta took its boys at seven into the agoge and raised them as property of the city, and Sparta stood for two thousand years as the standing warning of what a state becomes when it starts with the children. The Soviet Union built the Young Pioneers and the Komsomol to route a child's loyalty around his parents and plug it directly into the party. Mao's Cultural Revolution listed filial piety—reverence for one's own mother and father—among the Four Olds to be smashed, and schoolchildren were set to denouncing their parents in public as an exercise in civic virtue. The refugee from any of those systems arrived in America carrying one sentence in his luggage, and he would say it to anyone who asked what he had fled: the state that reaches for the children has told you what it is.

In 2026 the sentence has changed sides. Say "children belong to their parents" in respectable company now and the room hears a threat—an authoritarian claim, an anti-liberal claim, a claim that endangers children by placing them beyond the reach of the professionals. The polarity of a hundred years has reversed itself inside a single generation, and the reversal arrived without an argument anyone can point to, without a vote, without a day on which the country sat down and decided that the old floor was wrong. The floor was replaced while the house was occupied, one procedure at a time.

The hinge is a quiet one, and it is inside liberalism itself. The classical liberal order treated the family as the smallest sovereign thing in the world—a jurisdiction older than the state, into which the state entered by permission and on sufferance. The modern order dissolved that unit and kept dissolving, down past the family to the individual, and then down to the smallest individual of all: the child, holding rights of his own, seated across the table from his parents as a party with interests that may conflict with theirs. The moment the child becomes a plaintiff, he needs representation, and representation arrives on schedule wearing a lanyard. Authoritarian stopped naming a system and started naming a location: whoever holds unchecked power over another person. In a kitchen at midnight, the holder of unchecked power is the parent. So the state re-enters the home in a new costume, as the child's lawyer, and every entry reads as a rescue.

The two great family-weakening doctrines of the age arrive from opposite directions and meet at that same kitchen table. The collectivist weakens the family so the child belongs to the state. The autonomy liberal weakens the family so the child belongs to himself. The second destination sounds like freedom and functions like custody, because a twelve-year-old's self-ownership is a fiction that requires an administrator. Somebody has to hold the child's rights in trust, interpret them, decide when they have been violated, and enforce them against the people at the table. The administrator is never the child. It is the counselor, the caseworker, the court, the state. "Belongs to himself" is "belongs to the state" after a copy edit, and the parents in the room can tell, because in every concrete dispute the child's sovereignty is exercised by an adult they did not choose.

Both doctrines need the parent demoted, and the reason is structural rather than personal. The parent is the only rival transmitter in the child's life. Loyalty, faith, memory, the names of things, the judgment of what is shameful and what is sacred—all of it reaches the child through the parent, or it reaches him later and weaker, against the parent's transmission. A system that wants the child's allegiance has to get past the transmitter, and getting past him requires reclassifying him first: from authority to obstacle, from the child's first government to the child's first risk factor. The demotion is never announced as a demotion. It arrives as concern.

The Soviet Union understood the destination and built a shrine at it. Pavlik Morozov was a boy of thirteen who, in 1932, informed the authorities on his own father, and was murdered for it—the state said by his own family—and the empire canonized him in textbooks, songs, statues, and a cult that lasted generations: the child whose loyalty to the state outranked his blood, held up before every schoolchild in the Soviet Union as the model of what a child is for. The wedge between parent and child, made sacred. Modern American practice runs the same structure at low temperature and would be offended by the comparison, which changes nothing about the structure. The counselor who keeps a child's confidences from his parents as a matter of policy, the record the parent learns about after the decision it documents, the professional trained to regard the home as the hazard and the institution as the refuge: the parent who objects is told the secrecy is protection, and sometimes it is, and the structure is Morozov's either way—an institution teaching a child that his confidant sits in an office and the people to be protected from sit at his table.

One American said the quiet part on television, once, in plain words. In 2013, Melissa Harris-Perry told an MSNBC audience that Americans have to break through the private idea that kids belong to their parents or kids belong to their families, and recognize that kids belong to whole communities. The sentence caused a week of scandal, and the scandal was misplaced, because the sentence was simply honest—the one candid statement of the doctrine on record, spoken by a believer who had not yet learned that the doctrine advances by procedure and dies by quotation. She said kids belong to whole communities. Whole communities act through their institutions. Institutions are staffed. The staff has offices, forms, and visiting hours.

The doctrine has a zoology, and the zoology gives it away. In its vocabulary the parent has been quietly reclassified as a breeding pair: the two adults who produced the child, a biological event the system acknowledges and then routes around. Kennel practice supplies the management model, and in kennel language every step of it sounds humane. You leave the puppies with the mother long enough to wean. Then you place them in proper homes. Every word is standard practice, decent practice, the practice of people who care about puppies—for animals. A family processed as livestock management never has to be argued with, only managed, and the reclassification does its work in silence, the way the older argument's sealed community did: the family as a well with a child at the bottom of it, the parents as the water to be drained, the state arriving with a bucket and a syllabus and the child's own good held up as the warrant.

The older American answer is still on the books, and it was written at full strength. In 1925, striking down an Oregon law that would have compelled every child into public school, the Supreme Court wrote the sentence the whole structure rests on: "The child is not the mere creature of the State; those who nurture him and direct his destiny have the right, coupled with the high duty, to recognize and prepare him for additional obligations." Pierce v. Society of Sisters. Nearly half a century later, Wisconsin v. Yoder let Amish parents take their children out of school after the eighth grade, against the state's claim that two more years of formation belonged to it. The cases are old. The presumption inside them has never been overruled. It has been routed around, procedurally, one confidential file at a time, by a doctrine that cannot afford to meet it in the open and has never had to.

The case against the presumption is real, and it deserves to be stated at full strength, because a doctrine that survives only against a weak version of its enemy deserves to fall. Children are not property. A parent holds a child the way a trustee holds an estate, and trustees can loot, and some do. Some homes are cages. Some parents are the danger the child needs rescuing from, and when the extended family and the neighbors and the church have failed or looked away, the rescuer is the state or the rescuer is nobody, and a child cannot wait for a better doctrine while he is being harmed. Pierce itself was argued about fit parents; the presumption was built for the ordinary home and says nothing comfortable about the worst one. And the presumption's defenders enter every hearing carrying a structural handicap: the right is general or it is nothing, so the doctrine of parental primacy must stand up in public and cover the worst father in the county. The state's advocates argue from that worst family. The parents' advocates argue from the median one. In a hearing room the worst family wins the exhibit round every time, because its exhibits are photographs.

All of that is true, and none of it settles the question, because the live question is the default. A backstop is an exception, and an exception has a shape: it arrives with a warrant, a named danger, evidence, and the burden of proof carried by the party doing the taking. It fires rarely, on cause shown, against resistance, in the open. An exception that fires on suspicion, on doctrine, on a professional's standing discomfort with the values at a family's table, has stopped being an exception and become the rule wearing the exception's coat. Whether the state may carry a child out of a burning house was settled long ago, by everyone, and no party to this fight proposes to relitigate it. The fight of 2026 is over who is presumed to hold the child on an ordinary Tuesday, before any fire has been alleged by anyone. Whoever holds the default holds the children. Everything else is procedure, and a doctrine that installs the exception as the default has reassigned custody of the country's children without once winning the argument in the open—without once having to say, in words, that children belong to the state.

Custody follows the presumption, in law and in life. A country that presumes the child belongs to the state will get the state it presumed, delivered one form, one file, one confidential conversation at a time, each delivery reasonable, the sum of them a different country. A country that presumes the child belongs to his parents keeps the older danger and the older freedom in the same house, which is where they have always lived and where every attempt to separate them has ended with the state holding both. The presumption is the whole argument. Children belong to their parents.

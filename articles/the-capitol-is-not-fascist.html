The Capitol Is Not Fascist

An article by Chris Abraham, November 23, 2026

This article, read aloud:

Share: X · Facebook · LinkedIn · Reddit · Bluesky · Threads · Email

The Capitol makes nothing and eats everything. Coal comes from District 12, grain and produce from District 11, fish from District 4, textiles, lumber, power, livestock—district after district exists to send its work uphill to a city of consumers who dye their skin, alter their bodies, dress for parties, and watch children die on television for entertainment. Panem runs on extraction. A producing district starves beside its own coal seam while the Capitol holds a banquet.

Fascist regimes in Italy and Germany ran the other way. Mussolini's economists named their aim productivism—the maximization of industrial and agricultural production in the national interest. Heavy industry took the money. Steel, shipbuilding, pig iron, chemicals, armaments. The German autobahn became the emblem of the new order, concrete poured as propaganda, and the motorway itself had taken its model from Italy's autostrada, built in the mid-1920s. Both regimes staged huge rallies and parades, built youth movements to raise a generation inside the doctrine, and organized workers' leisure through state bodies—the Italian Dopolavoro, founded in 1925, and the German Strength Through Joy program modeled on it. The worker got flattered, organized, uniformed, and marched. Production was a cult and mobilization was the liturgy.

The Capitol has no production cult. Capitol citizens hold no rallies for coal output, join no youth corps for the harvest, and receive no honors for making anything. Their civic religion is consumption—fashion cycles, surgical alteration, betting, feasting, and the annual Games as the season's great broadcast. President Snow rules through a small court, a propaganda apparatus, and the Peacekeepers posted in the districts. The population at home stays home, entertained and fed.

Mobilization and pacification are opposite technologies, and the difference decides the argument. A mobilizing regime arms its own people, drills them, sends them to build and conquer, and demands their energy as its fuel. Panem disarms the districts, fences District 12 behind electrified wire, forbids hunting, punishes the gathering of food, and keeps its subject population weak, hungry, and separated district from district so no common cause can form. The Games themselves serve pacification twice over—as punishment remembered and as spectacle absorbed. Katniss states the first function plainly in the opening pages: the Games tell the districts to look how their children are taken and sacrificed, with nothing they can do about it. The second function plays out in Capitol living rooms, where the killing arrives as programming.

Suzanne Collins named her sources in interview after interview, and Berlin appears nowhere in the list. She read the myth of Theseus and the Minotaur at eight years old—Athens forced to send seven youths and seven maidens to Crete, to be devoured in the labyrinth, as punishment from a conquering power—and she called Katniss a futuristic Theseus. She drew on Spartacus, the slave whose rebellion she loved in film, the defeated slaves standing one by one to claim the leader's name. She built an updated version of the Roman gladiator games. The originating night is documented in her own telling: lying in bed, channel-surfing between reality television programs and actual footage of the Iraq War, young people competing on one channel and young people fighting a real war on the next, the lines blurring until Katniss's story arrived. Rome, Crete, Thrace, American television, an American war. The architecture is classical and the grievance is populist—a capital city living off a hinterland it despises.

George Orwell diagnosed the label problem before Panem existed. In his 1944 Tribune piece "What is Fascism?" he catalogued the word applied in print to nearly every party and body in England and concluded that, as used, it had lost the last vestige of meaning. Two years later, in "Politics and the English Language" in 1946, he gave the condition its sentence: the word Fascism had by then no meaning except in so far as it signified "something not desirable." Eighty years on, the word still does that work. It marks the speaker's disgust and describes nothing. Calling the Capitol fascist tells a reader how to feel about Snow and nothing about how Panem works—where the food goes, who holds the guns, why the districts stay divided, what the Games are for.

District 13 breaks the simple fable from inside the books. Thirteen survived underground after the first rebellion, militarized, rationed, collectivist in its daily order—meals scheduled, resources pooled, every citizen assigned a function in a society run as a garrison. President Alma Coin leads the second rebellion from that garrison and, with the Capitol fallen, takes interim power for herself on the ground that elections can wait. Her proposal to the surviving victors is the tell: a final, symbolic Hunger Games, with Capitol children as the tributes, offered in place of mass executions and put to a vote of seven traumatized people. Katniss votes yes as a maneuver, walks to Snow's execution with her bow, and puts her arrow through Coin instead. A story built as a warning against one named ideology from the 1930s has no need of Coin. Collins built her anyway, because her subject is power that feeds on people, in whichever uniform it arrives—Snow's decadent court or Coin's gray discipline. The arrow chooses between two tyrannies and refuses both.

The American rhyme sits closer to the text than any European one. District 12 is Appalachian coal country, the Seam named for the coal seams, its miners walking to the morning shift through cinder streets, its children trading extra entries in a death lottery for tesserae—a meager year's supply of grain and oil per person. Katniss at sixteen carries twenty entries; Gale, feeding a family of five, carries forty-two. Coal country in the real America produced company towns where the operator owned the houses, the store, and the law, and it produced mine guards as an institution—private armed men paid by coal associations to keep union men and organizers out.

Blair Mountain, Logan County, West Virginia, August and September 1921: some ten thousand union miners marched and fought Sheriff Don Chafin's force, a private army financed by the Logan County Coal Operators Association, along a ridgeline fortified with machine guns. Private planes hired by Chafin's side dropped homemade bombs—gunpowder, nuts, and bolts, and gas bombs—on American miners on American soil. President Harding sent federal troops and Army bombers for surveillance, and the battle ended under federal bayonets. Seven years earlier, at Ludlow, Colorado, on April 20, 1914, the Colorado National Guard and guards of the Rockefeller-owned Colorado Fuel and Iron Company attacked a tent colony of some 1,200 striking miners and their families; the tents burned, and the dead included two women and eleven children who suffocated and burned beneath one tent. The Peacekeepers fit that lineage with precision—company force in government dress, posted among a captive workforce, guarding the fence, the square, and the whipping post. The Gestapo analogy reaches for a foreign horror to explain a domestic one that has its own names, dates, and counties.

The sneer completes the structure. Capitol stylists and escorts treat district people as barbarians—quaint, dirty, amusing, half-animal in their manners and appetites. Effie's complaints about District 12 table manners run through the books as comedy, and the comedy carries the politics: the people who mine the coal and grow the food are held in contempt by the people who burn the coal and eat the food. Historical fascism flattered its in-group producers as the nation's spine, the worker-soldier exalted in poster and parade. Panem exalts no producer anywhere. Its hierarchy runs on consumption rank—who eats, who watches, who wears this season's color—and its contempt flows downhill toward the hands that feed it. That contempt, aimed at miners and farmers and fishermen by a capital that produces nothing, is the oldest populist grievance on this continent, older than the republic's parties and written into its labor wars in blood.

The ruled never file one report on the ruler. The von Trapp household, the one the world knows from The Sound of Music, ran on a captain's whistle and naval discipline, and the children who grew up under it spent decades filing two incompatible journals. One child's journal says Daddy loves me, that's why he's so hard on me, he wants to make me better. Another's says fuck Dad—one day I'm putting that guy in a nursing home. The real von Trapp children argued in print for years over the film's portrait of their father: some insisted he was warm, that the martinet was a screenwriter's invention, that the man at their table bore no resemblance to the cold disciplinarian on screen; others carried harder accounts out of the same house. Same father, same table, two archives, and neither cancels the other. Panem's districts file the same split record. District 2 raises the Peacekeepers and believes in the Capitol the way a garrison town believes in the army that feeds it, and the Careers of Districts 1, 2, and 4 train from childhood and volunteer for the Games, honored to be chosen, certain the house that takes their children loves them and is making them better. Other districts bury their tributes and curse the city's name. The testimony depends on which child you ask, and both children are telling the truth about the same father.

Panem is Rome wearing American clothes—bread and circuses as a governing system, a tribute lottery out of Crete, a slave revolt out of Spartacus, and a coal war out of Logan County. The Capitol is a consumption aristocracy guarded by mine guards, pacifying a producing hinterland with hunger and television. Fascism, as Orwell already knew in 1944, is the word people reach for when they have stopped describing the thing in front of them.

This piece began with Isabel Brown, a voice I had never encountered before this week. Her November 19 episode of The Isabel Brown Show, Isabel Brown's "I Don't Think We Read the Same Hunger Games", said the plain thing first: Collins wrote a warning about centralized power and totalitarianism, and the viral fight over Maya Hawke's "fascism" line is two audiences reading different books and calling the other side illiterate. She was right, and she said it before the rest of us got our essays up. The flowers are hers. Her book, The End of the Alphabet: How Gen Z Can Save America, makes the longer version of her case for her own generation.

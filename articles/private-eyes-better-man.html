Jason Priestley Is Teaching Me How to Be a Better Man

An article by Chris Abraham, October 13, 2026

I am becoming a better man, and better at relationships, and I owe it almost entirely to a sweet, unpretentious, very Canadian detective show. Let me be clear up front: I am not being snarky, and I am not being ironic. This is true love.

The show is Private Eyes. It ran on Global in Canada from 2016 to 2021, five seasons and 60 episodes, filmed and set in Toronto. Jason Priestley plays Matt Shade, a former pro hockey player who stumbles into partnership with a private investigator, Angie Everett, played by Cindy Sampson. It is totally derivative of Moonlighting, and that is a compliment; Moonlighting is a fine thing to learn from. If you remember Maddie and David, you already know the shape: banter, chemistry, a case of the week, and two people who are obviously right for each other refusing to admit it.

I watch it on Hulu, here in Arlington, Virginia, and I never skip the intro. The credits roll to "Private Eyes," the Hall & Oates hit, and I love Hall & Oates, so every episode starts with me singing along. It never gets old.

And Jason Priestley is fantastic in it. He's a real actor, a sharp comedian, and an outrageous charmer. What a silver fox. In my head he's about 5'2", which isn't true; the internet mostly puts him nearer 5'9". Doesn't matter. Short King energy is a state of mind, and he has it. The whole cast rocks.

So much ethos

Under the jokes, this show is a manual for how to treat people. Look at what it models:

  • Business partners. Shade and Angie run the agency together, argue, cover for each other, and keep the partnership intact through all of it. I've never once managed that.
  • Men and women as friends. For seasons, Shade and Angie are friends first: equals who respect each other, trust each other, and actually like each other. Whatever comes later is built on that.
  • Disability, done right. Shade's daughter, Jules, played by Jordyn Negri, is visually impaired, and she's written as a whole person with her own plans, opinions, and love life, never as a plot device.
  • Divorce with grace. Shade's ex-wife, Becca, played by Nicole de Boer, is still family. They co-parent, they show up, and nobody burns anything down.
  • Fathers and daughters. Shade with Jules, Shade with his own dad, Don, who runs a diner, and Angie running the agency she inherited from her late father. Even Angie's mother, Nora, a former gambler and small time con, is still family.
  • Grace and mercy. People take the sword for each other, and then they stick around to help heal the wound.

How Canadian is it? Very.

Plenty of shows film in Toronto and pretend it's Chicago. This one doesn't. Toronto plays itself, Shade is a hockey man, and nobody apologizes for any of it. Now there's a sequel, Private Eyes West Coast, which premiered on Global on September 30, 2026, and on The CW in the United States on October 5. Shade and Angie have moved to Victoria, British Columbia, married, with a young child. I am not too proud to admit I'm thrilled.

What Matt Shade has that I don't

Shade's whole thing, as I see it, is that he keeps coming back. He gets told off, he gets it wrong, and he shows up again anyway, warm and a little ridiculous, never too proud to try once more. Watching him, I finally noticed the thing I never do.

Here's my flaw. If someone tells me to fuck off, I fuck off. Forever. Even if what they actually wanted was for me to fight for them. I don't want to step on anyone's agency, I'm not playing games, and when someone says "I hate you, don't let the door hit you," I hear it as an instruction and I go.

Some of that is my wiring. I have SDAM, so I don't get the felt replay of a relationship that most people use to decode a moment like that: the memory that last time she said it, she called back crying, so it means "come get me." The words in front of me carry the whole weight. Aphantasia means I can't picture the face behind the text. ADHD brings rejection sensitivity, and leaving is the fastest way to end the sting. And I grew up learning that when someone says go, you go before it turns into a fight.

Leaving is one decision. Leaving forever is another.

Taking people at their word is a kind of respect, and I'm keeping that. The cost is that anyone who says "go away" while they're flooded, or as a test, loses me over one bad moment. I've been making two decisions at once: leaving, and leaving forever.

Worse, when I'm the one who leaves, I burn the bridge on the way out. I've done it with jobs, with business partners, and with someone I loved. When I talked this through with Claude, it put the difference better than I could: self-respect says "I won't stay where I'm not wanted." Pride says "I won't let you see that I wanted to stay." Leaving is self-respect. The scorched earth is pride, making sure nobody ever catches me wanting anything.

I didn't know I had that kind of pride until that very second.

Groveling is pride's word for vulnerability

I used to file the normal moves under "groveling" and "chasing." Calling twice. Saying "I miss you." Saying "I was out of line." Asking "can we talk?" Those aren't humiliations. They're what people do when someone matters to them. Pride just renames them so that skipping them feels like dignity.

And I don't do any of them. That's the real flaw, more than the leaving. Matt Shade does all of them, every episode, and he's the most charming man on the show.

The plan: reps

These are skills, and skills take practice, so I'm starting where nothing is broken:

  • Texting people I like: "Been thinking about you. Miss talking."
  • Making the second call when a friend doesn't pick up.
  • Saying "that was out of line" over something small, out loud, on the same day.

And for the next time somebody tells me to get lost, I have a line that respects them without playing games: "I'm taking you at your word and giving you space. If that's not what you meant, you know where to find me." Then I actually go, with the door left standing.

I do want to reconnect with the people on the other side of those burned bridges. I'm not there yet, and I know a false start would just be another push. I'll know I'm ready when I can send the message without needing any particular answer back. Then it's a gift, not a test.

Until then I have plenty more Private Eyes to study. Thank you, Jason Priestley. I'm taking notes.

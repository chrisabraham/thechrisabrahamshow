What Private Eyes Is Teaching Me About Pride, Leaving, and Going Back

An article by Chris Abraham, October 13, 2026

I have a flaw that has cost me jobs, business partners, and someone I loved. I've known its shape for years without seeing it clearly. What finally showed it to me was a Canadian television show, Private Eyes, and I want to write down what it's teaching me while it's still fresh. I mean every word of this. It isn't a joke, and it isn't irony. I love this show, and it is making me a better man.

Why a television show

This isn't a review. I'm writing about Private Eyes because it role models, week after week, how people should actually engage with each other. To me it's 80 percent psychology and 20 percent entertainment, and the psychology is the part I've been missing.

The setup is simple. The show ran on Global in Canada from 2016 to 2021, set in Toronto, and a sequel, Private Eyes West Coast, began this fall. Jason Priestley plays Matt Shade, a former pro hockey player who becomes a private investigator alongside Angie Everett, played by Cindy Sampson. The cases are the frame. What happens between the people is the substance: how they disagree, how they apologize, how they forgive, and how they keep showing up for each other. Priestley plays Shade with a warmth and decency that make those things look like strength, which is exactly what I needed to see.

What the show models

These are the lessons I'm taking from it, one relationship at a time.

  • How to be business partners. Shade and Angie disagree, push back, and cover for each other, and the partnership survives every argument. I have never once kept a partnership intact through a real fight. They do it as a matter of course.
  • How men and women can be friends. For seasons, they're friends first: equals who respect each other's competence and actually like each other. Whatever comes later rests on that.
  • How to love a child who sees the world differently. Shade's daughter, Jules, played by Jordyn Negri, is visually impaired. She's written as a whole person, with her own plans, judgment, and independence, and her father has to learn to let her have them.
  • How to be divorced with grace. Shade's ex-wife, Becca, played by Nicole de Boer, is still family. They co-parent, they show up, and their daughter comes first. Nobody keeps the war going.
  • How to honor your parents. Shade keeps close to his father, Don, who runs a diner. Angie carries on the agency she inherited from her late father, and keeps her mother, Nora, a former gambler and small time con, in her life anyway.
  • How to show grace and mercy. People take the sword for each other, then stay to help heal the wound. Mistakes are forgiven, and people are allowed to come back.

That last one is the lesson I need most.

My flaw

If someone tells me to fuck off, I leave, and I leave forever. Even if what they wanted was for me to fight for them. I don't want to override anyone's agency, I don't play games, and when someone says "I hate you, don't let the door hit you," I take it as an instruction.

Part of this is how I'm built. I have SDAM, severely deficient autobiographical memory, so I don't get the felt replay of a relationship that most people use to read a moment like that: the memory that last time she said it, she called back in tears, so it means "come find me." The words in front of me carry their full weight. Aphantasia means I can't picture the face behind them. ADHD brings rejection sensitivity, and leaving is the fastest way to stop that pain. And I grew up learning that when someone says go, you go, before it becomes a fight.

Leaving, and leaving forever

Taking people at their word is a form of respect, and I want to keep it. The cost is that anyone who says "go away" in a flooded moment, or as a test, loses me over a single bad hour. I've been making two decisions at once: to leave, and to never come back.

And when I'm the one who leaves, I burn the bridge behind me. Talking this through with Claude, I heard the distinction that finally made sense of it. Self-respect says, "I won't stay where I'm not wanted." Pride says, "I won't let you see that I wanted to stay." Leaving can be self-respect. Burning the bridge is pride, making sure nobody ever sees me wanting anything. I didn't know I carried that kind of pride until that moment.

Vulnerability is not groveling

I had filed the ordinary moves of love under "groveling" and "chasing": calling twice, saying "I miss you," saying "I was out of line," asking "can we talk?" They aren't humiliations. They're what people do when someone matters to them. Pride renamed them so that skipping them would feel like dignity.

I don't do any of them. That, more than the leaving, is the real flaw. Watching Matt Shade, I see a man who makes those moves without embarrassment, and who is admired for it.

What I'm doing about it

These are skills, and skills take practice, so I'm starting where nothing is broken. I'm telling people I like that I've been thinking about them. I'm making the second call when a friend doesn't pick up. When I'm out of line over something small, I'm saying so, out loud, the same day.

For the next time someone tells me to go, I have words that respect them without playing a game: "I'm taking you at your word and giving you space. If that's not what you meant, you know where to find me." Then I go, and I leave the door standing.

I want to reconnect with the people on the other side of the bridges I burned. I'm not ready yet, and a reach I don't fully mean would only be another push. I'll know I'm ready when I can send the message without needing any particular answer back. Then it's a gift, not a test.

Until then, I'm grateful to a television show for showing me, episode by episode, what it looks like to stay, to forgive, and to come back. Thank you, Jason Priestley, and thank you to everyone who made it.

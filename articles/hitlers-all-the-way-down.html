Hitlers All the Way Down

An article by Chris Abraham, November 23, 2026

This article, read aloud:

Share: X · Facebook · LinkedIn · Reddit · Bluesky · Threads · Email

The story is told of William James. After a lecture on the order of the universe, an elderly woman in the audience informed him that he had it wrong: the world rests on the back of a giant turtle. James, patient, asked what the turtle stood on. Another turtle. And beneath that one? She had been waiting for the question. It's turtles all the way down.

American moral cosmology runs on the same architecture, with a different animal in the stack. Hitler died in a bunker under Berlin on April 30, 1945, eighty-one years ago, and he remains the working standard of evil in a country that has produced fresher monsters of its own and imports new ones by the news cycle. Every argument of any size ends at him. Every strongman is measured against him. Every negotiation is Munich, every concession is appeasement, every opponent of any party is a few rallies and a uniform away from the bunker. He is the reserve currency of American condemnation: the asset every faction holds, the one denomination accepted everywhere, the gold in the vault that backs all the paper accusations printed on top of it.

The vault holds him alone, and the reason is bookkeeping. The twentieth century's other great criminals cannot do the job. Stalin's dead run into the tens of millions; Mao's run higher. Their files, however, are kept in the wrong drawer. They are filed as communists, as socialists, as men of the left—which places them inside the Western left's own family tree, cousins at the table. A speaker who reaches for Stalin as the type specimen of evil has opened an audit of his own house, and the audit will be conducted by his enemies, in public, with his own books. Hitler costs nothing. Fascism was quarantined to the other team two generations ago and the quarantine has held, so the comparison can be spent by anyone, against anyone, without the spender's own accounts ever being examined. The purity of the asset is the whole of its value. He is the one monster a man can invoke without confessing anything.

A currency so specialized requires a fixed definition, and the modern telling has supplied one. Hitler's evil, as it is now taught and repeated, reduces to a single trait standing for the whole: the hatred of the Jews. The wars of conquest, the police state, the euthanasia wards, the bureaucratized murder of Slavs and Roma and the disabled—all of it survives in the record, but the catechism has simplified the man into the supreme antisemite, the outer boundary of human conduct marked by one hatred above the others. For decades the definition was safe to hold because the trait could be located, reliably, in one direction. Jew-hatred was a thing the other side did. The definition did its work and indicted only strangers.

The trait has migrated, and the definition has followed it home. The exhibits of 2026 arrive in the order the calendar presents them. A mass movement fills the streets of Western capitals under the banner of a free Palestine, and its central chant describes a country emptied from the river to the sea—one state removed from the map, its Jewish population's national existence ended as a matter of program. Its spokesmen explain that anti-Zionism carries no hatred of Jews, and the crowds march behind signs that carry the hatred without the explanation. By the standing definition, the supreme trait, located now in the street left: Hitler. Next in the calendar stands Nick Fuentes and the groyper following he has built under the America First banner, young men whose broadcasts traffic in open contempt for Jews—Holocaust arithmetic mocked, Jewish influence named as the country's disease, the old libels read aloud into a microphone for an audience that grows by the year. By the standing definition, located now in the nationalist right: Hitler. Then the extreme left's settled position: Israel is a colonial artifact to be dismantled, Netanyahu a war criminal to be removed, the Jewish state itself an error the century should correct. By the standing definition, located now in the seminar room and the editorial page: Hitler. The filings differ in stationery. The trait named in each file is the trait the definition selects.

The result is a paradox with the force of arithmetic. Everybody gets to hate Hitler. Everybody gets to be Hitler. The accusation is issued by each camp against the others in the morning and received by each camp from the others by the afternoon, and because the supreme comparison now lands on every address in the country, it delivers its sentence to none of them. A boundary that runs through every camp marks no boundary. George Orwell watched the same inflation consume the word "fascist" while the war still ran: by 1946 he recorded that the word had no meaning left except "something not desirable." Mike Godwin, watching the early internet in 1990, stated the folk version as a law of probability—as a discussion grows longer, the chance of a Hitler comparison approaches certainty. Both men were describing a currency being printed without backing. Print enough of it and the notes still circulate; they simply purchase nothing. The word "Hitler" in 2026 buys no conviction. It is small change now, spent a hundred times a day, and a man informed that he is Hitler files the information with his weather report.

Underneath the filings, though, the stack goes down one level further, and the level at the bottom is load-bearing. With the antisemitism charges stripped away—every one of them, from every direction—the camps still resemble each other in the one conviction none of them examines: that making people do things is an acceptable way of getting things done. It is the premise under every platform. Each faction arrives at the election carrying a list of the people it intends to make do things. Make them pay. Make them hire. Make them sell. Make them stop selling. Make them speak the formula, make them stop speaking it, make them leave, make them stay, make them open their doors, make them close them, make them hand it over, make them take it back. The lists are long and the lists are detailed and the lists are the entire content of the programs. The factions quarrel with genuine hatred over the personnel—over who gets made and who does the making, over whose list is executed first. The making itself is never on the ballot. No faction campaigns on the people it will leave alone, because there is no constituency for leaving people alone, and a platform plank that reads "these citizens will be permitted to live unmade" would be received by every other camp as a confession of unseriousness. Coercion is the water. The arguments are about the temperature.

The premise has a history, and the history has a control group. For a hundred years one American camp ran the opposite experiment: it tried to persuade. The program was a list of loves the country was to be brought to, one at a time—social justice, gayness, transness, blackness, brownness, pagans and witches, sex, abortion, health care. The instruments were the instruments of persuasion, hearts and minds worked through television, cinema, advertising, and fashion, and the method was exemplarism: put the model citizen on the screen and let imitation do the rest. Archie Bunker's son-in-law, the Meathead, argued the new country into existence across a living-room set in the 1970s. The West Wing staffed an entire presidency with exemplars a generation later, decent and brilliant and tireless, governing beautifully in prime time so the audience might learn to want the real thing. The sermons ran in every medium the century invented.

The persuasion failed, and it failed in a specific way: the country took the exemplars as entertainment and rubber-banded back as politics. Every advance that outran the persuaded produced the allergic answer, larger than the advance. Abortion, imposed by a court in 1973 and never carried by persuasion, was stomped back by a court in 2022. Guns answered at double speed: twenty-nine states now run constitutional carry—carry a handgun with no permit at all—and the count has stood at twenty-nine since Louisiana made it so in July 2024. The old strategy had been the frog's: heat low, heat slow, the water warming a degree at a time. The regression is the sound of the frog noticing the pot.

So the camp that had spent a century asking arrived, tired, at the other instrument: we just have to make you. The making is now done in the open, in Western Europe and here, fought hardest around immigration, around trans policy, around men's and women's sports—the fronts where persuasion was conceded lost and compulsion took the field. And then came the confession, in January 2021, when the platforms removed a sitting president of the United States from Twitter and Facebook. They removed me from YouTube. Through COVID the removals ran globally, governments and platforms together, arguments deleted by the million. A ban is a surrender document. You only ban the argument you have stopped believing you can win. The century of persuasion ended with the referees clearing the hall. Had that war on speech never been declared, it would be an entirely different world. The harm runs on the century scale, because trust spent that way compounds like everything else in this stack, and the bill does not expire.

That is the bottom turtle, and it explains the ones above it. A politics built on making people do things requires, at regular intervals, a people whose making is the program—a category of persons whose correction justifies the machinery and whose resistance proves the machinery necessary. The antisemitism now visible on every side is one premise finding its oldest available object, blooming in three directions in the same season. The Jews are the standing candidate for the role in the Western record, the name already in the file, and a civilization that has agreed in advance that somebody must be made will keep reaching for the file it has always reached for. Hitler's actual government was that premise with an army and a filing system: a whole population designated as the ones to be made—made to register, made to sell, made to wear the mark, made to board the trains. The trait the catechism selected, the Jew-hatred, rode on top of the premise the catechism never mentions. Every camp in 2026 holds the premise. Every camp therefore holds the part of Hitler that did the work, whatever its members feel about Jews, and each camp can see the fact clearly—in the others.

In 2026, everybody is Hitler: the word was kept alive for one job, to mark the boundary past which argument ends, and a word that marks everybody marks nobody. Underneath the word, unmarked and unexamined, the premise sits where it has sat all along, holding up the whole stack—everybody's shared conviction that people are material, and that the making of them is what politics is for.

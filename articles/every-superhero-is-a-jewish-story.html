Every Superhero Is a Jewish Story

An article by Chris Abraham, November 23, 2026

This article, read aloud:

Share: X · Facebook · LinkedIn · Reddit · Bluesky · Threads · Email

America's one original mythology was written by the sons of Jewish immigrants, in New York and Cleveland, for ten cents a copy.

Superman came first, in Action Comics No. 1, cover-dated June 1938, created by Jerry Siegel and Joe Shuster, two kids from Cleveland who had met at Glenville High School. The story they told, in thirteen pages sold for $130, runs as follows. A scientist on a dying world puts his infant son in a small ship and fires him at Earth moments ahead of the explosion. A Kansas farmer and his wife find the boy, raise him as Clark Kent, and teach him the manners of the heartland. He grows up carrying a second name under the first one, the name from the lost world—Kal-El, with El sitting in it the way it sits all through Hebrew, in Michael and Daniel and Israel itself, the name of God worn as a suffix by angels and by a people. He can do things no one around him can do. He hides that fact for a living. He wears glasses, slouches, stammers a little, takes the ribbing of the newsroom, and saves the city on his lunch hour under a name the city gave the part of him it could see.

Two teenagers built that out of their own furniture. A baby sent away from a doomed home in a vessel, found and raised by strangers in a foreign house, growing up to reclaim an inheritance he had to keep hidden to survive—Siegel and Shuster never needed to name the source, and mostly they didn't, and the scholars have been naming it ever since, Moses in the bulrushes first among the candidates, the golem of Prague standing behind him, the immigrant boy at Ellis Island behind both. My own read has always been simpler and closer to the bone. Every Jewish kid in America in 1938, and every immigrant's kid of any kind, knew the double name and the double life in his own kitchen. One name for the street and the school roll. One name for home and for God. Power you did not advertise. A lost world your parents described at the table that you would never see, which had made you, entirely, and which explained nothing about Kansas.

Captain America arrived three years later and dropped the coding altogether. Captain America Comics No. 1, cover-dated March 1941, on sale in December 1940, created by Joe Simon and Jack Kirby—Kirby born Jacob Kurtzberg on the Lower East Side—shows the hero in flag colors driving a right hook into the jaw of Adolf Hitler, in a Nazi command post, with Nazi officers diving for cover. Pearl Harbor was nine months in the future. America was formally, loudly, majority-opinion neutral, with an America First movement filling Madison Square Garden and a German American Bund that had filled it before that. Simon said afterward that Captain America was a consciously political creation, that he and Kirby were morally repulsed by Nazi Germany and wanted their say. They got an answer. Hate mail poured into the Timely Comics offices, obscene phone calls, the theme of them death to the Jews, and menacing groups of men took to standing in front of the building on 42nd Street until employees were afraid to go out for lunch. Police protection got posted. Mayor Fiorello La Guardia called personally, on Simon's account, and told them the city of New York would see that no harm came to them. That last detail rests on Simon's own telling, in his memoir of the comics business, and I flag it as a one-source story—but the threats, the mail, and the moment are documented beyond his book, and the cover itself needs no memoir at all. It sits there in four colors. Two Jewish kids from New York declared war on Hitler on a newsstand, at a dime a declaration, while the State Department was still exchanging ambassadors with him.

The pattern holds straight through the industry's family tree. The publishers were Jewish—Martin Goodman at Timely, which became Marvel; Harry Donenfeld and Jack Liebowitz at National, which became DC. The editors and writers and artists were Jewish in proportions that had nothing to do with the country's demographics and everything to do with which doors stood open in 1938: publishing, garment, entertainment, the trades a smart boy could enter without a WASP firm's permission or a university's quota saying otherwise. Stan Lee was Stanley Lieber. Bob Kane, who built Batman with Bill Finger, was Robert Kahn. Will Eisner built the Spirit. Kirby alone co-created half the Marvel universe—the Fantastic Four, Thor, the Hulk, the Avengers, the X-Men—drawing with a cigar in his teeth, a kid from Essex Street who had fought street gangs and then fought in France under Patton, landing after D-Day and drawing reconnaissance maps because somebody found out the private could draw. These men were barred, in living memory, from the country clubs, the white-shoe firms, whole neighborhoods and professions, in the country their parents had crossed an ocean to reach. They responded by inventing that country's gods.

The X-Men made the subtext into the text. Lee and Kirby launched the series in 1963: a school in Westchester County run by Professor Charles Xavier, gathering children born different—mutants, the next step, Homo superior—born with powers that arrive at puberty, unchosen, unhideable in the long run, and feared on sight by the ordinary world. The series settled into its own description of itself, repeated across decades until it became liturgy: sworn to protect a world that hates and fears them. A people set apart by birth, gifted in ways their neighbors resent and fear, tasked in their own founding story with protecting the very population that would cage them if it could, forbidden by their teacher from answering hatred in kind. Chris Claremont, who took the book over in 1975 and wrote it for sixteen years, eventually stopped letting the metaphor do the work alone. He gave Magneto—the X-Men's great enemy, Xavier's friend and mirror—a past in plain fact: Erik Lehnsherr, a survivor of Auschwitz, introduced in flashback in Uncanny X-Men No. 161 in 1982, working in a hospital in Haifa alongside a young Xavier, a man whose entire philosophy was cremated and rebuilt in the camps. After Claremont, no reader could pretend the story was about something else. It had always been about something else, and now it had a number on its arm.

The engine under all of it has a Hebrew name. Tikkun olam—repair of the world. In the liturgy it belongs to God's unfinished work handed to human hands; in American Jewish life it became the practical creed of a people who fixed, built, healed, defended, and taught in a country that had not always wanted them, at rates wildly out of proportion to their numbers. The superhero is tikkun olam in a cape. Power arrives unearned, by birth or accident—a rocket, a serum, a mutation, a radioactive spider—and the genre's entire moral machinery exists to answer one question: what does the powerful person owe the world that gave him nothing? The answer, in story after story, year after year, is everything. He owes it protection without thanks. He owes it mercy toward people who fear him. He owes it restraint, the held punch, the villain webbed to a lamppost for the police rather than dropped off the roof. Avenge yourself and you have stopped being the hero of a Jewish story and become the villain of one—Magneto's whole tragedy, stated in one line. The wound is real. The wound explains him. The wound licenses nothing.

My last name is Abraham, and I have stood a half-step outside that door my whole life, as an admirer. I grew up being treated as if I were Jewish by a world that reads a name off a roster and decides, and I never once corrected the impression with any energy, because the company it put me in was company I was proud to be mistaken for. Before Hawaii—ages zero to six, in the years before my parents moved us to Honolulu—they could only get me into Jewish preschools. Those were the doors that opened. My first friend was a boy named Lewis Goldberg. At George Washington University I pledged Phi Kappa Psi and my pledge name was "Zeus," which the brothers considered a joke about the name and the beard and the general Old Testament weather around me, and I wore it happily. In my late teens I read Art Spiegelman's Maus—a comic book, note, the supposedly disposable form, carrying the Holocaust in panels of mice and cats—and it rearranged my furniture permanently, one of the most influential books of my life, sitting on the same shelf in my head as the serious novels and doing more work than most of them.

I am a total Zionist, self-described, and I support Israel fully—the one state on earth founded on the proposition that the story does not end with the camps, that a people rocketed out of a dying world gets to build a home and defend it and be ordinary in it, which is the most radical ending the twentieth century produced. And my admiration runs wider than one state. Jews are about two percent of America. Two percent produced an outsized share of this country's science, its law, its medicine, its comedy—and its one exported mythology, the superhero, the story every child on earth now grows up inside, from Lagos to Jakarta to São Paulo, whether or not that child has ever met a Jew or could find Israel on a map. I have called Jews the only American superpower, in conversation, for years, and I mean it as arithmetic wearing a compliment's clothes. Superpowers are measured by what the world cannot imagine itself without. The world can no longer imagine itself without this story.

The story, at bottom, is a discipline disguised as a fantasy. Every kid who ever wore a towel as a cape learned the moves before he learned the words for them: you are stronger than the people around you in some way they can see and some ways they can't, the difference will cost you, hiding it will cost you too, and the power settles nothing until you decide what it is for. Siegel and Shuster decided at eighteen, at a kitchen table in Cleveland, with Europe already burning in the newsreels and their own fathers' generation carrying the old names and the old memories into American ground. They decided it was for the protection of the world that made the deciding necessary.

Kal-El still flies. The Kansas parents still get the last word on who he turned out to be.

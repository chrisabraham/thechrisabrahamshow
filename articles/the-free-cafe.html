The Free Café

An article by Chris Abraham, November 21, 2026

This article, read aloud:

Share: X · Facebook · LinkedIn · Reddit · Bluesky · Threads · Email

Free is the most destructive price a neighborhood can be offered, when the free is paid for by money made somewhere else. The pet project—the store carried by an outside fortune, with no need for its own till to balance—does not join a local economy. It eats the economy's shops one by one, and then it leaves, and the street it fed on is a desert.

The story that was not true

A story went around this year about a famous actress. In the telling, she had been paid $55 million for a single movie, and she took the money and opened a café where everything was free—free coffee, free food, a seat for anybody who wanted one, no till at all. The actress in the story was Margot Robbie. The story is not true. There is no such café, and there never was.

The fabrication matters less than the applause. Millions of people passed the story along because it described the world as they want it arranged: a fortune, finally, spent correctly. Nothing in this world needs to be true. It needs to be responded to. The café is fiction; the longing is a fact, and the longing deserves an answer, because a free café built in earnest would do exactly what its admirers never picture. It would close every café around it.

Picture the street while it lives. The free café has the line out the door, because a line is what free builds. The café across the street, the one with the till, watches its morning rush—the two hours that pay its rent—standing in a queue for coffee that costs nothing. Its regulars are not traitors. They are shoppers, and shoppers pay as little as possible too.

Nobody pays in proportion

Everybody pays as little as possible. The poor man pays as little as possible, and the billionaire pays as little as possible, and the sameness is a defect in neither man. A wage is not a percentage of the payer's wealth. Nobody with money opens a ledger, totals a fortune, and pays a share of it for a cup of coffee or an hour of work. The price of the work is set by the next person willing to do the work. The price of the coffee is set by the shop that can sell it cheapest and stay alive.

A working café's prices are that arithmetic made visible: beans, rent, wages, and the thin remainder that keeps the door open another month. Every shop on the street lives inside the same arithmetic. The pet café lives outside it. Its losses are settled in a bank account in another city, out of money earned in another trade, and its prices can fall to zero because its prices were never carrying anything.

Loyalty does not repeal the arithmetic. A neighborhood will tell a survey it loves the local shop, and then it will buy where the price is lower. The love is genuine. The buying is arithmetic, and the arithmetic always collects.

The gallery standard

The finished form of the species hangs in Aspen. The fancy gallery there—the rich divorcée's gallery, kept by a woman whose settlement pays the rent—has beautiful walls, serious prices, and no expectation that the art will ever cover the costs. The till is a decoration. The gallery is a room its owner enjoys owning, and it can stay open forever on those terms, because what pays its rent is a divorce decree.

I grew up in Hawaii, and I knew the gallery scene there. I do not believe any of those galleries made money as galleries. The ones that showed money, friends in that world say, made it as laundries: a painting's price is an opinion, and an opinion is the cleanest thing in the world to wash money through. Hang a canvas, price it by declaration, sell it to the right buyer, and dirty money comes out the other side as art commerce.

The taxonomy is short. A gallery survives with a fortune under it, or a laundry inside it, or it does not survive. The beauty is the furnishing, in every case.

The retiree army

The cast is wider than actresses and divorcées. Every town has the bored retiree: a business sold, a pension banked, decades of energy with nowhere to go. He opens the storefront he always dreamed of and runs it as a gift. He does the work himself for free, hires help at wages the market never quoted, prices below his own costs, and gives the product away when the mood takes him. He feels generous, and by his lights he is.

His red ink does not frighten him, and the reason is the tax return. The losses run against his other income, so a share of every dollar the shop bleeds comes back to him at tax time. The pet project is subsidized twice: once by the fortune behind it, and again by the Treasury. The working owner across the street is subsidized zero times. His red ink is only red ink, and it kills his shop on schedule.

The retiree's own ending is the bike shop's ending with a pension behind it. He ages, or his health turns, or a grandchild is born two states away, and the storefront he ran as a gift closes the way a gift ends—suddenly, and with no successor, because a gift cannot be sold. The street keeps the vacancy.

The bike shops

The proof of the mechanism is the bike shop, and there are a million of them. The argument was made to me this week, and it is the strongest form of the case, because every town has watched the whole cycle happen.

An enthusiast with money made elsewhere opens the bike shop of his dreams in a town that already has two. His stock is deeper, his discounts are fatter, his mechanics are paid above the going rate, and his repairs are priced like favors. He can afford all of it for the simplest of reasons: the shop does not have to pay for itself.

The working shops cannot answer him. Their prices are arithmetic, and arithmetic loses to a subsidy every day of the week. The first one closes. Then the second. The town's riders all shop at the pet store by then, and nobody marks the moment, because the prices were wonderful and the man was so clearly one of the good ones.

Then the owner gets bored. The shop was a feeling, and feelings rotate. He closes the doors, and there is no buyer for the business, because a shop that never made money is worth nothing as a business. The town ends with zero bike shops. The real ones are dead, the pet one is a memory, and the riders drive forty miles for an inner tube.

The municipal form

New York has elected to run the same machine at city scale. Mayor Zohran Mamdani's program includes city-owned grocery stores—five of them, one in each borough—and City Hall has allocated $70 million to establish them. The stores carry the pet project's own design, stated proudly by their authors: no rent to pay, no property taxes, no profit to make, buying and selling at wholesale. The promised discount on the shelf is the subsidy with a price tag hung on it. His fare-free bus promise runs on the same principle: the fare is abolished, the cost moves to the tax bill, and the service answers to politics from then on.

The bodega across the street from a city store pays rent. It pays its suppliers, it pays its taxes, and it marks up its goods by the margin that keeps its lights on. It cannot compete with a store excused from arithmetic. The city store does not have to be better. It has to be subsidized, and it is.

The bodega's markup was never pure profit either. It paid for the six-in-the-morning opening, the midnight closing, the tab carried for the regular whose check lands Friday, the watch kept on the block by a man who knows every face on it. The city store's discount prices none of that, because the city store sells groceries. The bodega was selling groceries and tenure on the street, in one receipt.

Then the politics turn, as politics does. Administrations end, budgets tighten, and a store with no profit has no defenders when the cutting starts. It closes quietly, in a budget line, years after the bodegas it undercut have become delis, then vacancies, then a bank branch. This time the desert is public policy, drawn up in advance.

The bill

The sequence is always the same. Outside money arrives wearing generosity. Prices collapse below the cost of staying alive. The working shops die in order of their honesty. The money loses interest, or loses an election, or loses the fortune, and departs. The street is left with neither the gift nor the economy the gift destroyed.

Free is the most expensive price a neighborhood ever pays.

The Truck Stops Itself, or It Doesn't Stop

An article by Chris Abraham, October 28, 2026

This article, read aloud:

Share: X · Facebook · LinkedIn · Reddit · Bluesky · Threads · Email

A buddy of mine on Facebook, from Boston, posted the memo this morning. I call it the memo because I have read it a hundred times this year, in the same order, in nearly the same words, from a hundred different mouths. It opens with the father's line, delivered at a press conference on Tuesday: "Do your job and stop shielding Alex's killers." Then the biography. Then the video. Then the demand. My buddy types it out as if it had just occurred to him, and maybe it has, every time. Here it is, line by line, with the answers the memo never waits for.

The verdict arrives first

Start where he starts: "Alex's killers." A killing is a fact. A killer is a verdict. The investigation the memo spends forty lines demanding exists to decide the very thing its first line has already decided, and a man who needs the conclusion printed before the inquiry begins does not want an investigation. He wants a ceremony. I commented on the post with the whole of my position, and I will keep coming back to it: facts don't matter as much as law. Facts are exhibits. Law is the machine that weighs them. A perfect video, shot from four angles, in daylight, still has to be carried into a courtroom and fed into a legal test—justification, authority, reasonableness—or it decides nothing at all. "Let the facts decide what happens next" is a sentence for people who have never watched facts sit in an evidence locker while lawyers argue about what they mean.

The noble story

Alex Pretti was 37. He was an ICU nurse at the Minneapolis VA. He cared for veterans. He was an American citizen. My buddy recites the biography the way the memo always recites it, as if goodness were armor, and I grant every word of it: a good man, a useful life, a family that loved him. None of it was visible on that street in January. None of it appears anywhere in the legal test. And none of it should. If those agents shot a man they had no right to shoot, the wrong is identical had he been a drifter in the country for a week, and a principle that needs a nurse, a flag, and a résumé to activate is not a principle. It is casting.

Citizenship is paper

I never understand why people care that he was a citizen. Citizenship means more human? More worthy of being immune from putting oneself between federal agents and their mission? A passport is paper. A carry permit is paper. Danger doesn't care about someone's nationality; danger and risk don't care about your noble story. These statuses live in courtrooms, in statutes, in headlines—in every place except the half-second in which men are grappling in a street and somebody sees a gun. I made this argument at length in ICE Protest Tactics and the Counterinsurgency Mismatch: citizenship is armor, real armor, and it keeps you off the plane. It does nothing for you inside a perimeter.

The video, and what it cannot show

The memo's evidentiary core: bystander video showed Alex holding a phone, helping another protester who had been pushed to the ground. He was carrying a handgun. Legally. No publicly available video has shown him brandishing it or attacking officers before he was shot. All true, as far as it goes, and it goes about half an inch. Legal carry looks like illegal carry during melee. The permit was in his pocket, or his wallet, or a database in St. Paul; the gun was in the scrum, where a gun is a gun and nobody has ever been calmed by the paperwork attached to one. And "helping a protester" is the memo's tender verb for the act at the center of the case: he put himself between federal agents and the work they were there to do. Zero people should die during boring administrative deportation operations. It's the intervention that kills. I worked the odds of that intervention out in Protest Russian Roulette: every spin is fresh, the cylinder has a floor of five chambers in America because the Constitution and the agents' restraint hold it there, and the cylinder doesn't know you are right. It does not know you are a poet, or a nurse, or a mother. It only knows the count.

They aren't police

Let me be clear about my own side of this, because the memo's readers will misread it on purpose: I am not saying it was good policing. They aren't really police. A police officer's work ends in a courtroom, before a judge, with a defense lawyer across the aisle and a jury that can say no. An extraction team's work ends at a departure gate, and its success is a number counted in Washington. Protesting federal agent armed extraction missions is dangerous because the missions are military operations against foreign nationals—domestic war operations, administrative in name alone—and they open with a tool that has a name of its own: shock and awe. Intimidation and humiliation are tools, used deliberately to take control of a chaotic street before the street takes control of the operation. Five or six armored vehicles sent to collect one landscaper read as waste only to people who think the audience is the landscaper. The audience is everyone on the sidewalk, and the show is priced to make the next street cheaper than this one.

Two different movies

The protesters have cast ICE as Gomer Pyle: the bumbling Mayberry deputy, harmless, folksy, bound by sitcom rules, one "well, goll-ly" away from folding. You can shame Gomer. You can surround Gomer, blow a whistle in his face, park across his street, and wait for him to radio Barney and stand down. The men inside the perimeter are living in another movie entirely. Their working day, so to speak, is Black Hawk Down on a loop: roll into a neighborhood the operation reads as hostile terrain, seize the named men on the list, and get out before the street organizes against you. The targets live embedded in a population that warns them, hides them, and films the hunters—in Minneapolis the Somali community around Cedar-Riverside is the sea the fish swim in, and every phone on every corner is an early-warning system with a livestream. One side believes it is heckling a sitcom deputy. The other side believes it is running recoveries in Mogadishu with worse weather. Only one cast is armed, and the tragedy keeps writing itself in the gap between the two scripts.

Collateral damage

War grammar keeps a drawer ready for Alex Pretti, and the word on the drawer is collateral damage: a man who was never the target, destroyed inside the perimeter of an operation aimed at somebody else. The memo cannot afford that word. Collateral damage is a judgment about an operation, and the memo needs a judgment about murder, preferably pre-printed. I offer the word coldly, not kindly. A country that runs domestic war operations under administrative labels should be made to speak the war's own vocabulary out loud, in public, and then decide—by law, in the open—whether the filing stands.

The parents

The truest thing in the post is Michael and Susan Pretti. "It has been a miserable year for our family," his mother said on Tuesday, standing beside a folded flag. "All we want is justice for Alex, and that the federal government treat us with the dignity and respect that our son deserves." I believe them, entirely, and their grief still is not evidence. The state owes these people a lawful process, conducted in the open, with real answers at the end of it. It cannot owe them a verdict in advance, and sorrow does not get to set the standard of proof. A system that convicts on grief will acquit on grief too, the first time the sympathetic face is on the other side of the podium.

The court case is the point

Now the part of the story my buddy's memo treats as pure scandal, and I treat as the one functioning adult in the room. Hennepin County Attorney Mary Moriarty opened a criminal investigation and, in March, sued the Trump administration for the evidence in the Pretti and Renee Good killings. In July the federal government began handing over what Moriarty herself called voluminous material—body camera video, hard drives of statements. Then the transfer stopped: on September 10 the U.S. Attorney's Office told her the sharing was paused indefinitely, "in light of challenges with D.C." The state is back in front of a federal judge in Washington, asking for an order. The Justice Department answers that it is under no obligation to release further evidence while the state has initiated no proceedings of its own, and that the timing matches how these investigations proceed. Read that again, slowly. Two governments are fighting over officers' phones and interview recordings, and the fight is happening in a courtroom, under rules, before a judge. That is the investigation. It is already the thing the memo demands, running on law instead of on volume. "Release it, all of it" is not a legal instrument. Nobody in that courtroom will ever say it. It is a request addressed to the federal government's conscience, and requests addressed to consciences are the whole strategy—on the street in January, and on Facebook this morning.

Jumping in front of a truck

Protest-based resistance demands that the trucks and the agents stop themselves. That is the entire mechanism. A man steps into the road in front of a moving truck and the truck is supposed to brake out of respect for the stepping. Can you stop a truck? Or does the truck have to stop itself? A truck stops when its driver's foot moves, and a movement that can only succeed by converting the driver has built its whole theory of change out of the other side's conscience. When the driver shares the catechism, shame works, sometimes. When he doesn't, the theory has no second move. It has only a bigger body in the road. I wrote the convoy version of this in The Sheepdog Sometimes Has to Bite: no mission lets interference stop the column moving forward, a stopped column is the most vulnerable thing on the street, and the column knows it. The truck in this essay and the column in that one are the same machine wearing different paint.

Where trucks actually stop

Whether my buddy believes every line of the memo or has simply learned which lines a man posts to be seen believing them, I cannot tell from Boston, and it no longer matters: the memo writes itself now, and its authors are interchangeable. The real contest sits in a federal courtroom in Washington and a county attorney's office in Minneapolis, and it will be settled the way the shooting itself will be settled—by law, applied to facts, in rooms where citizenship, permits, biographies, and sorrows are read into a record instead of shouted at a windshield. If you want the truck stopped, that record is the brake pedal. It always was.

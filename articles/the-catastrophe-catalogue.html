The Catastrophe Catalogue

An article by Chris Abraham, November 4, 2026

Share: X · Facebook · LinkedIn · Reddit · Bluesky · Threads · Email

The count finished at 222 House seats called for the Democrats and a Senate at 54 to 44, with Alaska and Mississippi still out. The new Congress sits on January 3, 2027. Donald Trump remains president until January 20, 2029. Between those dates lie 748 days, and every one of them belongs to a government at war with itself: a president holding the veto, the pardon, and the armed forces, and a Congress holding the subpoena, the appropriation, and the confirmation. What follows is the catalogue of what the second set of weapons does to the first, itemized—probable first, possible second, impossible last. The paperwork for most of it already exists in committee offices, drafted during the years in opposition.

What a gavel is

A committee chairmanship is three powers wearing one title. The chair sets the hearing calendar, signs the subpoenas, and decides which transcripts get published and when. In the House, a committee majority can compel testimony and documents without a single vote from the other party. In the Senate, the same majority also controls the floor schedule and every nomination. On January 3, every gavel in both buildings changes hands at once, and the machinery they run starts the same morning.

The subpoena census. Every department, agency, and White House office receives its document demand inside the first ninety days: schedules, messages, call logs, visitor records, decision memos. The 2019 White House answered with a blanket refusal and litigated for years. This Congress watched that defense work, priced the delay into its calendar, and starts its lawsuits the same week it sends its letters.

The tax returns. The Ways and Means Committee requests the president's returns under 26 U.S.C. § 6103(f), the statute that lets the tax-writing committees inspect any return on written demand. The 2019 request took three years of litigation to produce. The legal question has since been litigated to its end. This request goes out in the first week, and the returns, once in committee hands, enter the Congressional Record whenever the majority chooses.

The confirmation freeze. A Senate majority is a veto over every judge, cabinet secretary, ambassador, and agency head the president names for the rest of his term. Vacancies stay vacant. Acting officials serve out their statutory shelf life under the Vacancies Act and then become hearing exhibits themselves. Pro forma sessions, the device the Senate used against Barack Obama, close the recess-appointment door, and the courts have already blessed the lock.

The money. Twelve appropriations bills become twelve negotiations, each with a hostage inside. Border construction is zeroed. Immigration enforcement is conditioned line by line. Justice Department components are funded with riders naming the offices the majority wants starved or fed. Every shutdown deadline on the calendar—there are several a year, plus the debt limit—is a scheduled crisis with the president cast as the man who can end it by signing. The power of the purse is the one congressional weapon the veto cannot fully answer, because a government that is not funded does not run, and the blame gets assigned on television.

The war

The war with Iran has been run from the White House on claimed authority and continuing resolutions. It now acquires a second management. The War Powers Resolution's clocks, dormant as a practical matter for decades, get wound in public: reports demanded on a schedule, a concurrent resolution directing the removal of forces, funding conditioned on certifications the administration cannot honestly sign. The statute has been treated as a dead letter by presidents of both parties. The majority's use for the statute is plainer: the title of a hearing series, and a roll call the president must answer in public every few months.

The commanders testify under oath, on camera, with casualty figures and cost accounting that the Pentagon will be ordered to produce weekly. Arms transfers face disapproval resolutions that the president vetoes, and each veto is a roll call the majority schedules for maximum daylight. The war's supporters in his own coalition already blame it for the midterms—the vice president said so on the record—and the hearings give that blame a gavel and a witness chair. Wars survive hostile Congresses. The funding fights of 2007 are the recent map. But every month of the war now runs through an appropriations process owned by the people who ran against it.

The revenge docket

Impeachment, the third. An inquiry is authorized by summer at the latest, and the articles are drafted from the 2019 and 2021 templates with new counts added: the pardons, the war, the emoluments, the pressure on prosecutors. The House needs a simple majority and has one. The Senate needs 67 votes to convict and will have at most 56. The trial proceeds anyway, because the process is the punishment and the acquittal is priced in from the first day. The deliverable is six weeks of sworn testimony in an election cycle, with the chief justice presiding over a verdict everyone can read in advance.

The cabinet queue. Impeachment resolutions against the attorney general and the secretary of homeland security follow the presidential articles as a matter of course. The historical record offers no comfort to either side: one cabinet officer has been impeached in 250 years—William Belknap, in 1876, acquitted—and the Senate dismissed the Mayorkas articles in an afternoon in 2024. The record does not slow the filings. Each one is a week of hearings with the officer's name in the headline and the word impeached attached to it permanently, whatever the vote.

The pardons. The pardon power is the one presidential weapon no court can review, so Congress investigates around it. Every January 6 pardon and every pardon with a donor in its family tree gets a file: recipients subpoenaed to testify about how their names traveled, intermediaries deposed, the pardon office's recommendation memos demanded. The power itself stays untouched. The market around it gets a klieg light and a transcript.

The family and the firm. The Trump Organization's records, the lender files, the foreign-money trails: the 2019 subpoenas to his accountants and banks are the map, and the litigation over them has already been run to the Supreme Court and back. Emoluments suits revive in friendlier postures. The crypto ventures, the coin, the media company's books—each becomes a committee exhibit series, because a president who kept his businesses has handed the opposition a second government to investigate, one with no executive privilege at all.

The ballroom. On election day the president was photographed at the construction site of his White House ballroom, a project built with private money from donors, on grounds the public owns. It is the perfect hearing property: photographs, donors, a wrecking ball, and a preservation statute. Expect the donor list demanded under subpoena, the preservationists in the witness chairs, and the renderings entered as exhibits. The majority could not have commissioned a better set piece, and it did not have to. He built it for them.

The states, amplified. Congress assembles, publishes, and hands over; prosecution belongs to the states. Criminal referrals travel from committee letterheads to attorneys general with the evidence bound and indexed, and the state files—the Manhattan and Albany models—reopen as joint productions with better research staff. Statutes of limitation get calendared in public against January 2029. The state cases were the first round of the lawfare war. The second round arrives with subpoena power behind it.

The lawyers. Bar complaints and disbarment campaigns against the administration's attorneys proceed as attrition warfare: every government lawyer who signs a filing acquires a personal docket, defended at personal expense, against committees that bill by the taxpayer. Winning the complaints is incidental. The mechanism is price: raise the cost of serving until the recruiting calls stop being returned.

The 25th Amendment campaign

Section 4 of the 25th Amendment has two doors. Through the first, the vice president and a majority of the cabinet declare the president unable to serve, and the vice president takes the chair. Through the second, a body created by Congress makes the same declaration alongside the vice president. If the president contests the finding, Congress decides, and sustaining the removal takes two-thirds of both houses inside twenty-one days. Those are the mechanics. The campaign that will be run under the amendment's name has almost nothing to do with them.

The campaign runs on paper and television. A presidential-capacity commission bill, filed in earlier Congresses and updated for this one, gets a hearing in the first session and a floor vote in the House, where it passes. Open letters from psychiatrists arrive on a quarterly schedule, the profession's old restraint about diagnosing public figures having been retired in the first term. Every Sunday program puts the question to the vice president's allies: will he do his duty. Every cough at a rally, every slurred word at a podium, every hospital visit acquires a panel of doctors on cable by nightfall.

The arithmetic is public and total. The cabinet officers serve at the president's pleasure and owe their jobs to his voters. The vice president's entire future is mortgaged to the president's coalition, which would read his signature on a Section 4 declaration as the century's betrayal. Two-thirds of both houses means scores of Republican members voting to remove their own president on a medical theory advanced by the opposition. The declaration will never be signed. The vote would never be close. Everyone staffing the campaign can do this arithmetic, and the campaign proceeds on schedule anyway, because its product is the caption: the word unfit, typeset under his photograph, refreshed weekly for two years, until the association is set in the public mind like type in a forme.

The tail risk inside the theater is a real event. A fall, a diagnosis, a night in a hospital converts the campaign into procedure in one news cycle, with the commission bill already drafted, the letter signers already assembled, and the argument already rehearsed for two years on television. The campaign's standing function is to be there, pre-built, on that morning. Until then it is a drumbeat. On that morning it is a government.

The tail risks

The filibuster. A Senate majority of 54 to 56 seats, a two-year clock, and a president who cannot be legislated around: the argument for ending the legislative filibuster returns in January and gets stronger with every blocked bill. Behind the rule change stands the real catalogue. District of Columbia statehood, worth two Senate seats in perpetuity. A federal elections act rewriting registration, districting, and campaign money for all fifty states. A court-expansion bill seating thirteen justices, its number drawn from the circuits. Each passes the House on party lines. Each sits one Senate head-count away from the president's veto, and head-counts move during sieges, because the members being counted are also the members being campaigned at.

A conviction, somewhere. No president has been convicted by the Senate. No cabinet officer has been convicted either, in two and a half centuries of trying. The articles being drafted this winter will be written by people who know that list by heart and regard it as a draft. American institutional records had a long half-life once. The recent schedule of firsts—indictments of a former president, a candidate raided, a speaker deposed by his own conference—suggests the half-life has shortened.

The seating fights. The House judges the elections and qualifications of its own members, by majority vote, and contested races get decided accordingly. Every disputed seat in the new House is a small constitutional crisis with a foreordained ending, and the majority that seats its own claimants in January governs with them all term.

January 20, 2029. The catalogue's last page is a calendar. At noon on that date the immunity of the office expires, and the state charges that no presidential pardon can reach—the pardon power stops at the federal line—become filable against a private citizen with a large legal exposure and a documented record, much of it assembled under oath by the Congress described above. Every prosecutor who wants him has the date pinned up. He has it too. The final two years of his presidency will be governed, on both sides, by that appointment.

The term

Add it up and the shape is plain. A president with two years left and no reëlection to win faces a Congress with two years of subpoenas, appropriations, confirmations, hearings, articles, and campaigns to spend, and a base that elected it for exactly this spending. He keeps the veto, the pardon, the military, and the bully pulpit. They keep everything that makes a presidency legible: the paper, the money, the calendar, and the record. The ratchet described on these pages before—each round's instruments inherited by the next round's hands—now turns with both chambers behind it.

The gavels change hands on January 3. The list above is what they are for.

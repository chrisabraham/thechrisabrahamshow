The Price of the Display

An article by Chris Abraham, November 1, 2026

Share: X · Facebook · LinkedIn · Reddit · Bluesky · Threads · Email

Nobody has ever met you. They have met your name on a page, your voice on a phone, your clothes in a doorway, your hair across a desk. The person himself stays home. Every room you will ever enter runs a filter on the evidence that does arrive, and the filter decides in seconds, from surfaces, before you have said anything that matters. The world trades in signals because signals are all that travel.

My best friend Mark Harrison, my personal guru, laid down the law under this years ago. Chris, he said, you act as if people could read your thoughts, as though every thought you have floats over your head in a thought bubble. People cannot read your thoughts. Every thought you have is your own. And most people are so self-centered they would never notice the bubbles anyway, because people barely notice other people. When they do notice, they have already decided to spend the attention. Everyone else is functionary, optional, and most of the time invisible. Mark was correcting my vanity. He was also describing the entire moral economy. What a man is has no public existence. What he does and what he displays are his whole public record, because they are the only parts of him that leave the skull.

Loud and proud is a resignation letter

Here is the center of it. A generation has been taught to load its display layer with everything the surrounding culture declines to value, and to call the loading pride. Loud and proud, on every surface at once: your words on Facebook and Threads and Bluesky, your face on TikTok and YouTube, your threads and tweets at full volume. Loud and proud in the flesh: the face jewelry, the blue hair, the red baseball cap, the tattoo on the face and the tattoo on the body. Loud and proud in the voice: the accent worn like a banner, the speech that signifies your education or your refusal of education to every ear in the room. Each item is a signifier aimed at the reader's dictionary, and the reader's dictionary prices it on arrival. Pride in what the market discounts is a way of resigning from the competitive pool before the competition starts. You signify yourself unhireable in your own hand, at full volume, and call the volume a virtue.

It sounds like a trick the power elite would design if they were allowed to design one: persuade the strivers to disqualify themselves, voluntarily, proudly, in public, permanently, and to experience the disqualification as self-respect. Call it a trick or call it weather. The beneficiary is identical either way. The incumbent never has to guard the gate. The applicants queue up to stamp their own rejections.

Behavior is the only courtroom

Being racist and behaving racist are two different things. I wrote that this morning under a stranger's meme about a grandfather excused by his era, and I will stand on it here. The old man may carry whatever weather he carries inside. Inside is his own country. The courtroom with jurisdiction is the room he is standing in, and the evidence admitted there is conduct: how he treats the people in front of him, today, at this table. Curtail the behavior and the being becomes a private matter between a man and his God, which is where it always belonged.

This is also the honest account of what changed in my lifetime. Stated attitudes moved some—Gallup's long series on approval of interracial marriage runs from 4 percent in 1958 to 94 percent in 2021. Behavior moved enormously, because behavior is what gets billed. People learned the ramifications of the act—the job, the contract, the friendship, the clip—and the acts grew rare in public long before the interiors were renovated. Deterrence did most of that work wearing conversion's clothes. I count that a victory anyway. A civilization that can only police conduct should police conduct well and leave souls to their owners.

Two kinds of evidence

The filter reads two classes of signal, and confusing them is where most arguments about it go wrong.

The first class can be taken off. The nose ring, the purple hair, the red hat, the face tattoo, the flag of any tribe worn into an interview room. Keeping a removable signal on at the moment of judgment is itself a behavior, and it transmits clearly: this display matters more to me than your comfort, expect it every day, price me with it attached. Offices read that transmission accurately, in every political direction at once. They filter for purple hair and red hats with the same hand, because the filter hunts friction, and any flag flown at full mast in a ritual of deference announces friction in advance.

The second class cannot be taken off. A name. A voice. The vowels a childhood installed. Here the same filter stops measuring behavior and starts measuring ancestry, and its accuracy collapses. Two economists—Marianne Bertrand and Sendhil Mullainathan—once sent out identical résumés differing only in the name at the top—Emily and Greg against Lakisha and Jamal—and the white names drew half again as many callbacks. The conduct on the page was word for word the same. Nothing had been displayed. Nothing had been done. The filter had read a birth certificate and called the reading a judgment of character. Any honest account of the machine prints that result in full, because it convicts the machine: a filter built to predict behavior will happily convict on blood when blood is the cheapest data on the page.

A decade later the adaptation was documented from the applicant's side. Sonia Kang and her co-authors found that minority candidates who whitened their résumés—names anglicized, racial cues scrubbed—raised their callbacks sharply, roughly doubling them for Asian applicants, and that employers advertising their commitment to diversity discounted the unwhitened résumé at the same rate as everyone else. The filter had not changed. The signals had. Camouflage, measured under laboratory conditions, works exactly as the field reports say it does.

Between the two classes sits the accent, half removable, and the tax it collects is paid by everyone whose vowels come from the wrong county, the wrong country, or the wrong side of the tracks in the same city. Too Southern pays it. Too Black pays it. The phone voice is a real technology and the people who master it are not sellouts. They are translators, and translation is a sovereign skill—until the moment it is demanded as tribute, at which point it becomes a confession that the hierarchy owns your mouth.

The leverage deck

Underneath the friction filter sits a colder one. A manager fears the hire who arrives fluent in the vocabularies of grievance—the race card, the gender card, the trans card, the assault card, the whole deck—because a claim costs the company the same fortune to defeat as to lose, and the process is the punishment either way. Human resources is the department that manages this exposure. HR is not the employee's friend. HR is the owner's immune system, and an immune system never asks which cell was right. It asks which cell endangers the body.

The filter built on that fear screens for knowledge, and knowledge is a scar's souvenir. The woman who knows exactly how an assault claim works usually learned it the expensive way. Screen out everyone who knows where the courthouse is and you have built a workforce of the never-harmed and the permanently quiet, which is a comfortable place to work until the day the building needs somebody willing to name a wrong in the system's own language. The grievance and the whistleblowing are the same muscle. An office that filters the muscle out entirely has insured itself against complaints and against rescue with the same policy.

The frog wardrobe

Nature solved the display problem long before we did, and her taxonomy is exact. The true poison dart frog advertises honestly: bright colors, real chemistry, a mercy to both parties, because the predator who reads the signal correctly never has to learn the lesson in his stomach. Then there is the frog dressed as a dart frog with no poison in the usual sense—except the metaphorical kind, which is real. I have baggage. I have trauma. I carry a diagnosis and a pharmacology. I have unresolved business with my father and with my family. I am angry at a society that has made me feel misunderstood, disrespected, and invisible. Read in first person, that inventory is a warning label on genuine contents. Contact will be expensive. The poison is social and cultural rather than chemical, and it prices the meal exactly the same way.

And then there is camouflage, the oldest strategy of all: hip hop in the bedroom, penny loafer in the interview room, Nike trainer in your own life. Erving Goffman gave the wardrobe its scholarly vocabulary in Stigma (1963): passing, when the discrediting signal is concealed outright, and covering, when it is merely turned down so the room can relax. Camouflage is what the powerless have always worn into rooms owned by other people. It works because the filter reads surfaces, and a quiet surface gets read as a quiet man.

Pride goeth before the fall for a mechanical reason. Display is a claim of power, and a claim invites an assay. The louder the display, the more certain the assay, and the assay is run by people with every incentive to find the claim unbacked. The officer's kit passes the assay because the state stands behind it. The face tattoo at the bank interview is the same grammar with nothing standing behind it at all. It is a warning color wrapped around an invoice, and the invoice is presented to the wearer.

The sign trade

The scholars of signs mapped all of this a century ago, downstream of a Swiss linguist named Ferdinand de Saussure, whose Geneva lectures survive as the Course in General Linguistics. A sign is a signifier and a signified, a surface and the meaning assigned to it, and the link between them is arbitrary—convention, nothing more. The French who inherited Saussure pushed the point until it squealed: the signified never arrives, the definition of every word is more words, meaning defers itself down an endless chain of signifiers. Jean Baudrillard followed the chain into the media age and reported its end state in Simulacra and Simulation: the signs detach from any original and circulate among themselves, copies with no originals left to consult. The hiring hall got there before the philosophers finished writing it down. Candidates are not rejected. Chains of signs are rejected, in a reader's dictionary the candidate has never seen.

Henry Louis Gates Jr. found the counter-art preserved in folklore and named it in The Signifying Monkey. The Monkey survives the Lion through signifyin(g): indirection, repetition with a signal difference, speech doubled so the powerful hear one text and the home crowd hears the second one underneath. Signification—the master's plain, stable, denotative meaning—shouts in one dictionary. Signifyin(g) works two dictionaries at once and never raises its voice. That contrast is the whole strategy question of a life lived under filters. Loud and proud is signification at maximum volume, addressed to a dictionary that prices it as friction. The Monkey chose the other art. He feeds the reader a surface, keeps his meanings in escrow, and walks out of the jungle with everything he came for.

I make my living inside this machinery, and I might as well say so in an essay about it. Perception management is applied semiotics. Clients pay me because the world reads a man's first page of results, his profiles, his displayed layer—and never meets the man. I have spent twenty years tending the gap between what people are and what their signs report. Some days the work feels like translation. Some days it feels like camouflage with an invoice attached. It is always the same work: the signified stays home, and the signifiers go out to be priced.

I run the experiment on myself, by the way, in public, continuously. Across my profiles the name mutates by pond—full Hungarian diacritics here, a mock knighthood there, plain and unadorned in the biggest room—under a banner of Princess Diana firing a submachine gun, above a bio that refuses to summarize me in two languages at once. I renovated my own name at full volume, in capital letters, in front of forty thousand followers. The number of people who have ever commented on it is zero. Mark's law, field-tested: attention is allocated, never given, and nobody's salary depends on reading me. Display is free exactly where nobody is paid to watch. It is priced exactly where somebody is.

I post controversial things under my own name and I know what they cost. They cost me standing in genteel society, every time, in exact proportion to their voltage. I pay it because my display is backed: my living is a reputation for saying the priced thing, and my clients hire the frog for being a frog. The kid at the bank window copying my volume with none of my backing is not brave. He is unarmed in the same posture, and posture was never the part that protected me.

So here is the whole ethic, and it fits in one breath. Your interior is yours; nobody can read it and nobody is owed it. Your behavior is the only testimony the courtroom admits, so make it testimony you can sign. Your display is a claim, so claim only what you can cash. And when you switch—when you translate, when you camouflage, when you put the penny loafers on—do it as a sovereign spending a signal on purpose, never as a subject paying tribute to a dictionary somebody else wrote. The signs will be read regardless. The only freedom on offer is authorship.

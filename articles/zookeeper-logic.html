Zookeeper Logic

An article by Chris Abraham, November 15, 2026

This article, read aloud:

Share: X · Facebook · LinkedIn · Reddit · Bluesky · Threads · Email

Zookeeper logic is the reasoning of the keeper, and the keeper is sincere. Every zookeeper believes he loves animals. He loves them the way art historians love art: framed, catalogued, preserved, safe from weather and touch and time, and never once consulted about the arrangement. Zookeeper logic begins with a true sentence, prayed in a hundred houses—the world is broken, and we are the ones charged to set it right—and it ends with citizens managed as animals in an enclosure: fed on schedule, housed, doctored, protected from every risk, and never asked what they want. The sentence is innocent; the traditions that carry it spent centuries bolting restraints onto it. The disease is the restraint stripped off and the belief kept at full strength, The keeper's standing bewilderment has a signature sentence: why do they vote against their own best interests? Don't they know the enclosure is safe—don't they know that in the full enclosure, a kind of prison where everybody is fed and doctored and nobody needs to take a risk, nothing can hurt them? Underneath the bewilderment sits the refusal of one fact: animals prefer to be free rather than safe and protected.

The keeper's love

Start with the love, because the love is real. A zookeeper does not hate animals; he has devoted his life to them, and he will tell you so with tears in his eyes at the fundraiser. What he loves is the collection: the species preserved, the bloodline managed, the specimen thriving under his care, on his schedule. The art historian loves art the same way. He loves the canvas behind glass, the attribution settled, the humidity held at fifty percent forever. What neither man loves is the thing itself at liberty—art that can be touched and faded and carried off, an animal that can leave. An animal in the wild is, in the keeper's ledger, an animal being lost. A painting in a farmhouse is a painting at risk. Preservation is the form love takes when it has quietly replaced the beloved's will with its own.

This is why the keeper cannot be argued out of it. Nobody sees the villain in the bathroom mirror; only the hero. He experiences his fences as devotion, his ration book as tenderness, his locked gate as the price of his love, and he is telling the truth about his feelings. The feelings were never the question. The question is the architecture the feelings build: an enclosure, a feeding schedule, and a population that is never polled. Keepers do not poll the collection.

Every house of belief hands its keepers a sentence. The one nearest to hand in American politics is tikkun olam, תיקון עולם, repairing the world, the old Jewish charge that progressivism took for its baseline in the 1950s—and the illustration matters only because the borrowing stripped it. In its home the charge came with guards: the sages capped even a ransom's mercy, ruling that some compassionate acts cost the whole order, and Rabbi Tarfon set the labor's terms in Pirkei Avot: “It is not your duty to finish the work, but neither are you free to desist from it.” Obligatory, unfinishable, nobody's to administer. What crossed over was the mandate with the guards left behind, and what arrives at street level is simpler: bulldoze them into compliance.

None of this acquits him where it counts. The animal does not experience the keeper's intentions; it experiences the fence. From inside the enclosure the tenderest keeper and the cruelest one are the same shape at the gate, and the love that fills the bowl does not unlock it. Evil does not require a villain. It only requires a man whose good intentions are somebody else's whole world.

The oldest charter

The West's founding mandate is older than any borrowing. Genesis opens with the grant: fill the earth and subdue it, rule over the fish in the sea and the birds in the sky and over every living creature that moves on the ground. Every steward since has quoted the grant. What the quotation drops is the very next verse, where the charter sets the table: the seed-bearing plants and the fruit trees will be yours for food, and to the beasts and the birds and everything that moves along the ground, every green plant for food—all of them, the wolf and the tiger included, issued vegetables in a world with no predation in it. Dominion as first written is rule over a garden where the ruler eats nobody. Meat enters the charter only after the Flood, as a concession, and with conditions still attached.

The pattern that governs everything after is complete in the first chapter: a mandate, a guard on the same page, and a posterity that carries the mandate at full strength and leaves the guard in Eden. The keeper is not a modern invention and not one faith's failure. He is what the charter becomes every time it is inherited by men who read the grant and skip the menu.

Hear me out

The keeper's signature phrase is “hear me out.” It is the sound of the gate being oiled. Hear me out: I know you don't like it, I know it isn't what you chose, but it is better for you than what your own agency suggests, and the reason you can't see that is that you are not thinking it through, you don't know any better, you can't take care of yourself. The phrase is everywhere now, the whole internet a ward round of hear-me-outs, and its logic never varies: your refusal is a symptom, and a symptom licenses treatment. Under it, everyone outside the credentialed and governing class is reclassified as a patient. The end of that road has a name, and it is hospice: total care, every need met, every risk removed, for people defined as no longer able. The keeper's promise to the population is hospice at scale—a world where being looked after completely is the whole of the good, and the price of admission is agreeing that you are dying.

The perfect enclosure is a jail

A cartoon making the rounds has a protester demanding her enemy be jailed, and the enemy agreeing with enthusiasm: send me to jail, where the healthcare is free and the housing is free and the food is free, where medicine is a right and treatment is a right, where there is water and a bed and everybody is equal and everybody is taken care of. The joke works because every word of the brochure is accurate. Run the keeper's checklist honestly—food, housing, medicine, safety, equality, care—and the institution that satisfies all of it, completely, with no gaps and no opt-outs, is a jail. The perfected enclosure and the prison are the same building. The difference is the signage, and the signage is for the keepers.

Elsewhere

Television built the keeper's utopia and called it the Federation. In Star Trek the world works: no hunger, no money, no mines on screen, no builders, no NIMBY wars over where anything goes, nobody unclean, nobody unemployable, nobody mad in the street. The starships glide, the replicators provide, and the dirt is simply elsewhere—in somebody else's quadrant, off-screen, in the episodes about other people's planets. That is how a perfected world is achievable: in fiction, where the mess can be moved out of frame by the writers. The earthly keeper attempts the same edit with zoning and service areas and shelter siting, and is forever surprised that the mess has an address in his own city, and a vote.

The open gate

The architecture has one fact it cannot admit, because admitting it dissolves the job. Animals prefer to be free rather than safe and protected. Open the gate and they go—toward the risk, the weather, the predators, the whole unpriced danger of the wild—because the wild is theirs in a way the enclosure never is. The keeper reads the escape as ingratitude, as madness, as proof the animals needed a stronger fence. He never reads it as the verdict it is. The bowl was full. The gate was locked. Given the choice, they chose the gate.

The republic runs the experiment in the open, on the wild mustangs of the Southwest. The mustangs are rounded up by helicopter for their own good—too many for the range, the experts say—and penned, fed, doctored, gentled, and sold off as pets. Every item of care is delivered. And the mustangs run from the helicopters until some of them die running, because a mustang's life is the mountains: the challenge, the competition, the living and dying by its wits, death included as part of the life and not a failure of management. The roundup is the only poll ever taken of the managed, and the managed vote no with their bodies.

American politics has already run the experiment at scale, and it is also why Trump was elected: his elections were a revolt of the animals in the zoo, the managed declining the management, taking the risk and the weather over another term inside the fence. The keepers' shock afterward was the keeper's shock exactly—ingratitude, madness, proof the fence was too low, a call for better explanations of why the animals run. And the Blue Wave was also a revolt against control, run by the other side's animals against the other side's keepers. The pendulum is the enclosure rocking: each coalition builds its fence, and each is astonished in turn when the animals inside it charge the gate. A wild horse rears against anybody who tries to get on its back—against wokeness and identity politics arriving with a bridle, and against ICE agents going in hard on a neighborhood, alike. The horse is not choosing a rider. It is refusing to be ridden.

Freedom still shows itself where the risk is still legal. A motorcycle is extremely dangerous, willfully so, and anybody can buy one. A bicycle in traffic is dangerous. Alcohol is dangerous. These are the popular dangers, open to everyone, and their survival is a sign the gate is not yet fully shut: a man may still take a risk that can kill him, on his own decision, without a keeper's signature. The line the keepers draw is not danger but direction. California is very anti-gun and extremely pro-motorsport, and motorsport is extremely dangerous; the difference is that a gun can kill somebody else, while the motorcycle mostly kills its rider. A risk that threatens only the animal himself is tolerated longest. The moment it can be pointed at the herd, the fence goes up. Skiing is extremely dangerous too, but it is a rich man's danger, priced and resort-kept, and a freedom only the comfortable can reach proves nothing about whether the country is free.

Not every animal rears. Every European I know is in love with his government and its corporate rulers, with the alma-mater love people carry for the school that made them—the institution can do no wrong, because it made them. The Germans above all. Berlin's great central park is the Tiergarten, the animal garden: the Elector's old hunting enclosure, where the hunting was legal because it was his, with the zoo at its edge to this day—the beloved enclosure at the heart of the capital, and nobody strolling it experiences it as a fence. The English are the exception; they hate their government on principle, from both directions at once, and always have. A population bred in the enclosure long enough stops rearing. It starts donating to the zoo.

The fear underneath it is old. The Puritans hated the wilderness—it scared them—and they razed it, cleared a continent of its lumber and developed the land to within an inch of its life. Europe had done the same, earlier and worse: Germany harvested itself so bare that it had to invent scientific forestry to survive, and the forests Europe is proudest of are plantations, trees in rows, a crop with a cathedral's reputation. A managed forest is a zoo for trees. That is stewardship with extreme prejudice, and the wildwood was its first collection; the keeper has been apologizing to it with plantings ever since. And the enclosure's first law is always a game law. The lord's animals fatten in his Tiergarten, and the man who hunts in the king's wood to feed his family is a poacher. Call his dinner bushmeat and shudder at it, and you have the keeper's whole vocabulary in a single word: his hunger is a crime because his eating was never scheduled.

The diver learns the other law. Underwater, a wild animal approaches you only when you quiet yourself—stop chasing, stop reaching—until curiosity does what capture never could, and the animal comes to you on its own terms, free to leave at a flick. Chase it, and it is gone, and it does not trust that water anymore. The churches preach stewardship as Christ-like humility, and the twelve-step rooms state the limit plainly: you cannot make someone recover, cannot force a metanoia, cannot demand an apology, cannot make someone love you. Stewardship practiced on people does not allow agency, and that is the whole of the charge against it. The keeper grabs. The diver waits, and is sometimes—sometimes—approached.

Freemasonry built the whole doctrine into two stones. On the lodge floor sit the rough ashlar and the perfect ashlar: the raw block as it comes from the quarry, and the same stone squared and smooth. The genius of the pairing is who holds the chisel. Only a man can make a perfect ashlar of himself, for himself; a man does not respond to being perfected externally, and a stone worked on by somebody else's hammer is not an ashlar at all—it is rubble. Improvement in the lodge is opt-in, by agency, by decision: something attractive set where it can be aspired to, never something administered. The keeper arrives with his own chisel and a plan for your shape. The lodge sets the finished stone on the floor and waits.

Underneath the argument sit two accounts of the world, and they do not reconcile. The keeper's account says the world is a broken vase and the repair is our job, forever—the gold in the seams is his highest virtue, and management is the method and the managed the material. The other account says the world is perfect with or without us on it, and people fuck it up by picking at it—that nothing was ever healed by being picked at, that the scab is not the disease, that the garden was fine before the gardener arrived with his plan. One man is a constant gardener. The other is a wildflower guy, and his whole program is to keep his hands in his pockets and let the field do what fields do. Zookeeper logic is the first account with its humility removed: a true sentence about a broken world, carried by sincere people who love the managed the way art historians love art, issuing in hear-me-outs, enclosures, hospice care for the able-bodied, and a paradise indistinguishable from a jail. The traditions posted their guards—work no man may finish, ransoms no community may pay, a repair that belongs to God. The keepers dropped the guards and kept the love. The animals keep voting with their feet, for the hills, for the risk, for the wild—because animals prefer to be free rather than safe and protected.

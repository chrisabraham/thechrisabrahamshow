Nobody Pays a Proportion

An article by Chris Abraham, November 22, 2026

Share: X · Facebook · LinkedIn · Reddit · Bluesky · Threads · Email

Nobody pays a proportion. A rich man does not pay a rich man's share of a sandwich, and a poor man does not get a poor man's discount on a roof. People do not pay percentages of what they earn, percentages of what they are worth, or percentages of what they own. They pay the price the pool sets. The pool is the other people in line—everyone else willing to do the work, take the table, or sell the thing. The payer's balance sheet never enters the room.

The rule shows itself in one wallet across one week. The same man pays hundreds of dollars for a night out without reading the bill, then shops a framing job on his house down to the last hundred like the money is coming out of his children's food. He is not confused, and he is not a hypocrite. He is paying two different prices for two different categories of thing. For the visible—the scene, the location, the story the evening tells—his money has no ceiling. For the commoditized, the fungible, the work that disappears under paint, his money is a knife fight, and the lowest bid wins it.

What I paid when the agency was mine

I started Abraham PR in 2006, and my rule was simple: pay the maximum. Everyone who helped me made at least twenty-five dollars an hour as a consultant. These were kids right out of college. I was billing fifty to seventy-five dollars an hour for the work, so the people beside me took home half of what the client paid for me, and they could have all the hours they wanted. Twenty-five dollars an hour in 2006, unlimited hours, no ceiling on the week—that was great money for a twenty-two-year-old with a fresh degree and a landlord. I was glad to pay it. Glad is the operative word. The ethos was not generosity as decoration. The ethos was that the money was real, the hours were real, and nobody in the arrangement had to feel small.

When Mark came on board as my partner, the ethos changed, because his arithmetic was different. His read was that I was selling people at double what I paid them, and that the spread, stated that way, made the arrangement sound harder than it was. The kids were making great money out of college. The hours were unlimited. Nobody felt exploited, because nobody was being squeezed—the rate was the rate, the work was there, and the check cleared. Mark was describing the pool. A consultant's price is what the next consultant will take, and twenty-five dollars an hour cleared that pool with room to spare. My pay-the-maximum rule was a preference laid on top of the market. His rule was the market itself, stated plain.

I kept my preference, and I keep it still. But the years since have taught me which of the two rules the world actually runs on, and it is not mine. I paid a proportion of my billing because I chose to. Nobody else in the chain was bound by my choice, and the moment a partner with different arithmetic sat in the chair, the proportion vanished and the pool remained. The pool always remains.

Fort Lauderdale

Mark lives in Fort Lauderdale now, and every time he goes out with his beautiful wife, the night runs into the hundreds. The food, by his own report, is crap—fusion this and fusion that, drinks built from whatever the distributor's bag holds, the same inventory every kitchen on the strip is working from. He would be happier, honestly happier, with a street taco or a Cuban sandwich eaten standing up. He says this himself, out loud, while paying. Then he pays the hundreds again the next weekend.

He is not paying for food. The food is the smallest line on the bill, and everybody at the table knows it. He is paying for location, location, and to see and be seen. He is paying to give his wife a night in the scene, in the rooms where the town displays itself. That category of spending has no ceiling, because the product is not dinner. The product is the evening's evidence: we were there, we belong there, the night counted. Against that product, a street taco cannot compete at any price, including free. The taco is better food and a worse evening, and the wallet knows the difference even when the mouth disagrees.

Every restaurant on that strip understands the arrangement perfectly. The kitchen buys from the same distributors as every other kitchen. The bag is the same bag. What the house sells is the room, the light, the address, the other tables. The markup is rent on meaning, and it is the most reliable markup in the American economy, because the buyer is not comparing it against the place across the street. He is comparing it against staying home, and staying home loses.

Arlington, where I live

Arlington runs the same split, at scale, every day. People here pay Arlington prices for the Arlington life without limit. The houses go for what the houses go for. The prepared food, the delivery apps, the coffee that is really a drinkable dessert—money flows out for all of it like the supply is endless. People in buildings I would charitably call tired order every meal and snack through DoorDash and Uber, paying fees on fees for the privilege of not walking to a kitchen. Time is the visible luxury, and they buy it at any price.

I am in this picture, in my own narrow way. I am a carnivore. I buy my food at Giant and I prepare it myself, and my diet is so limited it can run relatively cheap or infinitely expensive depending on the cut of meat I reach for. Even my stripped-down version of the Arlington life has no proportion in it. I do not spend a percentage of anything. I spend what the thing costs in the category it lives in.

Then the same Arlingtonian needs his house framed, or his roof done, or any of the big work a house eventually demands, and the wallet changes species. Now it is best price. Now it is three estimates and a spreadsheet. The ethos of Arlington and the whole DMV, when the work is a commodity, is four words long: lowest bid always wins. The man who paid nine dollars for a coffee drink at noon is, at two o'clock, squeezing a contractor over an amount he will spend on coffee by spring. Both transactions are rational. They are priced by different pools. The coffee has one seller on that corner at that moment. The framing has fifty hungry men with trucks, and the fiftieth man sets the price for all of them.

Twenty-six dollars for a sandwich

A friend took me to lunch recently at the Silver Diner up on Route 7, where Route 7 becomes King Street, near where the TGI Friday's is, or still is. I had not really eaten in restaurants in about ten years. My life does not route through dining rooms anymore. So I sat in a diner—a Gucci diner, a diner with production values—and ordered a regular lunch sandwich, and the sandwich was twenty-six dollars.

It blew my mind. Then I looked around the room, and the room was full. Nobody was wincing. Nobody was doing the math I was doing. Families, couples, guys on lunch breaks, all of them paying diner prices that would have bought a steak dinner in the Arlington I carry in my head, and paying gladly, because lunch out is an experience purchase, and experience purchases do not get audited. The sandwich was fine. The sandwich was not the product. The product was the booth, the hour, the company, the small ceremony of being out in the world at midday. Twenty-six dollars is what that ceremony costs on Route 7 in 2026, and the pool of people willing to pay it fills the room every day.

Every one of those diners, handed a contractor's estimate that evening, would become a different animal by dessert. The sandwich money and the framing money live in different accounts in the same head, and the accounts never transfer. One account is audited by the pool. The other is audited by nobody.

Green is green

I learned the premium half of this rule from the inside, at Edelman, where I worked when General Electric and Walmart were clients. The line inside those accounts was a sentence I have never forgotten: green is green. Offer a thing as green and it makes money. Walmart, the most ruthless price machine in American retail, sold organics at a premium, and sold everything else it could attach the same halo to, because the premium was available and the premium was enormous.

The premium buys the shopper's account of herself. It does not buy a better tomato in any way a scale or a lab could settle. The organic tomato says something at the checkout that the regular tomato cannot say, and the saying is the product. Walmart understood that the same customer who would drive across town to save four cents on detergent would pay double for the tomato with the story. Both behaviors are the same behavior. Price follows the category, and the category is decided by whether the purchase is visible—to the room, or to the buyer's own mirror.

That is why the premium never leaks. The green premium does not raise the price of the commodity beside it on the shelf. The detergent stays in its knife fight, priced by its pool, untouched by the halo two aisles over. Values attach to the visible purchase and stop at its edge. Everything else goes back to the bid.

The memory is the product

The largest infinite-money category of all is memory. People will spend an infinite amount of money on making memories, and they will say so out loud while they spend it. That is what the five-hundred-dollar hotel room is. It is not shelter. A roof and a bed can be had for a fifth of the price on the same street. The five hundred buys the remembering: the view the family will describe for a decade, the lobby the kids will measure every later hotel against. Families spend twenty thousand dollars at Disney or in Vegas. Some spend two hundred thousand. They are not buying rides and shows. They are buying the story the family tells at every table for the rest of their lives, and a story that durable is cheap at the price.

I stand outside this market, and standing outside it is how I finally understood its pricing. I have aphantasia and SDAM, so a hotel room is ephemeral for me—a moment on the lips, a lifetime on the credit card. The memory does not keep for me the way it keeps for other people. If I paid five hundred dollars a night for a room, I would stay in the room the whole time and not go out and do anything, because the room itself would have to be the whole purchase; there is no later dividend of remembering to collect. Other people are not buying the room at all. They are buying the remembering, and the remembering pays out for years. Once the memory is seen as the product, the price stops looking insane. It starts looking like the most rational money in the whole economy of the visible.

The flip side of the memory market is the functional market, and it is a knife fight with no romance in it. Anything merely functional gets the lowest bid for acceptable. Plumbing, wiring, car service, gardening—people pay the least they can for acceptable service, and they do not expect superlative service from providers like that. Acceptable is the spec. A plumber who charges for artistry loses the job to the man who charges for unblocking the drain. The work is invisible, the result is binary, and the pool of men who can do it acceptably is deep. That is the whole pricing story.

Every seller stuck in that pool knows the one escape hatch: repackage the commodity as the experience. Gardening becomes topiary. The moment a service is sold as experience and memory—as the thing the neighbors see, the thing the owner walks his guests past, the thing that says something about the house—it leaves the lowest-bid pool and enters the infinite-money country. The moment it is a monetized, commoditized service again, it is stuck back in the pool with the drain men. The work did not change. The category did. The category is the price.

Under the paint

So the map of a rich town's money is not hard to draw. Infinite money for the visible: the interior decorating, the mid-century modern Eames furniture, the room that announces its owner before he says a word. Infinite money for the scene: the Fort Lauderdale night, the Route 7 lunch, the dessert that drinks like a coffee. And for everything fungible, everything commoditized, everything that ends up invisible under paint—the framing, the rough work, the labor nobody will ever see or compliment—the money shrinks to the lowest bid in the pool, and the pool is deep and hungry and always has one more man in it.

Inexpensive is different from cheap, and the difference is the whole theory. Inexpensive is a fact about a thing. Cheap is a relation between a bid and the next bidder. A thing is cheap only when somebody standing behind the seller will do it for less. A thing is never cheap when it is the only door into the room where life is being lived. Nobody pays a proportion of what they earn, what they are worth, or what they own. They pay the pool. They always have. The wallet keeps two sets of books, and it has never once confused them.

America Became the Empire

An article by Chris Abraham, November 23, 2026

This article, read aloud:

Share: X · Facebook · LinkedIn · Reddit · Bluesky · Threads · Email

America is the Empire now, and the rest of the world has known it longer than we have.

Star Wars opened on May 25, 1977. Saigon had fallen two years earlier, on April 30, 1975. Richard Nixon had resigned three years earlier. George Lucas had built his villains out of parts any American kid in 1977 could read at a glance, and he built his heroes out of the other side of the same war.

The name did the first piece of work. Stormtrooper came straight from the Sturmabteilung, the Nazi Party's paramilitary arm, and from the German storm troops of the First World War before them. Costume designer John Mollo cut the Imperial officers in grey-green tunics, riding breeches, and boots that echoed Wehrmacht dress. Darth Vader's helmet carried the shape of the Stahlhelm. Palpatine climbed from chancellor to emperor the way Hitler had climbed from chancellor to dictator, dissolving a republic in stages while the senate applauded itself hoarse. Lucas said so himself, in interviews across decades, and he added the second source in the same breath: Nixon. Palpatine was a politician first. Lucas conceived the film as a reaction to the Nixon presidency and the Vietnam War, a story about how democracies turn into empires while nobody with a vote stops them.

The medal ceremony at the end of the first film closes the circuit. Luke Skywalker and Han Solo walk down a long aisle between ranks of soldiers standing at attention while Princess Leia waits on a raised platform. The composition comes from Leni Riefenstahl's Triumph of the Will, her 1935 film of the 1934 Nuremberg rally—low angles, perfect formations, geometry made out of men. Lucas gave the Rebels a Nazi rally for their victory scene and gave the Empire Nazi tailoring for its uniform, and American audiences in 1977 felt no friction at all. The visual language meant one thing in that theater: power on the march, whoever wore it.

I was eight in 1978. I saw the Empire the way every American kid in my row saw it, as a thing that lived somewhere else.

Fascism in my childhood frame had an address, and the address was foreign. The Soviet Union had it, with its May Day parades in Red Square, columns of missiles rolling past the reviewing stand while old men in heavy coats watched. China had it. North Korea had it. Ceaușescu's Romania had it, a concrete palace and a starved countryside. South Korea's old regime had it, generals in sunglasses running a country that made our televisions. Spain and Portugal had carried it into my lifetime and shed it only in the middle seventies, Franco dead in 1975, the Portuguese dictatorship fallen in 1974. The map in my head was clean. Tyranny wore a foreign uniform, spoke with an accent, and kept its people behind a wall or a sea. America wore the other costume. America was the small band in the woods with the stolen plans and the farm boy who could not shoot straight until the Force moved through him.

That map held because the casting held. In 1977 America could still plausibly dress as the guerrilla. The country had lost a war to rice farmers in black pajamas two years before, and Lucas used exactly that material: he said the Empire's fight against a smaller guerrilla force came from Vietnam, from his surprise at how few people spoke up against the war. In his early notes the Rebels carried the North Vietnamese part. A huge technological empire went hunting for a small group of freedom fighters in the jungle, and the audience was invited to root against the empire. American audiences accepted the invitation without blinking, because the empire on screen wore German tailoring and spoke in British officer accents. The tailoring did the distancing. Take the tailoring off and the story was about us, and everybody in the theater knew it at some level below speech, and the tailoring let them not know it.

Fifty years on, the tailoring is a hobby.

The 501st Legion was founded in August 1997 in South Carolina by Albin Johnson and Tom Crews, two fans who built stormtrooper armor for the rerelease of the original trilogy and posted photographs of themselves online. The Legion now counts more than 14,000 active members worldwide and more than 26,000 approved costumes. Its members wear screen-accurate Imperial armor—stormtroopers, Sith Lords, clone troopers, bounty hunters, the villains' whole wardrobe—and they wear it to charity walks, hospital visits, library readings, and parades. On January 1, 2007, two hundred of them marched in the Tournament of Roses Parade in Pasadena with George Lucas himself as grand marshal. Lucasfilm calls the Legion its preferred Imperial costuming organization. Members appeared as extras in The Mandalorian.

A uniform built from the Sturmabteilung's name and the Wehrmacht's cut now raises money for children's hospitals, and nobody at the charity walk flinches. The referent drained out. What remains is excellent tailoring, white armor polished to a shine, a helmet with no face in it, and the pleasure of standing in formation with fourteen thousand friends. The Legion calls itself Vader's Fist. Its charter promises costumed charity and volunteer work, and by every account it delivers both, thousands of events, hundreds of thousands of dollars raised. I believe the good faith completely. The good faith is the evidence. A symbol has finished dying when good people can wear it to visit sick children and the only thing anyone feels is delight.

Empires die the same way in the other direction. First the power is real and feared, then the power is real and resented, then the power becomes a costume other people put on to describe you.

Travel outside the United States and listen to how the story gets cast now. America collects the taxes in the modern form: the dollar as the world's reserve currency, sanctions that cut a country out of the banking system by a decision made in Washington, bases in more than eighty countries, aircraft carriers named for presidents patrolling other people's coastlines, technology platforms headquartered in California setting the speech rules for elections in countries whose languages their moderators do not speak. The outer planets in this telling are the places that mine the lithium, assemble the phones, host the bases, absorb the refugees, and watch their young people leave for the core. The Death Star in this telling has a flag with fifty stars on it, and the farm boy with the stolen plans speaks Arabic, or Spanish, or Mandarin, or Ukrainian, depending on the decade and the weapons package.

Americans hear this casting as slander because the domestic casting never changed. Inside the country the story still runs on 1977 rails: we are the Rebels, perpetually, by birth. Every American political movement dresses in Rebel orange. The left casts corporations and their captured state as the Empire. The right casts the federal government and its captured culture as the Empire. Both sides buy the same merchandise, quote the same farm boy, and claim the same stolen plans. A country where everybody is the resistance has no word left for the hegemon, so the hegemon goes unnamed at home while the rest of the world uses the name daily.

The Hunger Games shows the flip in a cleaner laboratory, because the dates are closer together and the audience is still alive and arguing.

The first film opened in the United States on March 23, 2012, directed by Gary Ross from Suzanne Collins's 2008 novel. Panem is a post-apocalyptic North America: a wealthy Capitol ruling twelve districts, each district assigned its industry, each district poor in a different flavor. District 12 mines coal in what used to be Appalachia. As punishment for a failed rebellion seventy-four years earlier, every district sends two children a year to fight to the death on television for the Capitol's entertainment. Katniss Everdeen, sixteen, a hunter who poaches to feed her family, volunteers to take her little sister's place.

The American audience in 2012 knew its seat instantly. We were District 12. The Capitol was coastal, decadent, powdered, eyebrowless, drunk on spectacle—a composite sketch of Washington, Manhattan, and Los Angeles drawn by the rest of the country, for the rest of the country. The districts made things, mined things, grew things, and died for the Capitol's amusement and enrichment. The film flattered the interior's account of itself: productive, plainspoken, exploited, one good shot away from rebellion. Jennifer Lawrence carried a bow, and half the country saw its own daughter.

The global audience in 2026 reads the same frames and assigns the seats the other way. America is the Capitol. America has the spectacle industry the whole planet watches, the streaming platforms, the award shows, the influencers with the dyed hair and the engineered faces, the capital city where the decisions about war and money get made by people who have never mined anything, and the military that enforces the district system worldwide. The districts in this reading are the countries that supply the cobalt, the garments, the call centers, the oil, the soldiers' replacements, and the tribute in the form of young people who die in wars the Capitol watches on screens and debates as content. The viewer's address changed, and the address assigns the casting—same film, same novels. The seventy-four years since the Dark Days do quiet work in that casting, too. To the teenagers who made the books a phenomenon, seventy-four years is prehistory, six lifetimes deep, and the Capitol reads as a condition of the world rather than a government with a birthday.

This is brand perception, and I say that as a man who works in perception for a living. A brand is the story told by people who do not own it. The owner writes the copy, buys the media, polishes the armor, and marches in the parade. The audience decides what the uniform means, and the audience's decision is the brand, whatever the owner's charter says. America wrote itself a Rebel story in 1977 and has kept the copy updated for fifty years: freedom fighters, underdogs, a republic resisting the dark side of its own power. The copy was true enough in the theater while the country was losing a war and impeaching its way out of a presidency. The copy stayed in circulation while the country became the largest military spender on earth by a factor of three over its nearest rival, the issuer of the currency every other country must earn to buy oil, and the landlord of the platforms where the planet argues. Audiences abroad did the arithmetic that audiences always do. They looked at who collects, who garrisons, who sanctions, who watches, and they cast accordingly.

The Empire in Lucas's films never thought of itself as the villain either. That detail was in the tailoring from the start. The Imperial officers believed in order, in a galaxy made safe and legible, in careers and pensions and the quiet satisfaction of a well-run sector. Palpatine's own pitch, across six films, was peace. He ended a corrupt republic's chaos and gave the galaxy a single chain of command, and the men in the grey-green uniforms served it as professionals serve any state that pays them and promotes them on schedule. Lucas built that psychology out of the German material deliberately: ordinary functionaries, excellent uniforms, an atrocity administered as policy by people who went home to dinner. The horror in the design was the administration.

America became the Empire the way Palpatine became Emperor, by acclamation, in stages, each stage voted or applauded or scrolled past, the republic's forms intact and functioning the whole way down. The bases stayed because each one had a reason. The sanctions stayed because each one had a target nobody wanted to defend at a dinner party. The carriers stayed because the sea lanes must stay open, and they must, and that is how an empire talks when it is telling the truth about its interests and still missing what it has become. A hegemon is an empire that has outsourced the naming to its critics, and the critics abroad have done the naming, in editorials, in classrooms, in the casting of American stories handed back to us with the seats reversed.

The stormtrooper armor at the charity walk is the kindest version of the ending. The symbol emptied out at home, turned to tailoring and fellowship and good works, while the thing the symbol was built to warn against grew up around the people wearing it, wearing a different flag. The Legion visits the children's hospital in the morning. The carrier group crosses the Pacific in the afternoon. Both wear the uniform of the power that runs the place, and only one of them knows it is a costume.

The Hunger Games half of this flip arrived by way of Isabel Brown's November 19 episode, "I Don't Think We Read the Same Hunger Games", from a commentator I had not seen before this week. Her title carries the whole phenomenon: one audience swore in 2012 that it was District 12, another swears now that the Capitol is a portrait of its enemies, and both are certain the book agrees with them. She named the split first. Credit where it started. Her book, The End of the Alphabet: How Gen Z Can Save America, carries her side of the generational argument.

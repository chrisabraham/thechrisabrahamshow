The American Dream Is a Dissatisfaction Machine

An article by Chris Abraham, November 29, 2026

This article, read aloud:

Share: X · Facebook · LinkedIn · Reddit · Bluesky · Threads · Email

Austria was number eleven.

The graphic that bounced around Facebook this year showed the Wharton/WPP BAV 2026 Best Countries Index for quality of life. Sweden at 100, Denmark 98.2, Canada 95.0, Switzerland 94.8, Finland and Norway 92.4, the Netherlands 90.8, Australia 87.5, Germany 82.9, Belgium 78.6. Then the “correction” deleted Austria, slid everyone up a seat, and installed the United States at number eleven with a score of 77.9 that does not exist. The real table has Austria at 76.7 in eleventh place. The United States sits at twenty-seventh with 48.6, last in the G7. The index is a perception survey of roughly fifteen thousand adults in thirty-three countries. It measures how the world sees a place, not how the people who live there feel about their days. The forgery still did its job: it fed the machine.

I lived in Berlin for three years, from 2007 to 2010, on a German residence permit. I spent a college year in Norwich, in England, at the University of East Anglia. I lived in Utrecht, in Holland, in the middle nineties, and I spent a summer in Helsinki, in Finland. Close enough, in each of them, to get a feel for the place. I was not a tourist in any of them.

What I noticed had almost nothing to do with the welfare state or the tax rate or the length of the summer evenings. Nobody was playing the game. The apartment was the apartment. The car, or the bike, or the tram, was transportation. The job at whatever firm was the job. The education they finished, the class they were born into, the work their family did before them—none of it was a scoreboard. Their life was their life. To an American eye that looks like a lack of ambition. A Dutchman happily running the shop his father ran reads as a man who gave up. A German couple renting the same flat for decades reads as people who never made it. That reading is the disease talking.

The game is the American one. Everyone is ranked against everyone else, all the time, and everyone knows their rank. You admire the person one rung above you and resent them in the same breath. You look down on the person one rung below you, mostly because you are afraid of ending up there. And the person on your exact rung—your neighbor, your coworker, your brother-in-law—is your real competition. That is who you measure the car against, the kitchen, the kid’s college, the vacation, the title. There is no psychologically neutral position. A person making forty thousand can resent someone making thirty, envy someone making fifty, and compete with someone making forty. A person making four hundred thousand runs the identical architecture with an extra zero. The numbers change. The psychology does not.

The American Dream has a moving finish line. It promises that if you work hard you can improve your circumstances. Somewhere along the way that promise becomes an obligation. You are not simply allowed to improve them; you are expected to. Own rather than rent. Own a better home. Drive a better car. Earn more. Get promoted. Send the children to better schools. Live in the better neighborhood. Retire with more. When you achieve those things the expectation resets. Yesterday’s achievement becomes today’s minimum. The two-bedroom apartment becomes inadequate once your colleagues own houses. The Honda becomes inadequate once the neighbors drive BMWs. The perfectly respectable job becomes inadequate once someone from your graduating class becomes a vice president. James Truslow Adams coined the phrase in 1931. By now it means one thing: wherever you are, it is not far enough. Keeping Up with the Joneses started as a comic strip in 1913. Every generation since has been told that standing still is falling behind, and that falling behind is a moral failure.

The Scandinavians wrote the opposite rule down. Aksel Sandemose put it on paper in 1933 as the Law of Jante: you are not to think you are anything special. The Swedes have lagom—just enough, the right amount. Americans hear Jante and think oppression. Danes hear it and go home to dinner.

The ladder never ends. Dissatisfaction is the DNA of every centimillionaire I know. Somebody with a hundred million dollars has finished the American Dream several times over, and he still measures himself against the guy with three hundred million, the guy with the better boat, the guy whose name is on the bigger building. Reaching the top rungs does not make the game stop; it just makes the rungs farther apart. The same itch that sends a guy in Ohio to buy a bigger truck sends a centimillionaire chasing his first billion, and neither one gets to stop climbing.

The money makes the comparison stranger. Judged by income, Americans are rich. The OECD puts the United States in the top three in the world for median disposable household income, behind only Luxembourg and Norway, and ahead of Sweden, Denmark, Finland, the Netherlands, and Germany. Counting everything a household takes in, public services included, the average American had roughly sixty-seven thousand dollars in purchasing power in 2023. The average German had about fifty-five thousand, the average Dutchman about fifty-one thousand, the average Finn about forty-five thousand seven hundred. The American in the middle earns more than his counterpart in nearly every country that beats him on the happiness tables. Holding them side by side is apples and oranges.

Wealth tells the rest of the story. UBS’s Global Wealth Report 2026 ranks the United States second in the world for average wealth per adult, at six hundred ninety-six thousand two hundred seventy-seven dollars, behind only Switzerland. Line every American adult up from poorest to richest, though, and the one standing in the middle holds about sixty-nine thousand dollars. That puts the United States twenty-eighth. The Dane in the middle holds two hundred three thousand seven hundred seventy-one dollars. The Dutchman holds one hundred twenty-seven thousand four hundred seven. The Finn holds roughly ninety thousand and the Swede roughly eighty-four thousand. Germany is the one country of the four below the American median, at about fifty-three thousand. The average American is rich because a few Americans are standing on the top rungs. The Danes kept their money on the floor where everybody stands. Americans stacked theirs up the ladder.

So the country with the bigger paychecks is the less satisfied one. The World Happiness Report literally hands a man a ladder, zero to ten, and asks where his life stands. In 2026 Finland sits at the top for the ninth year, score 7.764. The United States is twenty-third, score 6.816. A Dane in the middle of a quiet life puts himself near the top because he is content with what he has and his place in the world. An American with more money puts himself lower, because the only ladder he knows is the one with somebody standing a rung above him. Satisfaction is the whole measurement. Americans earn more, and every dollar of it goes into the race against the guy one rung up.

The happiness researchers will tell you the Nordics win on trust, on autonomy, on safety nets. Fine. Every one of those kills the ladder. A safety net means there is no rung below you to be afraid of. Trust means the guy next to you is your neighbor and not your rival. Autonomy means you get to decide what enough is, instead of letting the guy across the street decide it for you. Every explanation in those reports comes back to the same place. These are countries where nobody keeps score.

A satisfied man is a terrible customer. He does not need the truck, the second bathroom, the master’s degree, or the stock tip. He does not need to move to the better suburb to get away from the people he is afraid of becoming. He is already home. That is the threat a satisfied population poses to the American model, and it is why satisfaction gets sold back to Americans as laziness, as settling, as socialism.

Ambition and dissatisfaction are not the same animal. A man can want to become a better engineer, open a business, learn another language, become a better parent, travel, or improve his home without interpreting his existing life as a personal failure. You can want more without believing that what you have is inadequate. That is the distinction I noticed living in those places: not an absence of ambition, but a different relationship with the idea of enough.

The engine built the richest country on earth. It also built the most anxious one. Every time a ranking puts Sweden at 100 and the United States in the twenties, I know what is being measured. Swedes are measuring their lives. Americans are measuring each other.

The American Dream promises that everybody can move up. But it has created a society in which almost nobody is permitted to feel that they have arrived. We have turned the pursuit of a better life into an obligation to escape the life we already have. The dissatisfaction is not a bug. It is the feature that keeps the whole machine running.

One Taunt Is a Tragedy

An article by Chris Abraham, November 11, 2026

This article, read aloud:

Share: X · Facebook · LinkedIn · Reddit · Bluesky · Threads · Email

A freshman is gone from the University of Mississippi over thirteen seconds of doorbell video, and the country has spent a week on those thirteen seconds. Eighty years ago a white student making monkey noises at her Black neighbors in Mississippi was weather: constant, uncounted, beneath notice, part of the climate a Black family dressed for every morning. In 2026 the identical act is a lightning strike, with a name and a face and an attorney and a week of national attention. That is the paradox at the center of the Ole Miss story. The outrage is real. The outrage is also the measurement. A common thing never earns this kind of focus. Only a rare thing does, and this kind of racism has become rare enough in America that we are down to ones and twos, each one arriving as a tragedy, where once they arrived by the million as a statistic.

The facts fit in one breath. On Friday evening, November 6, three white women walked past an apartment door in Oxford, Mississippi. Two of them turned toward the Ring camera, beat their chests, and made monkey sounds, laughing as they passed. The apartment is home to three Black freshmen. One of them, Jakiyra Metcalf, posted the clip to TikTok, where it passed twelve million views. Alaina Kunes, eighteen, a freshman from Elkhorn, Wisconsin, named herself as the first woman in the video and apologized through her attorney, calling her actions “insensitive, inappropriate, and deeply harmful.” She said she had never met the women who live in the apartment and did not know their race. Her lawyer says she withdrew on her own, that the university neither suspended her nor asked her to leave. The university confirms only that she is no longer a student. The other two women remain unnamed, the investigation remains open, the university has warned that at least one student was accused wrongly, and on Wednesday the mother of one of the residents said she hates that Kunes withdrew.

The aphorism, reversed

The line everyone pins on Stalin, written down first by the German satirist Kurt Tucholsky, says one death is a tragedy and a million deaths is a statistic. The Ole Miss story is that sentence running in reverse. In 1946 the million were the statistic: racial terror in Mississippi was tallied, when it was tallied at all, in annual counts of lynchings, and the daily currency of slurs and taunts and caricatures never made a ledger because it was the medium of ordinary life. A white girl mocking the Black family down the hall generated no headline, no investigation, no statement from a vice chancellor, because there was no event. There was only Tuesday. Statistics are what a society keeps about the harms it has agreed to live inside. Tragedies are what it keeps about the harms it has stopped accepting.

Ones and twos

Now the count has come down to ones and twos, and look what a one gets. A face in every headline. An apology drafted by a law firm. A grandfather's car dealerships, fifty stores across four states, hauled into the story and issuing statements about a girl who never worked there. Death threats running toward her family. A sorority clearing its name of a rumor. A national argument about the third woman, the one who walked past in silence, and whether presence is guilt. Ole Miss has run this exact circuit before: in 2024 one white student aimed monkey gestures at one Black protester, was thrown out of his fraternity, and became a national story by himself. One gesture, one student, one cycle. That is the signature of rarity. Outrage on this scale is expensive, and no society spends it on the routine.

The psychologists found the mechanism

Harvard psychologists measured the machinery in 2018. David Levari, Daniel Gilbert, and their colleagues published a study in Science showing volunteers a thousand dots shading from purple to blue and asking which ones were blue. As the researchers quietly cut the number of true blue dots, the volunteers began calling purple dots blue. The category stretched to meet the scarcity. The team ran the same design on faces, and as threatening faces grew rare, neutral faces started reading as threats. They ran it on ethics proposals, and as unethical requests grew rare, innocent ones started reading as unethical. They named the effect prevalence-induced concept change: as a problem recedes, the definition of the problem expands to fill the space. Warned about the effect in advance, paid to resist it, the volunteers expanded their categories anyway. The country is running the blue-dot experiment on itself, in public, with better and better cameras.

The objection, answered

The standing objection says the cameras merely expose what always hid behind closed doors. The objection fails on the history. Eighty years ago this cruelty hid nowhere. It performed in daylight, in law, in newspapers, at lunch counters, because it carried no shame and feared no lens. Shame is the precondition of hiding, and shame is a modern arrival in this story. What the Ring camera caught in Oxford was an act its performers plainly expected to cost them nothing among the people they cared about, and the discovery that it cost everything is the distance between 1946 and 2026, measured in a single week.

So the thirteen seconds in Oxford carry two stories. The first is small and squalid: two young women performed an old cruelty at a stranger's door, and the women behind that door deserved neighbors better than that. The second story is the size of the reaction, and the reaction is the census. Monkey taunts from white students were once too common in Mississippi to count, and they are now so rare that a single one, caught by a doorbell, can end an enrollment, employ a law firm, and hold the country's attention for a week. That is what a statistic looks like after a society has beaten it down to ones and twos. It looks like a tragedy, because now, at last, it is treated as one.

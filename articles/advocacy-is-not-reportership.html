Advocacy Is Not Reportership

An article by Chris Abraham, November 18, 2026

This article, read aloud:

Share: X · Facebook · LinkedIn · Reddit · Bluesky · Threads · Email

On the Media is my favorite show, and this is a complaint about it.

It has been my favorite since the Garfield years. Bob Garfield and Brooke Gladstone relaunched it in 2001, and for twenty years it did the one thing almost nobody in media does: it said the quiet parts out loud. How a story gets framed. Who gets quoted and who gets described. Which words do the verdict's work before the evidence shows up. The show taught a generation of listeners to watch the watchers, and I am one of the listeners it taught.

I am listening to it now. The current episode carries a segment called The ICE Flight Tracker. Micah Loewinger, who became co-host in 2024, speaks with Gillian Brockell, introduced as a journalist and former flight attendant who is covering ICE flights and third-country removals. Her big piece ran in The American Prospect on October 2: how a city forced a private equity group to give up ownership of an ICE flight airline.

It is a remarkable piece of work. It is not reporting. It is advocacy, and the difference is the whole story.

What her work actually is

Start with what is genuinely in it, because a lot is. Brockell spent years tracking flight data. She documented Omni Air International's near-monopoly on long-range deportation flights. She counted a minimum of 76 removal stops between February and mid-June of this year, 10 confirmed third-country removals, trips lasting 32 to 68 hours. A man told her he and dozens of others were shackled for more than 73 hours on a deportation to Laos and Vietnam, and her flight data backed him up. She worked the ownership chain—Omni's parent ATSG, the sale to the private equity firm Stonepeak—and she found the lever: Stonepeak wanted to buy Chicago's parking meters, and Chicago's council could say no.

That is real shoe leather. Flight records, corporate filings, council votes. Nobody should wave it away.

Now consider how the beat began, because she tells it herself, on the segment and elsewhere. On May 20, 2025, lawyers were saying a plane was carrying people to South Sudan, a country they were not from, against a judge's orders. Brockell found the plane. When it landed to refuel at Shannon Airport in Ireland, she called the Shannon Airport police and asked them to check it—she believed the people aboard had been illegally removed, and she thought there might be a chance to get them off the plane. The plane sat for about two hours and took off. She still does not know if it was searched.

On the Media aired this as an origin story. Loewinger put it to her directly: when you called the Irish airport and, to use your word, you interfered—"I tried to interfere," she said. "I was not successful. I wish I had." She also said, on the same segment, that she sees herself as having a moral obligation to say something about how unsafe these flights are. A moral obligation is an advocate's charter. It was delivered under a reporter's billing, as color, as dedication, and the show moved on.

Now read her own account of what she was doing. She opens the Prospect piece by recalling her earlier Mother Jones story and saying she had hoped it would spur the pension funds invested in Omni's owner into improving conditions on the flights, or ending them altogether. With immigration officials in "maximum-cruelty mode" and the Supreme Court "rubber-stamping their every move," she decided the way to ground the planes was to hit them in the wallet. And in a parenthesis in the middle of the council fight, she discloses this: "I spoke virtually at one of these meetings and at a committee hearing in early July." The meetings were the community organizing meetings pressuring the council to block Stonepeak's deal. She was not covering that campaign. She was in it.

The piece closes by calling the Chicago outcome a blueprint that can be replicated in other communities, toward grounding ICE flights one plane at a time until they can be abolished altogether.

A reporter documents reality so the public can decide what to do. An advocate decides, and then works the record toward the decision. Brockell decided. By her own proud account, in her own words, she decided.

I am not against advocates

I am not against advocates. Advocacy is honorable work. Movements need advocates the way courts need lawyers, and some of the best people I know are advocates and say so on the label.

Brockell is not even hiding anything. She prints her hope, her strategy, and her participation in the piece itself. The American Prospect prints its commitments on its own label too. The dishonesty, such as it is, is not hers.

The trade itself knows the difference, even if the show will not say it. Brockell spent ten years at The Washington Post and left on the 2023 buyout—not fired, gone by her own choice, because, as she told Depth Perception this September, she is naturally opinionated and it had gotten increasingly painful to pretend she believed in "the whole objectivity thing," when she never really did. Then she pitched the flight stories around. Her words: "I found that a lot of places won't work with me because I interfered with this Ireland flight. Even left-wing places." Those editors are not her enemies. They share her politics and still hold the line she crossed. That is how load-bearing the line is.

It belongs to the billing. On the Media introduced an advocate as a journalist covering a beat, and the show that built its name saying the quiet parts out loud did not say this one. The audience was handed a participant and told it was an observer. Everything the segment built afterward stood on that mislabeled foundation.

The photographer's rule

I came up around photographers, and the old rule of that trade is the cleanest statement of the line I know. The photojournalist's job is to bear witness. He watches, he records, and the event in front of the camera stays the event, untouched by the man behind it.

That sounds cold, and it is cold, and the cold is the point. The camera is a window, not a participant. The moment the person behind it steps into the frame—testifies at the hearing, organizes the meeting, works the council—the audience can no longer trust that the events would have unfolded the same way without them. The record stops being a record and becomes a result.

Documentary filmmakers of any acclaim know this rule in their bones. The ones who break it are not bad people. They are something else. They are advocates with cameras, and the honest ones say so.

Why I built a paper on it

This is why I started Objective Daily News. I have written my gospel of reporting on this site already—the 2010 line, the record kept whole, malice assumed and stupidity never—and ODN is that gospel built into a machine. The analysis is OBJECTIVE. The clippings are SUBJECTIVE. Every source stands equal, and the reader gets the record complete enough to conclude for himself.

ODN is filing its own objective account of the ICE flights themselves—the planes, the restraints, the third-country removals, and the self-deportation door the government holds open beside them—because the item deserves the record kept whole. What Brockell documented belongs in that record. What Brockell did belongs in a different column, under her own honest label.

The label is the story

On the Media still does fine work, and I will still be listening next week. That is what makes this worth writing. A show nobody trusts can mislabel its guests all day and cost nothing. A show that taught its audience to check the framing has a duty to its own lesson.

Advocacy is not reportership. It never has been, no matter how good the shoe leather is, no matter how just the cause. The advocate wants an outcome. The reporter wants the record. Confuse the two and you do not get better reporting. You get better advocacy and worse information, and a public that slowly stops believing either one.

The record still has to be the record.

Matt Shade Is a Christ Figure

An article by Chris Abraham, October 25, 2026

Share: X · Facebook · LinkedIn · Reddit · Bluesky · Threads · Email

First I wrote about pride and going back. Then I wrote about partnership and perception. I told myself the curriculum was winding down, that a man can only learn so many of his life lessons from a Canadian detective show before he has to admit the show is not the point. Then I started season 4 of Private Eyes, and now there is a possible daughter, a first love kissing on Matt Shade, and Angie Everett in a bobbed haircut, and I am obsessed and I love this TV show too much and I don't even know who I am anymore.

Here is the mess, and it is a beautiful one. Years ago, before the agency, before Angie, Shade blew into a woman's life for one summer and had an affair with her, and then the summer ended the way summers do. She went back to an old boyfriend. Life went on, hers and his, and nobody—not then, not in four seasons of television since—has ever gotten a paternity test. So when a young woman comes out of the woodwork saying she might be his daughter, she is working from the same evidence the rest of us are: one hot summer and a resemblance. Then her mother shows up in person, and the mother is, and I say this as a man taking notes, extremely hot, and she is kissing on Shade like the intervening decades were a commercial break. Everything is unresolved. Nobody knows whose kid anybody is. It is the messiest this breezy little show has ever allowed itself to be, and I could not look away if you paid me.

And Angie. Angie Everett has a sexy new bobbed haircut this season, and I am obsessed, and she still cannot close the deal with Shade, and I need to be honest about how much of that I don't like, because loving a show doesn't require swallowing it whole. Angie is the license, the agency, the professional; her name is the one on the door. But too much of this season writes her as the one who pines—reacting to Shade, spite-dating lesser men as a coping strategy, carrying a chip on her shoulder where her plan ought to be. We don't like it when our strong women characters pine for a boy. It doesn't make her look smart or full of agency; it makes her a response monster, a reaction monster, a spite monster, and Angie Everett as written in this show's best hours is none of those things. Shade, meanwhile, sails through the same episodes unbothered, which brings me to the observation I cannot put down.

Here is my conceit, and I will defend it at parties: on Private Eyes, Jason Priestley's Matt Shade is the Christ figure. Not the suffering part—nobody's nailing Shade to anything—but the grace part, the part where people keep bringing him their worst moments and he refuses to charge them for it. Watch what happens every time somebody comes to Shade to apologize. An ex, a friend, a screw-up with a confession about some sexual or emotional mess, standing there braced for judgment, and Shade waves the apology off before it is even finished. Everybody's been a piece of shit. Everybody's blown up their life at least once. Everybody has done the thing they are confessing, or something adjacent to it, in the dark, at their worst. He who is without sin can cast the first stone, and Shade—the man with the affair, the maybe-daughter, and the wreckage of a hockey career behind him—knows exactly how many stones he's qualified to throw, which is none. Turn the other cheek is not a sermon with him. It's a reflex.

That's why the whole messy solar system of this show holds together. The daughter can come out of the woodwork because Shade won't greet the possibility with a lawyer. The mother can come back because she knows the summer won't be held against her. Ex-wives and old boyfriends and con artists and clients all orbit this man because he is the one place in their lives where the bill never arrives. Family rules, friends rule, loyalty rules, and you don't judge people for doing sexual or emotional things—not because those things don't matter, but because everybody at the table has done them and the only honest response is the one Shade gives: that's happened. It's done. What do you need?

So an update on the student, who is still, somehow, enrolled. I came to this show for a breezy procedural and stayed for lessons in pride, partnership, and perception, and season 4 has moved me on to graduate work: forgiveness, handed out wholesale, before it's earned, by a man who knows his own file is too thick to throw stones. I'm still behind him in the coursework. The difference is that now I know what the course is. Some of us get a therapist, some of us get a sponsor, and some of us get Jason Priestley playing a detective who forgives like it's his job—because on Private Eyes, it turns out, it is.

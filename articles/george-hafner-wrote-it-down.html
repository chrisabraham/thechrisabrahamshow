George Hafner Wrote It Down

An article by Chris Abraham, November 17, 2026

This article, read aloud:

Share: X · Facebook · LinkedIn · Reddit · Bluesky · Threads · Email

Yesterday, November 16, The Army Historical Foundation published “U.S. Army Chemical Corps Operations in Vietnam,” by Lieutenant Colonel George J. Hafner, Chemical Corps, USA-Ret.

I know George Hafner. I went to his son Keith’s wedding. George and his wife are lovely people, and somewhere along the way they became my friends and my proxy parents, the family a man acquires as an adult by being fed and worried over and claimed. I love the man.

The article

George served in Vietnam from July 1968 to July 1969 with the 1st Cavalry Division (Airmobile) in Quang Tri Province, just south of the DMZ. He commanded the 26th Chemical Detachment for his first months in country and then served as chemical officer for the division’s 2d Brigade. The article he has now written, in his eighties, is the catalogue of what his branch actually did in that war, drawn from his own service, the division’s operational reports for 1968 and 1969, and the 14th Military History Detachment’s 1971 study of chemical support in the division. Fifteen endnotes. His own photographs. The man did the reading for his own war.

The catalogue is the argument, and it is a working soldier’s catalogue. Chinooks hauling twenty-four fifty-five-gallon drums of persistent CS in a single lift, the drums rigged to burst a hundred feet over a bunker complex, the agent staying on the ground for up to thirty days, so infantry could walk into positions the enemy had already left. The E-158 cluster bomb for the opposite problem: ten minutes of incapacitation over a landing zone, long enough to get the first lift down. The XM-3 airborne personnel detector, the people sniffer, which read ammonia off the jungle canopy and turned hidden men into coded radio calls—ALPHA, BRAVO, CHARLIE, DELTA. George flew those missions himself, at treetop level, at eighty to ninety miles an hour, two detectors aboard, marking targets with smoke for the Cobra gunships flying with him. He prints the field reporting’s success figure, eighty to eighty-five percent under favorable conditions, and he prints the hazard in the same plain tone: the sniffer helicopters came back with bullet holes in them, and they crashed.

Flame next. Fougasse mines and Husch flares around the firebases, and flame and illumination in the perimeter fight at Landing Zone Grant in March 1969, when the position held. Then the improvisation that tells you the most about the man and his war. When Air Force fixed-wing support was unavailable, his people slung nets of napalm drums under Chinooks and dropped them on signal for the Cobras to ignite. On one mission the sling load was sized wrong. The release snapped the steel clamp up into the helicopter’s rear rotor blades, and the pilot afterward walked George up a ladder on the fuselage to show him the damage. George writes that he had preached sling length and payload weight to his own people until he was tiresome, and that he never forgot the lesson. Half a ton of napalm under a helicopter over jungle, enemy fire below, and the failure came from a measurement. That is a participant’s detail. No archive invents it, and no novelist improves it.

The rest of the catalogue runs the same way. Two-man chemical teams flown to tunnel mouths, one man going underground with detonation cord and bags of CS. Daily malathion spraying over the base camps to hold malaria down, and the numbers after the spraying stopped in late 1969: infection rates from about two percent to about fifteen. A captured rice cache, four hundred bags at fifty pounds each, enough to feed enemy units for months, sitting in triple-canopy jungle where it could not be hauled out. George recommended contaminating it in place with persistent CS. The division G-3 approved. His team of five flew in, buried a hundred bags of CS in the rice with detonation cord, and blew it. The cache and the ground around it were denied to the enemy for good.

The article carries his own photographs, and they do their own arguing. The young officer standing in front of the 1st Cavalry Division headquarters at Phuoc Vinh in 1969. An E-158 cluster bomb riding the cargo deck of a Huey. Napalm drums hanging in a cargo net under a Chinook. His detachment’s truck with bullet holes in it, parked at a base camp like a fact that needed no caption. A history written from paper alone can be argued with forever. A man printing his own pictures from his own war closes a certain kind of argument: he was there, the camera was his, and the holes in the truck are where he says they are.

And the close, which is two numbers. A division the size of the 1st Cavalry kept fewer than sixty officers and men on full-time chemical duties. Thirty-nine Chemical Corps soldiers were lost in the entire war, on the Chemical Corps Museum’s count, which George cites. Sixty men at a time did all of the above, inside a division of thousands. George ends his article the way he ran his detachment: with the count of his people.

The correction

Ask the street what the Chemical Corps did in Vietnam and the street says one word: defoliation. Agent Orange. Ranch Hand. George’s article opens with that association and corrects it twice, once on share and once on scope. On share: the Air Force flew about ninety-five percent of the spraying, on the Institute of Medicine figures George cites. The Corps developed, managed, and employed the tactical herbicides at ground level, with trucks, helicopters, boats, and hand sprayers around the perimeters. On scope: defoliation of any kind was one mission in a catalogue of a dozen, and the catalogue is the article. A branch of the Army got filed in public memory under the single most controversial thing it touched, and the rest of its war—the sniffers, the tunnels, the flame, the drums—stayed out of the public telling for fifty years, until the branch’s own officer recorded it. My newspaper’s special report this morning files the straight record around the article: the dioxin totals as a ladder, the settlement arithmetic, the allied court cases, each number with its instrument named.

Tell it yourself

The article matters past its subject. The men and women who were there are the primary source for that war, and the primary source is dying on schedule. George’s generation of officers is in its eighties. Every year the ratio shifts: fewer witnesses, more literature. And the literature is waiting with its hands open—the propellerheads and the lame historians and the stupid academics and the dumbass mini-politicians and the stupid professors, a whole clerisy that makes its living telling other people’s wars from secondary paper. They work from documents because documents are what survives. They arrange the documents around a thesis because a thesis is what their guild pays for. And the man who flew the sniffer at treetop level becomes a citation in somebody else’s argument about the war’s meaning. The missions flatten into the controversy. The controversy flattens into the verdict. The verdict is whatever the decade ordering the book needs it to be.

George refused the arrangement, and the refusal is the news. In his eighties he sat down and did the work himself: the operational reports, the detachment study, the museum’s count of the dead, his own photographs, including one of his detachment’s truck with the bullet holes showing. He wrote it in the first person, in a plain soldier’s prose, with his sources printed at the back where anyone can check him. He claimed no glory. He argued one correction, filed one catalogue, and ended with the count of his branch’s dead. That is what a primary source looks like when it decides to survive.

Every veteran of every war is sitting on the same choice George made. The telling defaults to the clerisy unless the witness takes it. Paper outlives people, the people who arrange paper for a living know it, and they are patient. The counterweight is witnesses writing now, while the writing is possible, in their own names, with their own photographs, saying I was there and this is the order it happened in. The historians will cite George’s article someday. Good. Let them. A citation of the man beats a reconstruction of the man every time, because the citation carries his order of events and his count of the dead, and the reconstruction carries the reconstructor’s.

I never wore the uniform. My standing in George’s life is a wedding I attended and a family’s kindness to their son’s friend, compounded over years into proxy parenthood, claimed from their side as much as mine. He and his wife are lovely people. I love the man, and I am proud that at his age, with his war fifty-seven years behind him, he did the unglamorous work of writing it down right. Read George’s article. His example is the hint, addressed to anyone who was ever there for anything: write yours down too, while the writing is possible.

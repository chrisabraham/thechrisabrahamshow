Ten Days on Hallow

An article by Chris Abraham, October 31, 2026

Share: X · Facebook · LinkedIn · Reddit · Bluesky · Threads · Email

Ten days. This morning I posted it to X the way men post gym selfies: 10 day streak! Eighty-one people saw it. One person liked it. One man answered, a fellow named Etho, and he said the truest thing that can be said about a streak: “Ten days in, the habit’s officially sticking.”

He is right, and it is the whole story. The streak is the story.

A Catholic app and a complicated man

The streak lives on Hallow, the number one Catholic meditation app, which makes me a walking ecumenical incident. I am not a Catholic at the moment. I am catholic full-time; the small c is doing heavy lifting in that sentence, and I mean it to. Catholic with a small c means universal: the whole thing, the whole Church in all its centuries. By that definition I qualify easily. By the Roman definition I do not, and Rome and I are both at peace with the arrangement.

The paperwork says Episcopal. I was confirmed into the Episcopal Church, and my taste runs smells and bells—Anglo-Catholic, the high end of the candle budget. I have not darkened a church door in a while. I am also a Freemason, dues paid, thirty-second degree, which adds a second set of dues, a second door, and a second room full of men who also believe ritual is a load-bearing wall. I am a Jesus freak with an Episcopal confirmation, a Masonic apron, and a Catholic app. My religious life has several memberships and no headquarters. The app does not care. The app just counts the days.

Red letters first

Here is what the days consist of. I fall asleep to Hallow most nights; scripture read aloud puts me straight out, and it has for a long while now. The new part is the daily Gospel, taken in order, and my attention sits exactly where the printers put the emphasis: the red letters. The words of Jesus, printed red so a tired man cannot miss whose voice it is.

I am reading the New Testament the way it is bound, front to back, Gospels first. After the red letters come Peter and Paul and all the rest of it, men writing letters about what the red letters meant. I am looking forward to them. Arguing about Jesus is the family business of the whole Church. I just want to hear the man himself first, at length, in order.

The whole routine, played to a sleeping man

The Gospel is only the head of the train. Hallow builds me a routine every day, and on the nights I wake at three or four in the morning, which is most nights, I start the whole thing rolling and let it run: the Daily Gospel; the day’s reflection (today’s is called “When Pride Pulls Up a Chair!”, which feels targeted); the men’s devotional; the Daily Exegesis; the contemplation; the homily—today’s is “All Hallowtide,” it being Halloween; the Angelus; the Desert Fathers with Bishop Erik Varden; the morning offering; the Mass readings; the Examen; and a rosary at the end of the line.

It is a lot of content, and most of it plays while I am asleep. Some mornings I sleep straight through the reflection, the men’s devotional, and the contemplation, and the Desert Fathers preach to a pillow. My calendar motto, every day of the year, is “Speak, Lord, for your servant is listening.” The servant is listening. The servant is also unconscious, and the Lord, so far, has not held it against him. The streak counts those days anyway. I have decided to count that as grace rather than bookkeeping.

There is a downside. Those overnight hours used to belong to Coast to Coast AM: Art Bell’s ghosts, George Noory’s UFOs, shadow people, remote viewers, the whole carnival of the American night, and I was an addict. The hours belong to Hallow now. My addiction to Coast to Coast AM has been curtailed by my addiction to God A.M. That is a terrible joke. It is also the truest sentence in this piece: the night got new management, and the new management reads the Gospel at me while I sleep.

Meanwhile, the machines have read my Substack

Ask Google’s AI Mode about me and Hallow and it will tell you that I spent Lent inside the app, came out the other side writing an essay called “The Tale Wagging the God,” and went back to Rome. The uncomfortable part is that the machine is mostly right. The essay exists. I published it in April, after a Lent spent inside Hallow, and it says what it says: I am going back to Rome. “Something cracked open” is a line from it. Those are my words, not the machine’s invention.

What the machine cannot hold is a season. It reads an essay written in the heat of April and reports it in October as a settled state—a conversion with the paperwork filed. The man who wrote that essay is the same man who said, three sections up, that he is not Catholic at the moment but catholic, and that he has not darkened a church door in a while. Both are true. They are true in different months. The AI has no months. It has a paragraph, and in the paragraph I am converted, filed, and done.

I am not done. I am ten days into a streak.

A buffalo, an ESV, and no thee or thou

Yesterday I pre-ordered a New Testament. Only the New Testament, bound in buffalo hide, about forty dollars. The binding pleases me more than it should. A buffalo hide can take a beating and come back asking for more; the New Testament has been doing exactly that for two thousand years. It seemed like a fair match.

It is an ESV, the English Standard Version, and that choice will start more arguments than the buffalo. I like the ESV because I can read it. The King James is magnificent and it is an antique, four hundred years old, dressed in an English nobody has spoken at a kitchen table in centuries. People hear reverence in the thee and the thou. I hear costume, and reverence that needs a glossary is performance. I read what I read, and what I read is a Bible that talks like a person.

Day eleven

Ten days is nothing. Ten days is also how every durable thing in my life started, one unremarkable day stacked on the last. The streak never asks what I believe on day ten. Belief comes and goes; I have told better Christians than me exactly that, and I hold to it on the days I hold to anything. The streak asks a smaller question: did you show up? Ten days running, the answer has been yes.

Tonight is day eleven. I will fall asleep to the Gospel, red letters and all, and Etho’s verdict gets one day truer.

The Isolationist Populist Gets My Vote

An article by Chris Abraham, November 19, 2026

This article, read aloud:

Share: X · Facebook · LinkedIn · Reddit · Bluesky · Threads · Email

In 2028, my vote for President of the United States goes to the isolationist populist. Whoever runs on that standard gets the vote, and the standard is the whole of what I am buying: an end to wars of choice and regime change, an end to democracy exported at gunpoint, and a government that answers to its own people first and spends its treasure and its attention at home.

If the Republicans nominate JD Vance and he runs as the conservative populist isolationist of his public record, the vote goes to Vance. If the Democrats nominate Alexandria Ocasio-Cortez and she runs as the progressive populist isolationist of hers, the vote goes to Ocasio-Cortez. The criterion outranks the tribe. The vote follows the standard, and the standard wears no jersey.

My ballot has never belonged to a tribe in the first place. I have voted for Democrats with enthusiasm and for Republicans with hope, and I have stayed home on a Tuesday when a coalition I joined broke its promises to me. The jerseys rotate. The standard is older than every party now standing: it is the foreign policy of the republic's first century and a half, the one that warned against foreign entanglements and declined to go abroad in search of monsters to destroy.

The terms, in plain English

Isolationist, as I use the word, describes a republic that keeps its armies at home, guards its own shores and its own skies, trades with every nation on earth, and leaves the governing of other nations to the people who live in them. Friendship, commerce, and honest dealing with all; garrisons, subsidies, and errands for none.

Anti-adventurism is the working half of the creed: the long missionary project of the American government, the standing assumption in Washington that foreign capitals are remodeling jobs and foreign peoples are raw material, is retired for good. Populist completes the definition. The government's first account is owed to its own citizens, and a president's mission field is his own country.

The sword stays sharp throughout. A powerful military guarding American soil, American waters, and American skies sits at the center of this creed, and the defense of the homeland is absolute and unquestioned. What retires is the errand, the rescue, the export. The republic keeps its strength and brings it home.

Alliances earn their keep the same way. An alliance that guards the homeland and its own hemisphere is an instrument of defense. An alliance that drafts American soldiers into other continents' quarrels is an errand wearing a treaty's clothes, and it gets judged as an errand.

The ledger

I have written the long version of the why on this page already, in Saving Pagan Babies: conquest dressed as rescue, conversion as the moral cover, collapse as the harvest. The short version fits in a paragraph, and the paragraph is a casualty list.

Iraq. Libya. Syria. Yemen. In each one, the United States and its partners knocked the lid off a strongman's state, and under the lid waited the war of all against all that the lid had been sitting on for generations. Afghanistan got twenty years of the complete missionary package, and the government built there collapsed in a matter of days in August 2021, while the builders were still drafting their after-action reports.

The order of operations never varied. The invasions ran at the tip of the spear, conquest first, soldiers through the door before any story was told about why they were there. Democracy arrived afterward, exported "with extreme prejudice" as the moral cover that followed the troops. The spear was never about saving anyone. The saving story was Act 2's costume, sewn while the shooting was already underway.

The Costs of War project at Brown University counts roughly 940,000 direct deaths in the post-9/11 wars. Its modeled estimates of the indirect deaths, the ones carried off by destroyed water systems, wrecked hospitals, and collapsed economies, run from 3.6 to 3.8 million human beings. I cite those indirect figures as what they are, the project's estimates, built on its stated method. The direction of the arithmetic survives any honest audit of the decimals.

My own rule of thumb, earned across decades of watching the pattern repeat: the unintended consequences and the blowback run generally seventy-seven times worse than anything the planners envisioned on the morning they set out to make the world a better place. Every wave arrived certain it was the exception. The saved were never asked whether they wanted saving. Cultures five thousand years old were fed into an American civics lesson, and the lesson plan kept ending in rubble.

The hypocrisy

At home, America holds cultural sovereignty sacred, and I hold it with her. Native Americans may be as Native American as they want, African Americans as African American, Latin Americans, West Africans, the Hmong: once Hmong, always Hmong. Every tribe in this country keeps its own beliefs, its own serenity of culture, even its language barriers, and nobody in public life would dare sand any of it down. Abroad, the same country brings every sovereign culture to heel and maps American Western hegemonic culture across it, turning everybody into pablum. Diversity at home and homogenization abroad cannot both be sincere. It's a Small World needs a break. Let it be a large world, where sovereignty means something.

Everybody in the exporting class wants the entire world federated based on California English: one cultural-linguistic monoculture, the values and dialect of the California professional class administered everywhere. NOT ME. I am the holdout, and this declaration exists because I refuse the desire. Their single real constraint is that they never want to be caught with blood on their hands, or cultural blood on their hands. So the project runs through aid agencies, NGOs, flags, and curricula rather than open conquest. The costume exists so the hands photograph clean.

A friend of mine, retired military, spent the Biden years working in our embassies, Warsaw and then Tbilisi, and he reports that through the whole Biden administration the Pride flag flew at the embassies as a matter of course. The week the administration changed, they were all gone. A flag is a standard planted. A standard over a foreign capital carrying the current administration's identity doctrine is the export project in cloth, and a thing that vanishes the week the government changes was never diplomacy. It was doctrine.

None of it washes back. At home the pluralism is absolute, and I defend it as absolute: Muslim, Somali, Indian, every community in America keeps its own flavor for exactly as long as it wants. Nobody has to learn English; English is not even the national language. The forms and the schooling run in every tongue, ESL ad infinitum, and that is the promise kept. The concession, honestly made: even under that promise, everybody knows the slow erosion starts anyway. Time and America sand people down across the generations without any program at all. We all know that second-generation people end up talking like Texans. From Vietnamese shrimping boats to full-on French Creole drawl, the Gulf Coast ran the whole experiment in two generations, with no program behind it at all. Two generations, start to finish. The weather needs no ministry and no budget. That is erosion by living, and erosion by living is weather. The grandchildren's culture is not the old one erased. It is a creole, something new minted on American soil: Creole Hmong, Hmong bones with a Texas accent. The weather never deletes a culture; it creolizes it. Only the machinery deletes. My objection stands against the deliberate, state-run conversion project abroad, the machinery, and it has never stood against the weather nobody controls. Total cultural sovereignty at home, total cultural conversion abroad: the asymmetry is the whole argument, and the conversion is what this vote refuses.

The mission at home

A nation's money, blood, and attention are finite, and a president spends all three from one purse. Every decade America spent remodeling other nations came out of the republic's own house: the hollowed-out towns, the bridges and water systems aging in place, the sons and daughters spent in other people's deserts, the treasury mortgaged to projects no voter ever approved in plain language.

Attention is the scarcest of the three. A president who wakes to cables from foreign capitals governs America in his spare time. A populist president reverses the order of the morning. His congregation is his own people: his street, his town, his county, his country, in that order, and the rest of the world receives a good neighbor behind a strong fence, trade on honest terms, and the dignity of being left alone to run its own affairs.

The monk has the right of it. Tend your own garden. The garden is enormous, and it has been waiting a long time for a gardener whose whole ambition is the garden.

Two vessels

Vance, in his public life, has argued against the forever-war consensus with a consistency rare in his party's leadership: skepticism toward open-ended aid, toward entanglements that outlive their purpose, toward a foreign policy written by people who never pay its bills. That is the conservative populist shape of the standard. Ocasio-Cortez, in hers, has opposed interventionism and military adventurism from the left, voting and speaking against the blank-check war state across her career in Congress. That is the progressive populist shape of the same standard.

Notice what the standard does to the familiar map. On this one question, the populist right and the populist left stand closer to each other than either stands to its own party's war wing. The circle bends at exactly one point, and the point is the one that decides whether American soldiers die in other people's countries.

I disagree with each of them on a long list of other questions, and I expect to go on disagreeing in print for years. The disagreements stand at full size. This vote has one question on it, and on that question the standard is the standard.

The conditional is the whole offer, and I mean it as an offer. Either party may pick the standard up. Either party may leave it lying on the ground. I am pricing my coin in advance, in public, so both parties know exactly what it costs to earn it.

The invoice

I voted for Donald Trump three times. Every vote was a transaction, and each one carried a single written invoice: no new foreign wars, no nation-building adventures abroad. That was the whole of the purchase—one foreign-policy promise, bought from the only seller offering it, three elections running. I have never been a Republican, and I never defended the man. I paid for a promise.

A second promise rode on the same invoice: an end to identity rule in domestic politics, the managerial practice of sorting Americans into categories and governing the categories. Nonintervention abroad and populism at home were the two lines on the bill. I paid for both.

The collection came in November. On the eve of the midterm elections, the United States launched strikes on Iran: the first line of the invoice, broken with bombs the night before the voting. A coalition that does that has defaulted, and a defaulted loan gets collected. I collected mine the only way a voter can. I stayed home on midterm Tuesday.

The bloc logic underneath all of this is older than the default. I wrote it out long form in June, in The Populist Bloc Just Switched Tickets: the populist bloc is loyal to a posture—anti-establishment, anti-technocrat, anti-forever-war—and it changes vehicles the moment the vehicle stops delivering. Passengers owe a vehicle nothing once it leaves the route.

So the 2028 offer in this piece is the same invoice, printed in advance this time. I have collected on a broken one before. Both parties know the terms. Now they know the collection is real, because it has already happened once.

The standard stands

Maybe neither party meets it. Parties are machines built for winning, and the war consensus has fed a great many careers in both of them. The standard stands anyway, written down where I can point to it in four years and in eight, and where the parties can point to it themselves the day one of them decides to earn it.

I have argued on this page that America fits BRICS better than NATO, that the republic's future is a strong house in its own hemisphere, minding its own region and its own people. This vote is that argument with a ballot attached to it.

There is a traveler's stake in it, too, and it is personal. If I ever backpack around the world, I want every country on the road to be more itself, deep in its own culture, its own norms, its own exemplar ways: the real Qatar, the real Malaysia and Indonesia and Cambodia, the real Stans, the real India and Bangladesh. The export project flattens all of it into a themed copy of America, a Disney or Vegas version of everywhere. A man who hopes to walk through the real world someday has his own selfish reason to leave it alone.

A vote is the one coin a citizen holds.

Mine is priced in a single currency now, and the price is printed on the coin: bring the republic home. Run on that, from the right or from the left, in a red jersey or a blue one, and the coin is yours. Spend another generation on the rescue business abroad, and I will keep the coin in my pocket and spend my one vote on the record instead, exactly as I am doing here.

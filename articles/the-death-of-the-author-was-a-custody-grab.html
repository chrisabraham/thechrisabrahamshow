The Death of the Author Was a Custody Grab

An article by Chris Abraham, November 23, 2026

This article, read aloud:

Share: X · Facebook · LinkedIn · Reddit · Bluesky · Threads · Email

The death of the author was announced as a liberation, and it functioned as a custody transfer. A book separated from its author is an orphan. Orphans do not stay free. Orphans get adopted, and the adopting is done by the people who administer orphans.

Plato described the condition twenty-four centuries before the doctrine got its French paperwork. In the Phaedrus, Socrates warns that a written text rolls about everywhere, reaching readers who understand it and readers who have no business with it, and the text cannot tell the difference. A text cannot answer a question put to it. A text cannot defend itself when it is attacked, twisted, or read against its grain. It needs its father, the man who wrote it, to stand beside it and protect it. The modern doctrine read that passage and drew the opposite moral: the father's absence was renamed the child's freedom, and the orphanage was renamed the republic of letters.

Roland Barthes published "The Death of the Author" in 1967 and supplied the legal theory. The Author, capital A, was demoted to a scriptor, a hand that happened to be holding the pen when the language wrote itself. Barthes said the quiet part in a single sentence: the birth of the reader must be paid for by the death of the Author. Jacques Derrida built the wider jurisprudence around the same holding—meaning unmoored from intention, the text a machine that runs on perfectly well with its maker struck from the record. Derrida even wrote the commentary on Plato's passage, a famous essay worrying the Phaedrus for fifty pages, and arrived at the orphanage anyway: the father demoted to a character inside his own text, his protection reclassified as a metaphysical superstition. The academy spent fifty years working out the procedure. The author's testimony about his own book became the one testimony a trained reader discounts on principle, the father's statement ruled inadmissible before the hearing opens.

I met this doctrine young, and I met it as good news. George Landow published Hypertext in 1992, arguing that the new electronic text and the French theory were the same event arriving by two roads, and I believed him completely. In 1994 I co-wrote My Name Is Scibe with Judy Malloy and six other writers on ARTS WIRE and The WELL, a hyperfiction fathered by a committee on purpose, built to fork. In 1997 and 1998 I helped build Item 8, a collaborative hypernarrative that ran on The Morning News, 241 files of story with the authorship deliberately smeared across the whole crew. In a hypertext the author builds the exits from his own work with his own hands and calls the building freedom. A text escaping its author looked like the entire promise of the medium. The reader would walk in through the links and write back. I was twenty-four when Scibe went up, and the doctrine felt like weather clearing. I have had thirty-five years to watch where the road runs. It runs to an office where orphans are processed. The faith of those years had a hole in it that none of us examined: we assumed the liberated text would be administered by its readers, one by one, at kitchen tables and terminals. We never asked who administers a text at scale, in the syllabus, in the franchise, in the press. Administration at scale is a profession, and professions have interests, and the first interest of every administrator is the custody that employs him.

Suzanne Collins is alive, and she has testified. The origin of The Hunger Games is on the record in her own words: a woman channel-surfing late at night between a reality television competition and footage of the war in Iraq, the two feeds blurring into one; behind that, the Greek story of Theseus and the children sent to the Minotaur; behind that, Spartacus and the arena. In November 2026 a new film in the series opened, and Maya Hawke, who plays Wiress in it, spent the press tour calling the franchise anti-fascist. The fans claimed the books for their side of the current argument with the confidence of heirs reading a will. Collins's stated sources, given in interview after interview across fifteen years, contain no Berlin and no fascism; they contain a war she watched on television and judged, a myth about children fed to a monster as tribute, and a slave who led a revolt in an arena. The record of her intentions is unusually complete. It changes nothing. Collins's own account of her own work was admitted into the discussion and set aside in the same motion, one reading among many, carrying no privilege for having been the reading of the woman who wrote the books. She is not dead. She is not silent. She has answered the question directly, in public, repeatedly. The doctrine exists to make her answer count for nothing.

J.K. Rowling is the completed case, the adoption after the paperwork has cleared. Her books have been formally reassigned in the public argument to their readers, who are said to own them now in a way she no longer does, and Rowling herself is discussed as an obstruction inside her own bibliography, a squatter in the house she built, to be routed around by film adaptations, theme parks, and fan custodians. During the quarrels over her, the doctrine was invoked by name, in print, by critics who needed a respectable theory under the taking: the author is dead, long live the readers. A slogan of the emancipation turned out to be a transfer form with a signature line. Whatever a person thinks of her positions, the mechanism is the exhibit and the mechanism is the same one running on Collins at an earlier stage: the best-selling living author in the world cannot get her testimony about her own work admitted at face value, because the court struck the father's testimony as a class, years before her particular case came up.

The seizure has a procedure, and the procedure has two steps, because a direct taking would embarrass everyone involved. The author is reclassified first. A woman who defends anything traditional—sex as a real category, the family as the unit that stands between the child and the state, her own novels as a warning against the central power—gets re-entered in the ledger as the female face of white male supremacy, an honorary white man, a handmaiden whose womanhood was a clerical error. The reclassification is an administrative act. It requires no argument about the books. Once it is entered, the second step changes its name: nobody has expropriated a woman of her life's work. Her readers have been liberated from a patriarch. The adoption proceeds without a contest because the contest was settled in the filing office.

The parallel underneath all of this is the custody doctrine in literary dress, and the dress is thin. Separate the child from the parent and the child becomes administrable by anyone carrying a form. Separate the book from the author and the book becomes administrable by anyone carrying a reading. Custody follows the presumption, in libraries as in law. The standing presumption of 2026 holds that texts belong to their readers, and a custody assigned to everyone in general is exercised in practice by whoever administers the reading at scale—the franchise, the fandom's officer class, the press tour, the syllabus committee. The orphan was promised freedom. The orphan got a case manager.

Authors do not own meaning forever, and any honest reader knows it from his own shelf. Shakespeare belongs to everyone, in part because no author survives to object and in part because four centuries of readers have found true things in those plays that no Elizabethan planted there. Books outgrow their makers as a matter of course. A reader who finds in a novel the sentence that saves his life has stolen nothing from anybody. D.H. Lawrence gave the other side its best witness, an author testifying against authors: never trust the teller, trust the tale. Lawrence had watched readers hang his own books with his own intentions, and his sentence survives because every reader has had the experience underneath it—a book that grew past the person who planted it, a meaning that arrived in the reading and was never in the writing. All of that is true, and none of it requires the doctrine. The line runs between a reading and a reassignment. A reading is free. A reassignment of custody is a taking, and the difference shows in the handling of one piece of evidence: the author's own testimony about the work, admitted as evidence or ruled inadmissible by doctrine before the case is heard. A court that refuses to hear the father has not freed the child. It has adopted him, and the refusal was the adoption hearing.

Katniss Everdeen, in the books Collins actually wrote, is a weapon of her time. She is nobody's timeless weapon. The story ends with the bow put away, the war over, the girl in the meadow listing the dead and getting on with the unglamorous work of living in District 12. A book with its author alive and testifying can be laid down the same way when its time ends, because the father can stand beside it and say what it was for and what it was never for. The orphaned book enjoys no such retirement. It can be conscripted forever, reissued every season, aimed at each year's Capitol in turn by hands the author never met, at targets she never chose, under a flag she never sewed. Kill the author and the book can never be laid down. It can only be reissued.

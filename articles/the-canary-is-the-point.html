The Canary Is the Point

An article by Chris Abraham, November 14, 2026

This article, read aloud:

Share: X · Facebook · LinkedIn · Reddit · Bluesky · Threads · Email

In America, it is not illegal to be an asshole. Words are not violence, and hate speech is the canary in the coal mine of the First Amendment: the hateful speaker is the case the amendment exists to cover, because free nice speech never needed an amendment and never will. A right is defined at its worst case, at the speaker nobody in the room can stand. The First Amendment is defined by protecting hateful speech exactly as the Second Amendment is defined by free and unfettered access to firearms, by poor people too, since a right only the rich can afford is a subscription. Every amendment has its own point of balance, its own equilibrium, its own canary, and the canary's whole job is to be the first thing the gas reaches. America is the only country on earth that wrote this down and meant it at full strength. Everywhere else, the thing called free speech is approved speech, kind speech, speech kept inside the mission and ministry of the government of the day. The American record of the last decade proves the exception from the inside: a government that could not jail its critics leaned on the companies that carry them, the companies folded, and the fold is the tell. And the government may not do by funding what it may not do by statute, which is why a publicly funded university that cancels a speaker has not escaped the amendment. It has run into it.

The canary

Free speech that everybody in the room approves of is a weather report. It costs nothing, risks nothing, and protects nothing, and no constitution in history has ever been drafted to guarantee a man's right to say the thing the ministry was about to say itself. The amendment covers the other speech: the sermon that empties the pews, the pamphlet that empties the room, the jerk on the street corner with the sign. Hate speech is the ultimate canary because the hateful man is the one speaker every generation wants to make the single exception for, and a speech right with a single exception is a speech right with a door in it, and doors get used. While the canary sings, every other speaker in the mine is safe by a wide and measurable margin. The day the canary stops, the margin starts moving, and the margin has never once moved toward more speech.

Words are not violence. Violence is a fist, a brick, a fire, and it is already illegal everywhere in this country under laws nobody disputes. The man who calls a word violence is asking for the police powers built for the fist to be laid on the word, and he is asking in the full knowledge that the fist-laws will be aimed by whoever holds them this season. Speech answers speech in the American settlement. The screech answers the speech, the counter-protest answers the march, and the state keeps its hands off both. That is the whole machine, and the canary is its gauge.

Each amendment keeps its balance at the same kind of point: the worst case, the case that makes decent people want to look away. The Second Amendment is defined by free and unfettered access to enough firearms, and the access has to reach the poor man or it reaches nobody, because a right priced out of a poor man's reach is a permission slip sold at a counter. The First is defined the same way, at the same altitude of discomfort. Its canary is the hateful speaker, standing exactly where the gas gathers first.

The only country

America is the only place with the First Amendment. Other countries have speech clauses, fine sentiments in handsome documents, and every one of them arrives with the exceptions already printed in the margins: speech is free insofar as it is responsible, insofar as it is kind, insofar as it does not wound the dignity of the state or the feelings of the approved. That is a license, and a license can be revoked by the office that issued it.

Germany runs the honest version of the license, honest enough to print its bans in the criminal code. Stand in the middle of a German street and quote Mein Kampf at the top of your lungs and the state will explain the settlement to you. Wear a swastika on a T-shirt and the shirt itself is contraband, the symbol banned by law, the opinion made a crime before it finishes becoming a sentence. Germany decided that certain ideas are too dangerous to be heard and built the decision into the police power, and Germany calls the remainder free speech.

England runs the genteel version. The English have no First Amendment and no equivalent, and what they have instead is a long practice of the police arriving over words: people arrested for social media commentary, for posts and jokes and memes that step outside the mission and ministry of the government they live under. The knock comes for the unkind post, the wrong opinion, the joke told in the wrong tense. Speech that the authorities approve of flows freely in England, and speech they disapprove of is a police matter with a case number. Approved speech is free speech in London and in Berlin alike. In America the asshole on the corner is the proof of the opposite system, and he is load-bearing.

The COVID proof

The doctrine got its field test during COVID, and the test exposed the one workaround the amendment had left open. The platforms took down dissent by the ton: doctors off-script on the virus, citizens off-script on the lockdowns, jokes, questions, data. The defense was ready-made and legally tidy. A private company can do whatever it wants. The companies were doing whatever they wanted, and what they wanted was whatever the government told them to want.

The practice has an old name, jawboning, and it has a paper trail with dates on it. In Bantam Books v. Sullivan, decided in 1963, the Supreme Court looked at a state commission that merely wrote letters to book distributors, suggesting certain titles be withdrawn, and ruled the letters coercion all the same. In 2024, in National Rifle Association v. Vullo, a unanimous Court said it again: a government official may not coerce a private intermediary into punishing speech the government disfavors. In August 2024, Mark Zuckerberg wrote to Congress that senior Biden administration officials had repeatedly pressured Meta to censor COVID content, humor and satire included, and wrote the sentence the decade will be quoted by: “I believe the government pressure was wrong.” In September 2025, Google told Congress the Biden administration had pressed it over content on YouTube, and creators thrown off the platform under those policies were offered their channels back. The record itself, the letters and the admissions and the dockets, stands in the special reports at Objective Daily News, for anybody who wants the receipts beside the doctrine.

Coercion in this system never wears a uniform or serves a warrant. It arrives as the noticed-things treatment. We noticed your taxes. We noticed your merger. We noticed your Section 230, the liability shield that says a platform is not responsible for what its users say, the shield the entire business model stands under, held over the company's head on a thread by the same government asking for favors. Behave, and the shield holds. The takedowns and the shadow bans followed the requests the way weather follows a front, and almost none of it ever passed through a legislature, a vote, or a signature. A censorship arrangement with no censor to sue is the perfected form of the thing the First Amendment was written to prevent, built entirely out of private companies doing whatever they wanted.

My own small exhibit

I am a minor exhibit in my own argument. My YouTube channel, youtube.com/chrisabraham, was down for three years. Three years, the whole channel, gone. It has been restored in full, everything back, and I still cannot quite believe it. Nobody ever told me what I had done. There was no charge, no hearing, no sentence, and no appeal with a person at the end of it. A private company did whatever it wanted, and what it wanted changed when the weather changed.

My belief about the restoration, stated as a belief, is that Elon Musk bought X and broke the arrangement. One platform defected, said the quiet parts out loud, published its own files, and the other companies felt the boot come off their necks. The pressure had never been law. It had been weather, and weather can change in a season. I wrote the platforming half of this doctrine in Here's Your Warning: everybody should be platformed, because platforming is not quarantine. The channel coming back is the same point wearing my own name. The speech was never adjudicated. It was just switched off, and then, when the wind shifted, switched on.

The mirrors

This morning's small case carries the whole doctrine in miniature. Students at a New Jersey university revolted over an event with the former commander of the Border Patrol, invited to campus by the student Republicans, and the revolt is being reported as a free-speech scandal. The screeching is speech. Shouting down a speaker is still free speech, because the Constitution deals with government and how government treats the people; it does not restrict counter-protesting, and a crowd with lungs is not the state. The line is violence. Short of violence, the students are standing inside the same amendment as the man they came to shout at, and so is he.

The mirror flips when the institution moves. A publicly funded university cannot cancel an event because of the content of speech an accredited student organization has invited. The hecklers exercising their rights is America working as designed. The university caving is the government acting, with the taxpayers' building, the taxpayers' police, and the taxpayers' money. Indirect restriction by a publicly funded university, a publicly funded library, a publicly funded anything, is government restriction wearing a longer lever, and the amendment reaches the whole length of the lever.

The funding lever runs in both directions, and it always has. Federal money has always come with the Constitution attached; the conditions are the enforcement the amendment carries into every institution the taxpayer funds. The Trump administration has saber-rattled and, in cases, actually withheld federal money from public institutions that suppress or deny free speech, and that is the same lever pointed the other way, at institutions doing by policy what the state may not do by law. A man who loves the lever only when it points at his enemies is doing politics. The doctrine is colder and simpler: the money was never a gift, the rules ride on it in both directions, and the first rule on the money is the First Amendment.

In America it is not illegal to be an asshole, words are not violence, and hate speech is the canary of the First Amendment, because a right is defined at its worst case: the First by the hateful speaker, the Second by unfettered access to firearms that reaches poor people too. America stands alone in writing the protection at full strength. England and Germany protect approved speech and police the rest, down to banned symbols and arrests over posts. When the American government could not lawfully silence its COVID critics, it leaned on the platforms carrying them, the platforms folded under pressure now admitted in their own letters to Congress, and one man's purchase of one platform changed the weather enough to give a three-year-dead channel its life back. The crowd may screech, because the crowd is not the government. The funded institution may not cancel, because the funded institution is. There is no ending to this story, and none gets invented here. The canary does not get a conclusion. It either keeps singing, or the mine is already filling, and the men underground learn which one in the order the gas reaches them. In America the bird is the hateful man with the sign, the jerk on the corner, the asshole the amendment was written for, since it was never written for anybody else.

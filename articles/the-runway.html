The Runway

An article by Chris Abraham, November 20, 2026

This article, read aloud:

Share: X · Facebook · LinkedIn · Reddit · Bluesky · Threads · Email

For many women, nine-tenths of a date has already happened before the man arrives. The pleasure lives in the runway: the days of imagining the evening, dressing it, casting it, running it forward in the mind until the real night has a finished production to answer to. A friend of mine named Mark put the number on it years ago, and the number has held up in every clearing I have watched since: ninety percent of the event is anticipation. The date itself is the last ten percent, and it gets graded against the ninety.

Mark's observation

Mark is a careful watcher of his own habitat, and his report came from years of evenings that went well on paper and landed flat in person. The restaurant had been right. The conversation had been easy. The report that came back afterward carried a disappointment out of proportion to anything that had happened at the table, and for a long time he filed that disappointment under mystery. His correction, when it arrived, was simple: the evening he attended was a different evening from the one she attended. She had been living inside hers for two weeks.

The runway starts at the invitation. A date set for Saturday week gives the imagination a long, lit strip to work with. She pictures the room, the dress, the moment of arrival, the version of the man who will stand up when she enters. Friends receive the early reports and add their own lighting. By the night itself, the event has a cast, a score, and a third act, and one animal at the table has seen the whole show already, several times, in a theater where nothing ever goes wrong.

The production

Hyperphantasia is the clinical name for a mind's eye that runs in high resolution, and it is common equipment. The woman with a vivid inner screen can taste the first course on Tuesday, hear the exact sentence of the compliment on Thursday, and feel the goodnight at the door on Friday, all before a single real minute has passed. Foreplay, in this economy, outranks the act it precedes, and anticipation outranks the date it announces. The runway is where the feeling is manufactured. The evening is where the feeling gets audited.

Psychology has names for the machinery. Affective forecasting is the mind's habit of predicting how a future event will feel. Impact bias is the error inside the forecast: the predicted feeling runs stronger and longer than the real one ever does. Focalism is the tunnel the forecast travels through, with the date glowing at the center and the ordinary weather of a real evening—traffic, a tired waiter, a headache building since lunch—left outside the frame. The production in her head has been edited by all three, and the edit is ruthless: every scene that survives is a good scene.

The contract nobody signed

Inside the production sits a script. The script assigns the man his lines: the look at the door, the sentence about the dress, the plan revealed at the right moment, the ease that proves he arranged everything while appearing to arrange nothing. Therapists call an arrangement like this a covert contract—an agreement one party has written, signed, and filed, while the other party has never seen the document. He is performing under terms he never received, in a play he never read, opposite an actress who has rehearsed her part for fourteen days.

The grading follows from the setup. A man at that table can have a genuinely good evening and still fail the audit, because the audit compares his evening against hers, and hers had better weather. He brought flowers; the production had him bringing the specific flowers she mentioned once in March. He chose a fine restaurant; the production had a table she had already sat at in her mind, with a view she had already admired. Reality competes against a fiction with an unlimited budget and no closing time, and reality loses on points most nights, through no crime of its own.

Mark coined a blunt name for the state this leaves behind: lady blue balls. The phrase is barroom language for a precise condition—the ache of an arousal, emotional in this case, that built for days toward a climax the real event failed to deliver. The build was real. The letdown is real. The man hears the letdown as a verdict on his evening. The record shows a verdict on the gap between two evenings, only one of which he was invited to.

The signal

A production needs raw material, and the material is the signal a man emits before the date: his messages, his voice on a call, the photograph, the remembered sentence from the night they met, the plan he described in three words and left unbuilt. The fantasy is constructed entirely from his emissions, which gives the signal a weight in this economy that the man himself rarely suspects. He thinks the date starts when he arrives. In her theater, he has been on stage for two weeks, performing in scenes he directed by accident.

The signal has one great advantage over the man: the signal never has a tired Tuesday. The signal never arrives with the day on its face, never checks a phone at the wrong moment, never tells a story ten minutes too long. The signal is the man with the fatigue edited out and the intention edited in, a highlight animal assembled from his best clearings. Every real animal competes poorly against his own highlight reel. The reel is also his work: she built the production, and he supplied every brick.

Grieving the fantasy

When the evening lands below the production, the feeling that follows has the shape of grief, and grief is the correct filing. A world she lived in for two weeks has closed. The disappointment at the table is mourning at a small scale, for an event that existed richly in one mind and nowhere else, and the mourning is real even though the loss is imaginary. Observers who mock the tears misunderstand the specimen. She is crying over a death. The death is of a fiction, and the fiction was hers, and she had moved into it.

Valentine's Day and the anniversary run on the same machinery at industrial scale. The calendar hands the imagination a fixed date and months of runway, and the culture paves the strip with advertising until the production carries the budget of a film. The man who treats February 14 as a dinner reservation has misunderstood the assignment. He is opening against a premiere that has been in previews since January.

The aphant at the premiere

My own field notes come from the far bank of this river. I have aphantasia: no mind's eye, no inner screen, no runway. I have never pictured a date before it happened, because I cannot picture anything. Every evening I ever took a woman to was a premiere for me—cold open, no previews, the whole event built live in the room or nowhere. The women across those tables had, in many cases, already seen a version of the evening I was improvising. We were attending different events in the same chairs, and for years I lacked the concept that would have explained the grading.

The condition carried one accidental advantage, and the picnic-basket years ran on it. A man who cannot pre-live an evening puts all of his craft into the happening itself, because the happening is the only text he will ever hold. The basket got packed like a stage set. The blanket, the river, the kayaks, the food nobody at the table had tasted before—each element had to work in the room, in real time, against no competition from a production in my own head. I was competing against hers without knowing the contest existed, and live craft was the only weapon my equipment allowed me. The record suggests it was the right weapon, aimed by accident.

The amended entry

The runway reframes the whole ledger of evenings that puzzled the men who reported them. The good date that felt flat afterward, the ordinary date remembered as magic because it matched a production the man never saw, the fury out of proportion to a small failure at the table—each entry reads cleanly once the ninety percent is entered into the account. The man was present for the last tenth of an event and graded on the whole. She was present for all of it, including the nine-tenths he never witnessed.

The practical knowledge is modest and usable. A man who understands the runway tends his signal like the opening act it is: clear plans, kept promises in the small things, material worth building with. He asks, sometimes, what she has been picturing, and treats the answer as field data rather than pressure. He holds the grading loosely when an evening lands flat for reasons the table cannot explain. The flatness may belong to a closed production, a grief with no villain, a fiction that died of its own budget. The notebook records the runway as part of the habitat now: invisible, load-bearing, and running for two full weeks before the man ever stands up at the table and smiles.

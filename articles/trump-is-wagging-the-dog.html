Trump Is Wagging the Dog

An article by Chris Abraham, November 24, 2026

This article, read aloud:

Share: X · Facebook · LinkedIn · Reddit · Bluesky · Threads · Email

Donald Trump is wagging the dog. He is the maestro of his own presidency, and he was winning so easily—too much winning, his own line—that he handicapped himself with a blue wave midterm to give himself a fight worth having.

Too much winning is his own line. He said it on the trail in 2016—we're going to win so much you may get tired of winning. A decade in, he got there. The winning came too easy, and an easy game bores a maestro. So he let the Democrats take both chambers in November. Not lost. Let. A handicap is weight a strong player carries on purpose so the contest stays interesting.

In Wag the Dog, a president's fixers invent a war in Albania and hire a Hollywood producer to stage it for the cameras. The spectacle is the tail and the public is the dog, and the tail exists to wag the dog. Trump collapsed the credits. He is the fixer, the producer, and the star in one person, with his own hand on the leash. The Trump is wagging the dog.

Trump is not the monster of this story. He is the hero of it, and he has been the hero from the first frame. The monster is a painting the Democrats made. It has three panels: ICE agents in the streets, deportation flights on the tarmac, and Roe v. Wade reversed by a court he built. They lettered MONSTER underneath and hung it in a gilt frame, and for two years they have lost their minds over their own painting—the marches, the cable panels, the late-night monologues, all of it on schedule.

He wears the painting. A man who hated it would spend his life fighting the painters. Trump put his Fulton County mugshot on T-shirts, coffee mugs, and flags within days of it being taken, and it raised him a fortune. The portrait is wardrobe. Every time they hang it again, they dress him for his next scene at their own expense.

His schooling was spectacle. He learned heat in the New York tabloids. He staged WrestleMania at Trump Plaza twice. He sat at the head of a boardroom table for fourteen seasons of The Apprentice. At WrestleMania 23, his man beat Vince McMahon's man, and Trump climbed into the ring and shaved McMahon's head while the crowd came out of its seats. Stone Cold Steve Austin has tackled him on that canvas, and he sold the fall like a professional. He knows to the dollar what a booing crowd is worth.

Wrestling runs on the face and the heel. The face is the hero the crowd pays to love, and the heel is the villain the crowd pays to hate, and a card with no heel on it sells no tickets. Trump has never worked the face's job. He works the heel's job with a face's confidence, because the best heels do, and because in the mirror he sees the face—he believes he is the hero, and a squeaky-clean hero draws no crowd. The movies call that figure the antihero. John McClane spends Die Hard barefoot, bleeding, and outnumbered, crawling through the air ducts of the Nakatomi Tower while the men who seized the building hold every floor. He ruins the hostages' evening and the FBI's plan, and he is the only man in the building who knows how the picture ends.

A hero's story needs the beating in the middle. Drago beats Rocky half to death before the comeback can exist. A man who wins wire to wire gets a parade, not a movie.

Trump's first Drago was the 2020 election. He lost the building and spent four years outside it, indicted and counted out by every professional counter in the business. Then he won it back. A president voted out and voted back in had happened once before—Grover Cleveland, 1892—and not once in the century and a third since. That was comeback number one, and a story that ends there is a one-picture deal. Halfway through the second term, undefeated, he had no third act left. So he took the second beating, on schedule, and he took it from his own side of the board: he let the midterms go.

The receipts, as reported: the Democrats took the Senate 56 seats and the House at least 258, their largest House majority since 1992. They gained 44 House seats, more than the 40 of the 2018 wave, and ran up the largest midterm House popular-vote margin since 1974, as the Associated Press reported it. Nick Fuentes spent the year telling his followers to stay home and then, on Election Day, to get out and vote Democrat. Trump accepted all of it. He praised the Democrats for campaigning hard. He blamed the loss on gasoline—short-term pain at the pump from the Iran war—and said the voters felt it and he understood. He offered to work with the new majority.

I stayed home on Election Day, and the returns say I had plenty of company.

Now the Democrats hold the tower. They have the committees, the subpoenas, the gavel, all forty floors of it. The hero is inside the building alone with a veto pen and two years of running time. Bob Good urged him to become the veto president, and Trump took the line up in his own words: they'll send over things that are insane, and he'll veto them. The arithmetic is plain. Overriding a veto takes 290 votes in the House and 67 in the Senate. The new majorities stand at 258 and 56. Everything they pass lands on his desk, and the desk is where it stops.

The last fight was never going to be overseas. Communism in Beijing or Havana is another studio's picture. His enemy is the rot inside his own country—the departments, the courts, the campuses, and the commissions that painted him and spent a decade trying to end his picture early—and his weapons are the ones a lone man in a taken building actually holds: the veto, the executive order, the pardon, the podium, and the clock.

The ending the production has promised since the first act is in kitchen-table denominations: eggs down, gasoline down, jobs back, paychecks heavier, the country rosy again—red, white, and blue, and pink in the cheeks. A lame-duck presidency is supposed to shuffle out a side door while the new tenants measure the drapes. This one ends with the maestro walking out of the tower at dawn with the girl and the day, having carried the weight he put on himself, on purpose, because the game was too easy without it.

Trump Is Wagging the Dog

An article by Chris Abraham, November 24, 2026

This article, read aloud:

Share: X · Facebook · LinkedIn · Reddit · Bluesky · Threads · Email

Donald Trump is wagging the dog. In Wag the Dog, the 1997 movie, a president's fixers invent a war in Albania and hire a Hollywood producer to stage it for the cameras. In that picture the spectacle is the tail and the American public is the dog, and the tail's one job is to move the dog. The producer works in the open, in front of the cameras, and he is also the star. He writes the spectacle, casts himself as its lead, and works the dog—the press corps, the Democratic Party, the whole howling country—with his own hand on the leash.

I believe that is how Trump is playing it. How he understands his own presidency, from inside his own head. The training was all spectacle. He learned heat in the tabloids. He staged WrestleMania at Trump Plaza twice. He spent fourteen seasons on The Apprentice turning a boardroom into a weekly morality play, with himself as the judge at the head of the table. A man with that schooling runs a presidency the only way he knows how: as a production. Ten years of the man fit the production. The fits are the evidence.

The movie even hands him the motto. In Wag the Dog, the fixer asks why a dog wags its tail, and the producer answers: because the dog is smarter than the tail. If the tail were smarter, the tail would wag the dog. A tail with a mind of its own runs the animal. Trump changed the staffing. The tail, the dog, and the man holding the leash are one cast member.

The Painting

The Democrats have spent two years hanging a portrait. It has three panels. ICE agents in the streets. Deportation flights on the tarmac. Roe v. Wade reversed by a court he built. Every panel is painted in the same red, and the word lettered under the portrait, inside the gilt frame, is MONSTER. The party that hung it has lost its mind in public over every panel. The marches in the avenues. The screaming panels on cable. The late-night monologues. On schedule, at full volume, for two years.

He wears the portrait. A man who hated the costume would fight the painters in court and in print every morning of his life. Trump sells the costume. His Fulton County mugshot, the most famous monster portrait of the decade, was on T-shirts, coffee mugs, and flags within days, and it raised him a fortune and a following. The painting is wardrobe. Every time the Democrats hang it again, they dress their opponent for his next scene, and they pay for the privilege.

The Heel

Professional wrestling runs on two jobs. The face is the hero the crowd pays to love. The heel is the villain the crowd pays to hate, and the heel's labor has a name: heat. Heat fills the building. A card with no heel on it is a card nobody buys. A hero with nobody to beat is a man standing in an empty ring, holding a belt he won from the air.

Trump has only ever worked the heel's job, and he works it with a face's confidence. Chest out, chin up, grinning into the hard camera while the boos roll down off the rafters. The best heels always do. The booing is the sound of the act working. And in the mirror the man sees the face. Donald Trump believes he is the hero of this picture, and the picture is built so that he is right. The crowd sees a villain because a villain is what the crowd was sold. By him. On purpose. At a markup.

WrestleMania 23, 2007, the Battle of the Billionaires: Trump's man beat Vince McMahon's man in front of a stadium, and Trump climbed into the ring and shaved McMahon's head bald while the crowd came out of its seats. Stone Cold Steve Austin has tackled him on that canvas, and he sold the fall like a professional. The man has drawn heat in an actual arena, for actual money. He knows to the dollar what a booing crowd is worth at the gate.

The job has a name in the movies: the antihero. John McClane spends Die Hard barefoot, bleeding, outnumbered, crawling through the air ducts of the Nakatomi Tower while the men who seized it hold every floor. He is rude. He is reckless. He ruins the hostages' evening and the FBI's plan, and he is the only man in the building who knows how the movie ends. A squeaky-clean hero draws no crowd and saves no one. The antihero takes the beating and the boos on schedule, because the beating and the boos are the price of the last reel.

The First Beating

Every Rocky picture is built the same way: the middle rounds belong to the other man. Drago beats Rocky half to death in the middle of their fight—Dolph Lundgren, a mountain of Soviet chemistry, standing over the canvas—before the comeback can exist. A hero who wins wire to wire has a parade. A hero who gets carried back to his corner has a movie.

The Biden election was Trump's first Drago. The hero lost the building in 2020 and spent four years outside it: indicted, impeached in the public prints, counted out by every professional counter in the business. Then came the first comeback, and the comeback has exactly one precedent in the record. A non-consecutive presidency. A man voted out and voted back in. Grover Cleveland did it in 1892, and nobody had done it in the century and a third since. In a news story, a beaten man goes home and writes a memoir. In Trump's genre, the beating is the floor the return is built on. He came back in 2024 with the girl, the belt, and the building.

The Second Beating, Taken on Schedule

A script that ends there is a one-picture deal. Halfway through the second term the production needed its second Drago. A hero entering his last two years undefeated has no third act left to play. So the midterms were allowed to happen to him.

The receipts, as reported: the Democrats took both chambers in November. Fifty-six seats in the Senate. At least 258 in the House, their largest House majority since 1992. Nick Fuentes spent the year ordering his followers to stay home, then to vote Democrat, and on Election Day he told them to get out and vote Democrat. A saboteur inside the hero's own camp. The wave arrived at full size.

In the 2018 midterms, his first time through this reel, the Democrats gained 40 House seats and called it a wave. This November they gained 44, took the Senate outright, and ran up the largest House popular-vote margin of any midterm since 1974, as the Associated Press reported it. By every ordinary measure the sequel outdid the original. The second Drago has to hit harder than the first, or the comeback reads as a rerun.

He accepted the results. He praised the Democrats for campaigning hard. He laid the loss on gasoline—short-term pain at the pump from the Iran war, a price he said the voters felt and he understood—and he offered to work with the new majority, while voices on his own side began naming his next role for him: the veto president. As politics, those moves are surrender. As production, they are stage management. The Democrats now hold the building. The committees, the subpoenas, the floor, the gavel. They have taken the tower, all forty floors of it, and the hero is inside it alone with a veto pen and two years of running time.

I stayed home on Election Day, and the returns say I had plenty of company. The crowd a heel works hardest is his own, and a crowd that believes the beating is real goes home sick and comes back hungry.

The Last Two Years

The final fight in this script was never scheduled abroad. The enemy in the last reel is the rot inside his own country: the permanent government that painted him, the departments and courts and campuses and commissions that spent a decade trying to end his picture early. Communism in Beijing or Havana is another studio's production. His war is domestic, and it is fought with the weapons a lone man in a taken building actually holds. The veto. The executive order. The pardon. The podium. The clock. The majority can pass whatever it can carry. Every page of it lands on his desk. And the arithmetic of the desk is absolute: overriding a veto takes two-thirds of both chambers, 290 votes in the House and 67 in the Senate, and the new majorities stand at 258 and 56. The veto pen, in this Congress, is a wall with a man behind it. The Democrats hold the building. The building's last door has his name on it.

The comeback's promised receipts are kitchen-table denominations, the ones the production has advertised since the first act: eggs down, gasoline down, jobs back, paychecks heavier, the country healthy and rosy again. Red, white, and blue, and pink in the cheeks. A lame-duck presidency is supposed to end in a shuffle and a whisper, the old man leaving by a side door while the new tenants measure the drapes. This one is scheduled to end with the razzle-dazzle up. The villains beaten inside the building they took. The hero walking out of the tower at dawn with the girl and the day.

He took the beating to buy these two years. The country is about to watch what he purchased with it.

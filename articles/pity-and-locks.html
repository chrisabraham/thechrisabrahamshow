Pity and Locks

An article by Chris Abraham, November 20, 2026

This article, read aloud:

Share: X · Facebook · LinkedIn · Reddit · Bluesky · Threads · Email

Cora Clancy was five. Dawson Clancy was three. Callan Clancy was eight months old. They lived in a house in Massachusetts with their mother and father, and in January 2023 their mother strangled them. Cora died. Dawson died. Callan died. Any account of this case begins there, with three children who had barely started, and every argument that follows answers to them or it answers to nothing. The law will spend years sorting the mother. The children are already sorted, permanently, and a notebook that starts anywhere else has started in the wrong place.

What she admitted, what she sought

Lindsay Clancy admitted strangling her three children. The admission stands in the record. The defense was postpartum psychosis and insanity, and the record of her seeking help is long and plain. Fourteen psychiatrist visits in five months. Thirteen medications in the same span. An inpatient stay. A woman went to the professionals again and again and told them, in the ways patients tell, that something inside her was wrong. The system saw her. The system prescribed for her. The system admitted her and discharged her. Then January came.

Watch that sequence the way a naturalist watches a troop move through a clearing. Each animal did the thing its role describes. The mother asked for help. The psychiatrists saw her and adjusted the medications. The hospital took her in and let her out. Nobody in the sequence behaved as a villain in a story. The sequence still ended on a floor with three small bodies. A habitat can fail without a single animal in it acting from cruelty, and that failure is the hardest kind to look at, because it offers nobody to blame cleanly and demands a harder question instead.

The jury that almost agreed

Her trial in the summer of 2026 ended on September 4, 2026, in a mistrial. The jury deadlocked eleven to one, with eleven jurors favoring not guilty by reason of insanity. Eleven ordinary people heard the evidence, heard the psychiatrists, heard the account of those five months of seeking help, and landed on insanity. One held out. The deadlock is itself a field note. A jury that close to unanimity has seen something real in the defense. The illness, as presented in that courtroom, persuaded nearly everyone who sat and listened to all of it.

A retrial is scheduled for March 29, 2027. In November 2026 the Massachusetts Supreme Judicial Court agreed to hear a double-jeopardy appeal, a claim that the state gets one fair attempt and that a second trial crosses a constitutional line. Outside the court, supporters rallied, many of them women wearing pink. They stood for a mother they saw as ill and failed by the medicine she begged for. Their standing there is behavior worth recording exactly, without heat. They had watched a woman ask for help fourteen times and receive a process, and they drew the conclusion their observation supported.

Explanation answers one question

A diagnosis explains. Postpartum psychosis, if that is what held her, explains how a mother who sought help for months came to strangle the children she sought help to protect. Explanation does real work. It tells the family what happened inside her. It tells medicine where its net tore. It tells the next mother presenting with the same symptoms that the danger is real and documented and must be met with more than another prescription. Explanation is a map of the interior, and the map in this case is detailed and sad and credible to eleven jurors.

Disposition asks a different question. Disposition asks where this person can safely be, given what she did. The two questions live in different rooms. A courtroom can answer the first with complete sincerity and still face the second standing untouched in the doorway. The act itself sets the terms of the second question. Three children are dead by her hands, by her own admission. Whatever moved the hands, the hands moved, and the danger standard is met by the act alone. A society deciding disposition starts from the bodies, because the bodies are the evidence of what can happen when the interior breaks again.

The old sanitariums understood the split

The old sanitariums held people with genuinely sad diseases. That was their population. Men and women whose minds had broken in ways that drew pity from anyone who spent an hour beside them, and who were still kept behind walls because the breaking made them unsafe for the street. The keepers of those places held both facts at once without strain. The disease was real and pitiable. The custody was necessary. Pity described the feeling owed to the person. The lock described the duty owed to everyone the person might meet on a bad day inside her own head.

Modern argument keeps trying to collapse those two facts into one, as if the pity, proved strongly enough, dissolves the lock. It runs the other way in a sane system. The stronger the illness, the stronger the explanation, and the stronger the explanation for an act this grave, the clearer the disposition becomes. A mind that can be taken over so completely that it strangles three children while their mother begs doctors for help is a mind that cannot be certified safe by a verdict form. The insanity defense, believed fully, argues for secure custody with more force than a guilty verdict does. Prison at least imagines a sentence that ends.

What an acquittal actually means in Massachusetts

A verdict of not guilty by reason of insanity in Massachusetts means commitment to a secure facility. The acquitted person goes to a locked psychiatric hospital, evaluated and held, released only when the state is persuaded the danger has passed, and that holding can run longer than a prison term for the same act. The pink outside the courthouse and the lock inside the facility belong to the same outcome. Supporters who want the illness believed are, whether they have priced it or not, asking for a woman to be held in custody by the state, possibly for the rest of her life, under a medical name instead of a penal one.

That is the ground where pity and custody meet without either one surrendering. She can be ill. She can have been failed by every professional who adjusted a dosage and sent her home. She can deserve the grief of strangers in pink standing in the cold for her. She can also be a person who, by her own admission, killed Cora and Dawson and Callan, and who therefore lives where the state can watch her, treat her, and keep the door locked until the watching persuades it otherwise. Both ledgers stay open at the same time. Closing one to honor the other is sentiment doing the work of judgment.

The sympathy ledger

Critics of the rallying sympathy called it toxic empathy, and the phrase points at a real allocation problem, even for a reader who dislikes the phrase. Sympathy is a resource a culture spends, and the spending shows its priorities. In this case the sympathy gathered around the living woman who could be interviewed, diagnosed, and defended, while the three dead children became the premise of other people's arguments. Cora at five had a whole observable life ahead of her, the entire field of it, and the same was true of Dawson at three and Callan at eight months. The notebook records them as individuals first, because they were individuals first, and their mother is a specimen in this habitat too, observed with the same patience and the same refusal to sneer.

She gets the patience in full. A woman who visited psychiatrists fourteen times in five months was trying, by every observable measure, to be caught before she fell. The falling happened anyway, through a net woven of appointments and prescriptions and one inpatient stay. Recording that honestly costs the disposition argument nothing. It strengthens it. A net that thin, torn by an illness that strong, argues for the strongest custody the law keeps, delivered with treatment, with dignity, and with the pity the case has earned many times over. Pity and locks. The culture keeps presenting them as opponents at a hearing. They are roommates. They have always been roommates, in every sanitarium that ever held a sad and dangerous person kindly, behind a door that stayed shut.

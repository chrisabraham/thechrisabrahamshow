Why Matt Shade Is Not a Douchebag

An article by Chris Abraham, October 26, 2026

This article, read aloud:

Share: X · Facebook · LinkedIn · Reddit · Bluesky · Threads · Email

I put the question to a panel before I put it to myself: why is Matt Shade not a douchebag? Google AI Mode, Claude, ChatGPT, Gemini, and Grok all took a run at it, and while they could not agree on Tex's last name—it is Clarkson; I checked—they all landed on the verdict I have been circling for four seasons of Private Eyes. I am at the beginning of the fourth episode of season 4 as I write this, and I am obsessed with a light Canadian procedural the way other men my age are obsessed with golf.

Start with the ingredients, because on paper the recipe is foolproof. Matt Shade—played by Jason Priestley—is a former professional hockey player, a man so beloved in his playing days that his nickname, Shadow, still opens doors years after his last game. He is handsome. He is funny. He has the easy charm of a man the world has always been glad to see walk into a room. He drives a silver 1969 Porsche 911T with his nickname on the license plate: SHADE 17. Television knows exactly what to do with that list. It makes the charming rogue who treats people as furniture. It makes the tortured genius whose brilliance excuses everything. It makes the loner with a bottle in the desk drawer and a dead partner in the backstory. Private Eyes got a Mensch instead—a gumshoe without gum or shoe, a hero whose whole method is other people.

Part of it is timing. Shade arrives already humbled, which no true douchebag ever is. When we meet him, the glory is over: the career has ended, the marriage has ended, and the man who used to be the main event in every room is a retired player with a famous name, a grown daughter, and no plan. A douchebag is a man still protecting a legend; Shade has nothing left to protect. What is left is the part the legend was covering up: a decent, funny, slightly lost man who is good with people.

And then the show does the bravest thing of all: it makes him the apprentice. I wrote about the partnership in an earlier piece, but it bears repeating here, because it is the load-bearing wall of the whole argument. Angie Everett—Cindy Sampson—is the real private investigator. The agency is hers, inherited from her father; the license, the judgment, and the standards are hers. Shade starts as her problem, becomes her hire, and spends his early seasons studying for an exam he keeps finding reasons not to sit. Even after she makes him a partner, he stays comfortable in the second chair. He defers. He learns in public. He lets the woman with the actual skill be better at the job than he is—on the record, in front of clients—and it never once curdles into resentment or a wounded-ego storyline. Picture the standard TV douchebag doing that for one episode, let alone season after season.

Then there is the house, which is full. The noir detective is a loner by contract: empty apartment, bottle, blinds. Shade's life is a crowded kitchen. His daughter Jules—Juliet, played by Jordyn Negri—is visually impaired, and his whole project as her father is to let her be a person rather than a worry: her own plans, her own judgment, her own mistakes, with him close enough to catch and far enough away to let her fly. His father Don—Barry Flatman—is a retired electrician who owns the Red Bird Diner and is a fixture of Shade's daily life. And his ex-wife Becca—Nicole de Boer, Jules's mother, a television breakfast-show host—still likes him. I wrote a whole essay about that marriage's aftermath: they raise their daughter together, they show up for each other, and the divorce never turned into a war. Shade is surrounded, at close range, by people who know him completely—and they keep choosing him.

But all of that is evidence. Here is the mechanism, and I think it is the secret sauce of the entire show: Matt Shade never tries to control anyone. Not his clients, not his daughter, not his father, not his ex-wife, and above all not Angie. What he does instead is notice people. He sees them, he hears them, he comes to know them, and—this is the part that cannot be faked—he likes them. It is also, not coincidentally, how he solves cases: Shade does not out-deduce a crime scene, he out-notices a room. Control is what the douchebag reaches for, because control turns other people into instruments. Shade reaches for the person, and lets the person stay a person. That is why Angie Everett, who trusts approximately nobody, can trust him: he has never once tried to manage her. I have called that grace before, in the gospel I am writing about this show, and it is the same thing seen from the other side—grace is attention that never tries to own what it sees.

I have a name for the disease Shade somehow never caught, because I coined it myself, in another life. Back in January 2012, writing for Biznology, I described Pretty Boy/Girl Syndrome: the pretty ones know they are pretty, so they sit back and expect the world to come to them—no outreach, no engagement, no unglamorous work, just the serene confidence that being lovely is a strategy. Shade has everything the syndrome feeds on. He was a nationally beloved hockey hero, and he is, by any honest accounting, a pretty boy. And instead of coasting on any of it, he does the work the syndrome says he should never have to: he notices, he listens, he shows up, he apologizes the same day he earns the need to. Call it benevolent pretty boy syndrome—the beauty and the fame fully metabolized, no airs left over, the syndrome cured by attention. He does all of it, I feel obliged to add, while possibly standing five foot two. Jason Priestley carries himself like the tallest man in any room he enters, which may be the whole lesson in miniature: presence was never about the tape measure.

Call it typecasting, because that is the word, and here it runs backwards. Priestley has been playing this man his whole career: Brandon Walsh was the moral center of Beverly Hills, 90210—the earnest twin, the designated good guy—while Dylan McKay got the brooding-stud posters. I was in the right era but the wrong gender to see that show; it was not made for me, and I never watched it. But here is what the posters never said: at twenty-one, Priestley was globally famous as America's good boy, and by his own cheerful confession he wasn't one yet. "A victim of youthful hubris," he calls it now—a kid who thought he knew more than he did. The record has the receipts: a 1998 DUI arrest, his Porsche wrapped around a telephone pole in the Hollywood Hills. And then, in August 2002, practicing for a race at Kentucky Speedway, he hit a patch of oil at nearly 180 miles an hour and went head-on into the wall—broke his back, both his feet, and his nose, and by his own telling his heart stopped. He lived. He married Naomi Lowde, the makeup artist who had stood by him through the recovery, in 2005; he raised a family; he built a second career behind the camera, having directed his first episode of 90210 at twenty-three. The role was a prophecy the man spent thirty years growing into. Which is why Shade's decency never feels performed: Priestley is not acting the humbling anymore—he paid for it. And it is why, at fifty-six, I totally buy that Shadow is the sexier guy. I buy it. Dylan is Tex with better hair, and the trustworthy one is the sexier one; it just takes a man about four decades to be able to say so.

Private Eyes is a Hallmark-shaped show—cozy, predictable, second chances served like diner coffee, a soft landing guaranteed—and it runs the master trope, the second chance, completely straight: the washed-up player, the rebuilt life, the slow-built love. But it quietly breaks the other trope on the sheet, the one where the rival is an unlikable, hyper-ambitious city boyfriend the heroine has to be rescued from. This show made the other man decent. Which brings me to Tex.

Tex Clarkson—played by Brett Donahue—is a private investigator out of Seattle who blows into Angie's life in the third season, and he is the genuine article: rugged, capable, calm in a crisis, and good. The writers refused every cheap exit. He is not secretly rotten, not a coward dressed as a cowboy, not a plot device waiting to betray her. Tex is also a Mensch, a different species of one—he is the man you'd love to become. And he is no match for Shadow, and I believe it. Angie's own mother delivers the verdict. Nora Everett ran a con or two in her day and knows a performance when she sees one, and she says it plainest—I'll give it to you the way I remember it, which is near enough to stand behind the sense of it: Tex is great, but he's no Shadow.

Here is why the verdict is just. Where I am in the show, Angie is pining—but she is still with Tex, and the will-they-won't-they is not two people longing on equal footing, because Shade is cyranoing him. Cyrano de Bergerac had the soul and the words; Christian had the face; Roxane fell in love with the letters. Watch the geometry here and it is the same triangle wearing a detective's license: Tex's challenge runs on borrowed lines, steadied by the one man whose intimacy with Angie is the real, unperformed thing. The stud needs the mensch's script. So the contest was never really Tex against Shade. It is Tex performing Shade against Shade simply being Shade—and a performance, however handsome, eventually runs out of script. Angie is not falling for the actor. She is falling for the author.

Underneath the geometry is a simpler difference. Tex woos. Wooing is a campaign—charm on a schedule, a self presented for approval, control wearing cologne. Shade doesn't campaign; he just is—the intimate friend people trust, the partner in the business and in the life, the man who stood by Angie through a frame-up and a jail cell, accumulating the one asset no rival can counterfeit: a shared past. The stud asks to be admired. The dad asks to be relied on. Tex arrives finished and offers chemistry; Shade offers accumulation—a life Angie is already living inside of. And Angie Everett does not need another adventure. She is the adventure already. She needs home, and home turns out to be the man who never tried to control her for one single scene.

And the sum of Tex's remaining case is not nothing: taller babies. Jason Priestley is, by any honest measurement—and I say this with love—wee. Tex is built like a door. If Angie is doing the arithmetic that women in her position do, the stud's brief writes itself in inches. It lost anyway. The new Private Eyes West Coast opens five years on with Angie and Shade married in Victoria, raising their baby son—which means the wee man won the only argument that was ever actually Tex's, and won it by being there until there was nobody else left in the room.

I should confess where I stand in this taxonomy, because it is not on the podium. I was always like Tex—the wooer, the campaigner, the man presenting himself for approval and wondering why the presentation never quite landed. My buddy Mark Harrison—five foot ten and a ginger—was and is way more Shade: he never campaigns, he notices people and likes them, and he is simply there, year after year, in a way that makes my best efforts look like advertising. I have spent thirty years as the front man, wondering why the letters didn't land, when the letters were never the problem. Ergo: I am learning, at fifty-six, all the lessons I should have known at nineteen, and a silly Canadian TV show—a Hallmark+ exclusive for years, now on Hulu, where I watch it in Arlington—has finally taught me. The instruction is the one I set for myself this October and keep failing upward at: notice people, never try to control them, show up, and turn the other cheek seven times seven. The student remains enrolled. All the teenyboppers crushed on JP in the 90210 days, and I developed my man crush in 2026, at fifty-six.

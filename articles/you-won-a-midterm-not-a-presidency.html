You Won a Midterm, Not a Presidency

An article by Chris Abraham, November 7, 2026

This article, read aloud:

Share: X · Facebook · LinkedIn · Reddit · Bluesky · Threads · Email

An Engagement Is Not a Wedding

On Tuesday, November 3, the Democrats won a midterm. They did not win a presidency. A presidency is a marriage, and this one will be decided by a bridegroom who has not said I do: the eighty percent of the country outside the winners' converted fifth, the uncommitted voters who decide presidential elections and owe no party anything. The wedding is in 2028. The courtship runs for the next two years, and everything the winners say in those two years will be said in the bridegroom's hearing. They are already spending the engagement as if the vows were behind them—target lists, crush-it statements, razor wire, a think-tank dossier of executives to haul before the cameras. The name for the condition is political premature ejaculation: everything spent on the first night of a midterm win, two years before the moment that matters, in front of the very voters whose consent is still outstanding. If the talk goes on at this register, the bridegroom will do what an uncourted bridegroom does. He will walk. Another conservative will take the White House in 2028, and Tuesday's win will be remembered as the high-water mark.

An engagement is a long examination. The bridegroom watches how the other party behaves when it believes it has already won: how it talks about his family, his faith, his money, his friends. He is not committed. He can end the arrangement at any point up to the vows, and he owes nobody an explanation when he does. The winners of a midterm hold a promise, not a marriage, and the promise is revocable every day for two years.

What Was Won on Tuesday

The Democrats won the midterms on Tuesday, November 3. They took both chambers of Congress. They did not take the presidency. They did not take the veto pen. They did not take the executive branch, the agencies, the Army, or a single judge. Donald Trump remains president for two more years, and the winners are already governing in their heads as if he were gone.

Counted plainly, the win is this. The Senate flipped. Democrats took seven Republican seats—Iowa, Kansas, Maine, North Carolina, Ohio, South Carolina, and Texas—and lost none of their own. James Talarico beat Ken Paxton in Texas. Roy Cooper won the open seat in North Carolina. Adam Hamilton beat Senator Roger Marshall in Kansas, which had not sent a Democrat to the Senate since 1932. The count stands at no fewer than 54 Democratic seats to 44 Republican, with Alaska and Mississippi still uncalled. The House flipped too. The Associated Press called the Democratic majority in the small hours of November 4, and the final total is still not settled, because races are still being counted. Democrats also flipped five governorships, including Texas, where Gina Hinojosa beat Greg Abbott.

That is a real win. It is also a fenced win, and the fence has numbers. Sixty-seven Senate votes convict a president. The Democrats have 54, maybe 56. Two hundred ninety House votes override a veto. The Democrats will land somewhere well short of that. Every bill the new Congress passes still has to survive the veto of the man they just spent a week promising to investigate. And the gavels themselves do not change hands until the new Congress convenes on January 3, 2027. The incoming chairmen quoted all over this essay do not yet hold subpoena power. They hold press releases.

A midterm is a counter-response. The country votes against the party in the White House, the way it has for generations, and the result measures the temperature of the president's second year. It does not measure the next presidential election. Reading a midterm as a coronation is the oldest arithmetic error in American politics, and the winners started making it before the counting finished.

The Talk Started Before the Gavels

The incoming chairman of House Oversight, Representative Robert Garcia of California, marked the House call with a two-word post: "LAWYER UP." He then named his first targets out loud in Huntington Beach: Trump family business dealings, the conduct of DHS and ICE, corporate corruption, and the Epstein files. Stephen Miller, former DHS Secretary Kristi Noem, and Corey Lewandowski were named.

His committee's party account posted the list the next morning: "Good morning to everyone except:—The Trump Crime Family—Crony corporations ripping off the American people—Corrupt Trump admin officials profiting off the presidency—Associates of Jeffrey Epstein." That is a target list published as a greeting. The audience for it is not the American middle. The audience is the faithful. The undecided eighty percent can read it too, and they do.

Representative Jamie Raskin of Maryland, the incoming chairman of House Judiciary, issued his statement the same day: "The political and financial corruption driving the Trump presidency is a civic emergency. Congress has a responsibility to confront all this corruption directly—to expose it and take all steps necessary to crush it as quickly as possible." Crush it. Those are his words, chosen for a statement, not caught on a hot microphone.

Senator Chuck Schumer, the incoming majority leader, reached for the same register. "Whenever Trump defies the will of the American people or the law," Schumer said, "we will launch investigations, take him to court, and entangle him in legislative razor wire." He added that "Americans are calling for accountability, not accommodation" and that "the days of Trump's supplicant Senate are over." On Morning Joe, asked how to balance policy against accountability, he did not hesitate: "Should we investigate? Yes. Trump is the most corrupt president we have ever had by a lot."

Representative Bennie Thompson of Mississippi, the top Democrat on Homeland Security, told the Post: "We will investigate the administration weaponizing DHS against Americans, its out-of-control immigration policies, and its blatantly corrupt self-dealing." Former Vice President Kamala Harris posted that Democrats should "be ruthless in using every lever of power to deliver economic relief for working families and hold this administration accountable for its deep-seated corruption. The people demand nothing less."

Underneath the quotes sits a document. Senior congressional Democrats have been studying a 181-page dossier from the Groundwork Collaborative, a progressive think tank staffed by former Warren and Sanders people, listing hundreds of company executives—tech giants, AI labs, gas firms, private prisons, nursing-home operators—to haul before televised hearings, in some proposals to be pressed to resign or pay restitution. Raskin, to his credit, backed away from the paper when reporters found it: "We're not targeting particular corporations. We're targeting systems and networks of corruption." A man does not distance himself from a document nobody was proud of.

The DSA Said the Quiet Part in Writing

The strongest evidence in this essay is a press release. The Democratic Socialists of America published their official reading of the result: "From coast to coast, American voters have emphatically endorsed socialism to oppose the corruption and barbarism of Donald Trump and the Republicans. They are ready to replace oligarchy and imperialism with Medicare for All, an arms embargo of Israel, and the abolition of ICE. With the largest number of socialists in Congress in U.S. history, we are ready to make their demands a reality."

The statement says the organization quadrupled its congressional presence and describes its elected members as "a core constituency to a potential socialist victory in 2028." A midterm counter-response, delivered in a country where the winning party's new Senate majority was built in Texas, Kansas, Iowa, and South Carolina, has been entered into the record as a national endorsement of socialism and a down payment on 2028. That is the overread in its purest form, in the organization's own words, on its own website.

Now that they believe they have power, the secrets come out. Retribution fantasies that needed to stay in the dark are being said at podiums, posted from official accounts, and printed in think-tank dossiers. One podcaster, Alex Pearlman, was reported demanding that Republicans burn their MAGA hats because the hats would soon serve as evidence. That item rests on one partisan outlet citing a social post, and it is printed here as reported, no stronger. The flavor around it needs no sourcing at all: the talk in the air runs to guillotines, to lists, to who gets punished first. Every word of it is a gift to the other side's 2028 advertising budget. You keep your powder dry for two years. You do not empty the magazine into the ceiling on the first night.

The Record Has Another Column

There are real specimens that cut against this, and they belong in the record. The incoming Speaker, Hakeem Jeffries, stood up on November 4 and said: "I haven't had a single member of the House Democratic Caucus talk to me about impeachment. We've been relentlessly focused on affordability and on accountability." He floated bipartisan common ground on affordability in the same appearance. Schumer, in the very Morning Joe segment quoted above, put the caveat in himself: "But number one, get those costs down." Reuters-based reporting on November 6 carried Schumer warning his own side that voters did not hand over the majority so Democrats could do whatever they want. Harris, days before the election, had called a third impeachment "not a good idea."

Senator-elect James Talarico went on Fox News the day after winning Texas and said he was ready to work with the president. On the border, he said: "Give the president credit where credit is due. He has done a better job than the previous administration in securing our border." Senator-elect Abdul El-Sayed of Michigan, asked for his first priorities, named ending the war in Iran, ending the tariff war with Canada, and reversing Medicaid cuts—a policy list, not a target list.

And the wave itself was not built by the wing doing the loudest talking. Jeffrey Denny's count of the result—analysis, not official record—puts the DSA and progressive wing at roughly one Senate seat and perhaps eight House seats, about 1.8 percent of the Democratic total, in an election won by moderate Democrats who ran on ordinary American concerns and kept their distance from progressive pressure. The people who won the seats are mostly not the people writing the statements. The statements are being written anyway, and they will be hung around the winners' necks.

The Wedding Is Still Two Years Off

The winners are skipping this arithmetic. The audience for "LAWYER UP" is the twenty percent who were already converted. Radical talk excites the vanguard and nobody else. These are apostolic conversations, disciples preaching to the choir, and a presidential coalition cannot be built out of a choir. The bridegroom is the rest of the room: populists, the voters who decide presidential elections and owe no party anything. He hears the retribution talk from the place where he is still deciding, and he draws his own conclusions.

The other eighty percent of the country is still out there, and a great deal of it is white and Christian and keeping score. If those voters conclude that the left is crazy, drunk on retribution, and carrying open contempt for them—anti-white, anti-Christian contempt, worn as a badge—the correction will not be gentle. They will vote another conservative into the White House in 2028, and the midterm of 2026 will be remembered as the high-water mark the left drowned in. A bridegroom who hears contempt across the engagement table does not usually make it to the altar.

The win was real: both chambers of Congress, a Senate at no fewer than 54 seats, five governorships. The win was fenced: 54 is not the 67 votes that convict a president, there is no veto override in these numbers, the gavels do not change hands until January 3, 2027, and the president the winners promise to crush still signs or vetoes every law they pass. The wedding has not happened. The bridegroom—the eighty percent who decide presidential elections—has not said I do, and he will spend the next two years listening to everything the winners say. Two years is a long time to be recorded. The chairmen have not yet got their gavels, the subpoenas have not yet been signed, and the man they are promising to crush will be on the ballot in everything but name in 2028. A party that understood the courtship would spend those two years lowering costs and keeping its voice down. A party that mistakes an engagement for a wedding will spend them making the other side's campaign film, one quote at a time—and will arrive at the altar in 2028 to find the bridegroom gone.

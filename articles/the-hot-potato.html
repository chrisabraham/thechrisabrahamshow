The Hot Potato

An article by Chris Abraham, November 20, 2026

This article, read aloud:

Share: X · Facebook · LinkedIn · Reddit · Bluesky · Threads · Email

A ruler who imprisons a voice inherits the voice. The cell amplifies the prisoner, the guards become an audience, and every official who touches the case looks for another pair of hands to hold it. Three thousand years of record show the same behavior in the same order. A powerful man hears a voice he cannot answer. He locks the voice away and calls the matter settled. The voice grows louder inside the walls than it ever sounded outside them. Then the passing begins, hand to hand, court to court, each holder certain the next holder will absorb the cost of a decision he refuses to make himself. The sealed letter travels the whole length of the banquet table. Nobody wants to be holding it when the music stops.

Herod kept John close

Herod Antipas arrested John the Baptist and put him in prison, and the record gives the reason plainly. John had told Herod that his marriage to Herodias, his brother's wife, was unlawful. Herodias wanted John dead for that sentence. Herod refused her, and his refusal carries the first strange detail in the pattern. Mark's account says Herod feared John, knowing him to be a righteous and holy man, and he protected him. The king jailed the prophet and shielded him in the same motion. Power had hold of a voice it could neither obey nor release, so power kept the voice in a cell down the corridor, close enough to visit.

The visits are the second detail. Herod heard John and was greatly perplexed, and he liked to listen to him. The prisoner had become a fixture in the king's inner life, a voice sought out in private and denied in public. Watch the behavior the way a field notebook records it. The animal with the keys returns again and again to the cage. The animal inside the cage changes nothing about his message to suit the visits. John gave Herod no softer version for the royal ear, and the king kept coming back to hear the same sentence, perplexed and drawn in equal measure.

A silenced man fades. A jailed man with a royal audience grows. Every courtier at Machaerus knew the king visited the prophet, and every guard knew the queen wanted him dead and the king would not allow it. John's standing rose with every week Herod kept him alive and listening. Herod thought the cell contained John. The cell had turned John into the most consequential voice in the building, the one voice the king could not stop hearing.

The banquet passed the head around

The end came through a party. Herod gave a banquet for his nobles and military commanders and the leading men of Galilee, and the daughter of Herodias danced for the guests. Herod, pleased and performing generosity before his court, swore an oath in front of everyone: ask me for anything, up to half my kingdom, and I will give it. The girl went to her mother and asked what she should request. Herodias had waited for exactly this opening, and her answer took one sentence. The head of John the Baptist, on a platter.

Observe how the horror travels after that sentence. The girl carries her mother's demand back to the king. The king, distressed, hides behind his oath and his dinner guests and sends an executioner. The executioner carries the head to the girl on a platter. The girl carries the platter to her mother. Four sets of hands hold the deed in turn, and each set passes it quickly to the next. Herodias authored the demand and held it for the shortest time of anyone. Herod signed the order and blamed the oath, the guests, the occasion, anything except the hand that signed. Even a beheading arrived as a relay.

Herod's own later behavior completes the entry. When reports of Jesus reached him, Herod said John, whom he had beheaded, had been raised from the dead. The voice he killed had outgrown the voice he jailed. In the king's mind the prisoner now walked out of the tomb, preaching again, beyond any cell or executioner Herod commanded. The hot potato had become a haunting, and the man who passed it along the table was the man it followed home.

Pilate washed and passed

Pontius Pilate ran the same drill with better props. He examined Jesus and announced his finding to the crowd three times: no guilt, no basis for a death sentence, nothing deserving death. Then he looked for a door out of his own verdict. He learned Jesus was a Galilean and sent him to Herod, who happened to be in Jerusalem, and Herod questioned him, mocked him, dressed him in a robe, and sent him back. The prisoner traveled from court to court in a single morning, each ruler treating custody of the case as the problem to solve, each ruler relieved to hand the man to the other.

Back in Pilate's hands, the governor reached for the basin. He washed his hands before the crowd, declared himself innocent of this man's blood, and told the crowd to see to it themselves. The washing is the most honest prop in the whole record. Pilate knew the verdict his own examination had produced. He also knew the price of delivering it, a riot in a festival city and a report to Rome with his name on it. So he performed innocence and delivered the prisoner anyway, to the cross, under a written charge he refused to amend. The hands came out of the basin clean. The record has kept them wet for two thousand years, and Pilate's name survives in the creed of the movement he tried to hand away, recited weekly by millions. He passed the prisoner. He kept the blame.

Paul went from hand to hand for years

Paul's case turned the passing into a career. Arrested in Jerusalem after a riot in the temple courts, he was held by the governor Felix at Caesarea for two years. Felix heard him repeatedly, hoped for a bribe, and left him in prison as a favor to his accusers when his term ended. His successor Festus inherited the prisoner the way a new tenant inherits furniture, and promptly tried to pass him back to Jerusalem for trial, a transfer Paul refused by appealing to Caesar. Then King Agrippa arrived for a state visit, and Festus brought the file to a fresh pair of royal ears. Festus admitted his position in plain words. He had no definite charges to write to Rome about the man he was sending there.

Agrippa heard Paul at length, in a hall arranged for the occasion, with Bernice and the commanders and the prominent men of the city in attendance. The hearing became a sermon with an audience of rulers. When the panel withdrew to confer, their conclusion entered the record: this man is doing nothing that deserves death or imprisonment, and he could have been set free if he had not appealed to Caesar. Governor, successor, and king had each examined the prisoner. Each had found the case empty. Each had kept him in custody and moved him one station closer to Rome, where the voice they declined to release preached for years under guard in the capital of the empire, in his own lodging, to everyone who came to him.

The pattern at the table

Lay the three files side by side and the behavior repeats with the regularity of a troop pattern. The holder fears the prisoner and protects him. The holder listens to the prisoner and grows perplexed. The holder searches for a transfer, an oath, a basin, an appeal, any mechanism that moves the voice into another jurisdiction. The voice, meanwhile, does the only work it ever does. It repeats its sentence. John's sentence concerned a marriage. Paul's sentence concerned a resurrection. The sentences mattered less to the pattern than the posture of the men holding the cells. Every one of them understood, at some level below policy, that the man in custody was dangerous to hold and more dangerous to harm, and that understanding paralyzed the hand on the key.

The observer sits inside this habitat too, and the notebook applies at home. Every institution keeps a version of the sealed letter moving down its table. A report too true to publish and too documented to bury. A complaint that names the wrong name. A witness whose account checks out and whose existence inconveniences everyone senior to the file. The mechanisms have modern names and ancient shapes: referred to committee, pending review, outside our remit, awaiting guidance from above. The letter keeps its heat through every transfer, because the passing itself certifies that the contents frighten people with power.

The kings and governors share one final trait. History remembers them almost entirely as handlers of the voices they jailed. Herod Antipas survives in the story of John. Pilate survives in the story of Jesus. Felix, Festus, and Agrippa survive as stations on Paul's road to Rome. Their reigns and armies fill footnotes. Their afternoons with a prisoner fill scripture. A ruler who takes hold of a voice trades his own place in the record for a supporting role in the prisoner's, and the trade closes when the cell door does. The letter is still on the table. It is still warm. The only question the record asks the next man in the row is whether his hands will finally open it, or pass it on and be remembered for passing.

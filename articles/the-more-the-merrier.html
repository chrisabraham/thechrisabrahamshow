The More the Merrier

An article by Chris Abraham, November 22, 2026

This article, read aloud:

Share: X · Facebook · LinkedIn · Reddit · Bluesky · Threads · Email

A Slate writer bought a gun last February and announced that conservatives should be terrified. The piece is going around Facebook again, months later, still doing its work. I am a conservative who owns guns, and I am not terrified. I am delighted. Every legal firearm an American buys—liberal, leftist, communist, it makes no difference to me—is another American with a stake in the Second Amendment surviving.

The headline was "Conservatives Are Terrified That People Like Me Are Buying Guns Now. Maybe They Should Be." It ran over a photograph of the author at an indoor range, both hands on a pistol, learning. She meant it as a warning shot across my side's bow. I read it as a recruitment poster for mine.

A person who buys a legal gun has put money, time, and identity into a right. They have filled out the federal form, waited through the background check, paid for the safe, the ammunition, the range time, the training. People defend what they own. A right exercised by a few million hobbyists is a constituency. A right exercised by a hundred million Americans of every politics is a fact of national life that no legislature touches.

The motive does not matter. Buy it because you fear the government. Buy it because you fear the other side. Buy it because you are certain the collapse is coming and you intend to be standing at the end of it. The gun does not care why you bought it, and what happens next is the same in every case: you learn to handle it safely, you get proficient, you meet the culture—range officers, instructors, the man in the next lane who lends you his staple gun—and the culture is polite, safety-obsessed, and welcoming in a way that surprises everyone who knew it only from headlines. Paranoia goes in. A gun owner comes out.

I know the conversion from the inside, because mine ran the other way. I grew up in Hawaii, where civilian gun ownership was culturally peripheral and administratively discouraged, and I spent years in Washington, DC, where handguns were effectively prohibited and ownership existed mostly as theory. I bought my first handgun at forty, in Virginia—a convert, not an inheritor. Converts are the zealous ones. Nobody recruited me into defending the right. Owning the thing recruited me.

America already holds roughly half a billion privately owned firearms, more guns than people, dispersed across tens of millions of households. That architecture only strengthens as the holding widens across every line the country divides itself by—party, race, sex, faith. A right held by one tribe can be bargained away as that tribe's eccentricity. A right held by all of them is just called America.

A right is a muscle. The First Amendment stays strong because millions of people say offensive, unpopular things with it every day and the republic survives the saying. The Second works the same way. Every new owner is another repetition, another voter for whom a ban is no longer an abstraction aimed at strangers but a law aimed at the contents of their own safe. Bans poll well against other people's property. They poll differently against your own.

The old guard of gun culture understands this better than its critics do. The Liberal Gun Club and the Socialist Rifle Association sell out their training classes, and the instructors teaching those classes are happy to have them. Nobody at the range door asks for a party card. An opponent who buys a gun has not infiltrated the right. They have joined it, on the only terms that ever mattered: they now own the thing the right exists to protect.

So buy the gun. Buy the AR-15. Learn it, get good with it, keep it in a safe, and keep your politics wherever you like them. Welcome aboard, everyone. The more the merrier.
